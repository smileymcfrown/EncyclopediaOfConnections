## 1. Family placement

### Proposed new family
- **Key:** `everyday fasteners & fixings`
- **Label:** "Screws, nails, pins, wall plugs and other hardware-shop fixings"
- **Baseline:** *Wood screw*.
- **Members** (suggestions 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 and 13 below): wood screws, wall plugs,
  hollow-wall anchors, drive-in pins, metal brackets and plates, nails, threaded rod and studs,
  eye bolts and hooks, and staples.
- **Existing items:** add the new family to `other_families` of 257 *Ordinary screw joint*,
  260 *Thread-locking hardware*, 222 *Blind threaded fixings* and 274 *Blind rivet*. Don't move
  them, so that item numbers and their current baselines stay put.

### Why a new family is better than spreading these over existing ones
1. **The existing families are mostly sorted by behaviour** (slips, clicks, snaps, locks one
   way). Plain standard hardware has no special behaviour: it just holds. Spread out, these items
   would pile into two families:
   - *clamping, magnetic & friction connections* would go from 12 to about 18 items. Magnets,
     collets and suction cups would sit next to nails and wall plugs.
   - *interlocking joints & joinery* would go from 24 to about 28. That family is about joint
     shapes (dovetails, tenons). Its own baseline (271) treats nails and screws as what holds a
     joint, not as the joint.
2. **It matches how beginners meet these parts and how references group them.** People buy them
   in one aisle and by the box. The USDA Wood Handbook's chapter is called "Fastenings" and covers
   nails, staples, wood screws, lag screws, bolts, metal plate connectors and joist hangers
   together (https://www.fpl.fs.usda.gov/documnts/fplgtr/fplgtr282/chapter_08_fpl_gtr282.pdf).
3. **It gives the finder a place for "fix it to the wall" and "fix wood to wood".** Run 3
   showed beginners describe parts this way ("like a drywall anchor"). There is a clear
   comparison baseline: the wood screw, against nails, staples, brackets and plugs.
4. **Size:** nine members is mid-sized for a family (the existing ones hold 4 to 27).

### Fallback if the owner prefers no new family
- Wood screws, nails, staples, and brackets and plates: `interlocking joints & joinery`.
- Wall plug, hollow-wall anchors, drive-in pins, and threaded rod and studs:
  `clamping, magnetic & friction connections`.
- Eye bolts and hooks: `cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware`.

### Suggestions that belong in existing families either way
- Hose clamp: `clamping, magnetic & friction connections`.
- Screw-in threaded insert: `print-in-place & 3D-printing joints`.
- Push-on edge trim: `snap & clip fits`.
- Shackle and quick link: `cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware`.
- Tamper-resistant fasteners: `locks, latches & catches`.

## 2. Suggested new types (ranked by usefulness to a hobbyist maker)

### 1. Hose clamp (worm-drive band clamp, ear clamp, spring clamp)
- **Description:** A metal band tightened round a hose or tube to squeeze it onto a spigot. It is
  also used as an all-purpose strap clamp for fixing things to poles and pipes.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 133 *Hose barb* names hose clamps only as the add-on that keeps tubing on a barb.
  - 170 *Band flange clamp* wedges two flanges together. It is a different job.
  - Wikipedia says the screw-band type "is used for non-plumbing purposes far more than the other
    types", for example mounting signs and antennas and making improvised pipe hangers. Run 3
    question 22 (a removable dust-hood joint) had no entry to land on.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Worm-drive (screw-band) clamp, also sold as Jubilee Clip (brand). It suits hoses of about
    1/2 in and up. Clamps can be chained end to end to make a longer one.
  - T-bolt clamp, for higher pressure.
  - Spring clamp, opened with pliers. Good in tight spaces, for example PC water cooling.
  - Wire clamp, whose overlapping ends avoid a gap in the squeeze.
  - Ear clamp and stepless ear clamp (Oetiker (brand)). These are permanent and tamper-evident,
    and closed with pincers.
  - Plastic ratchet clamps.
  - Cable ties as a light-duty stand-in.
  - Printed clamps: ratcheting and hardware-free hose clamps are popular on Printables.
- **Family:** `clamping, magnetic & friction connections` (other family: seals & pass-throughs).
  **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:**
  - Hose clamps are for moderate pressures only (Wikipedia). Use compression or crimped fittings
    at high pressure.
  - Cut band ends are sharp.
  - Over-tightening a worm drive can cut into soft hose.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hose_clamp (photos of screw-band, spring and wire clamps;
    sections on each type and on sealing)
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Hose_clamps (25 images)
  - Printables 485192 "150mm (6") Ratcheting Hose Clamp" and 1338893 "100mm (4in) Hose Clamp"
    (hardware-free clasp) (Printables API; pages not opened)

### 2. Screw-in threaded insert (wire thread insert, self-tapping insert)
- **Description:** A threaded sleeve or a coil of diamond-section wire screwed into a hole, giving
  a strong, reusable machine-screw thread in soft or brittle material, or repairing a stripped
  thread.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 56 *Heat-set insert* is melted in, and fails in resin.
  - 179 *Press-fit inserts* are pushed in cold.
  - 222 *Blind threaded fixings* is for thin sheet.
  - Screw-in inserts are a third way of fitting an insert, by screwing it in. They are named only
    in related lists. Run 3 question 24 (threads in resin prints) found this gap.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Helical (wire thread) insert, sold as Heli-Coil or Recoil (brands). It needs a matching
    tap and an insertion tool, and the driving tang is broken off afterwards.
  - Self-tapping insert, which cuts its own thread in a drilled hole (E-Z LOK, Taplok (brands)).
  - Key-locking insert (Keensert (brand)) and TIME-SERT (brand) solid bushings.
  - Screw-in insert nuts for wood.
  - For 3D prints:
    - In thermoplastic FDM prints, CNC Kitchen found Helicoils "add nothing over simply screwing
      into the plastic" (1 Nm against 3 Nm for heat-set inserts).
    - In SLA resin (a thermoset), SPIROL rates press-in inserts the most effective and
      self-tapping inserts "a viable alternative" with lower back-out torque.
- **Family:** `print-in-place & 3D-printing joints` (other families: everyday fasteners &
  fixings, or clamping). **Tier:** 1 (3D printing, especially resin).
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threaded_insert (sections "Externally-threaded inserts" and
    "Helical insert"; images TIME-SERT_thread_insert.gif, Taplok_Insert.jpg, Helicoil_plus.jpg)
  - https://www.spirol.com/assets/files/inserts-for-3d-printed-assemblies-us.pdf (SPIROL white
    paper: insert choice for FDM, SLS and SLA, with photos)
  - https://www.cnckitchen.com/blog/helicoils-threaded-insets-and-embedded-nuts-in-3d-prints-strength-amp-strength-assessment
    (pull-out and torque tests in PETG)

### 3. Drive-in fixing pins (roll pin, coiled spring pin, dowel pin, taper pin)
- **Description:** A short steel pin tapped into a hole drilled through both parts. It is held
  there by its own springiness or a tight fit, so the parts can't turn or slide apart. One common
  use is pinning a gear or hub to a shaft (cross-pinning).
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 266 *Shaft-to-hub joints* names cross pins only as aliases, among keys, set screws and clamp
    hubs.
  - 234 *Locating pins* uses dowel pins to line parts up, not to fix them, and its related list
    points to a "Taper pin and spring pin" entry that doesn't exist.
  - 218 *Press fit* is the general principle.
  - 6 *Shear-pin coupling* is a deliberately weak pin.
  - Run 3 question 18 (a set-screw flange working loose on a gearmotor shaft) had "roll pin" as
    a gap.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Slotted spring pin (roll pin, "C" pin): low cost, for steel parts that aren't critical.
  - Coiled spring pin (spiral pin), in light, standard and heavy duty. It keeps flexing after it
    is fitted, which protects the holes. Light duty suits plastic and soft metal.
  - Solid pins: dowel pins (parallel pins), grooved pins and knurled pins, and barbed pins for
    plastic.
  - Taper pin, for a reamed hole, with tapers of 1:50 metric and 1:48 inch.
  - Drift pin (drift bolt) for timber, from the Wood Handbook.
  - Fitting tips:
    - Drill both parts together.
    - Use a pin punch; a roll-pin punch has a nub on its tip.
    - Pin near the end of a shaft (rule already in 266, from Slocum).
  - SPIROL's table of pin functions: hinge (free or friction fit), hub and shaft, alignment, stop,
    joining.
- **Family:** `everyday fasteners & fixings` (fallback: clamping, magnetic & friction
  connections; other families: locating & alignment, clutches & couplings). **Tier:** 2, but
  high priority for robotics; it could be tier 1.
- **Safety:** wear eye protection when driving or punching out pins, because hardened pins and
  punch tips can chip or fly.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spring_pin (labelled photo of a slotted pin securing a shaft;
    coiled pin; light and heavy duty drawings)
  - https://uk.spirol.com/assets/files/cldp-wp-how-to-select-the-proper-pin-for-your-application-uk.pdf
    (SPIROL "How to Select the Proper Pin": solid against slotted against coiled; hub and shaft,
    hinge, stop and plastic examples)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taper_pin (diagram of a taper pin locking two shafts)
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Spring_pins and
    https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Taper_pins (images)

### 4. Wood screw (incl. coach screw and Confirmat screw) [baseline of the new family]
- **Description:** A pointed, coarse-threaded screw that cuts its own thread in wood or board and
  pulls the parts together under its head; the ordinary way to fix wood to wood.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 271 *Butt and lap wood joints* is the joint, and treats the screw as one of its fasteners.
  - 258 *Self-tapping screw into plastic* is for plastic.
  - 257 *Ordinary screw joint* covers machine screws and nuts.
  - Beginners must choose between wood screw, chipboard screw, drywall screw, coach screw and
    Confirmat screw, and know how to size the pilot and clearance holes. No entry covers that.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Head shapes, from the Wood Handbook: flat (countersunk), oval and round.
  - Pilot hole and shank clearance hole. For lag screws the Handbook gives the lead hole for the
    thread as a share of the shank diameter: 40-70% in low-density softwoods, 60-75% in Douglas-fir
    and Southern Pine, 65-85% in dense hardwoods.
  - End grain: screws hold on average about 75% of what they hold in side grain (Wood Handbook).
  - Coach screw (UK) / lag screw (US): a big hex-head wood screw turned with a spanner.
  - Confirmat screw: a fat, blunt cabinet screw in a stepped hole, for chipboard and MDF. It can
    be removed and refitted "dozens of times" (Wikipedia).
  - Drywall screws are brittle and hold poorly in particleboard.
  - Deck and structural screws.
  - Self-drilling screws for wood to steel.
  - Pocket screws, which stay in 271.
- **Family:** `everyday fasteners & fixings` (fallback: interlocking joints & joinery).
  **Tier:** baseline.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.fpl.fs.usda.gov/documnts/fplgtr/fplgtr282/chapter_08_fpl_gtr282.pdf (USDA
    Wood Handbook ch. 8 "Fastenings": wood screws, lag screws, lead holes, end grain; Fig. 8-7
    shows screw parts)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screw (sections "Wood screw" and "Lag screw"; images
    Wood_screw_with_legend.svg, Lag_screw_(05).jpg)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confirmat_screw (assembly drawing; stepped drill bit)
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Wood_screws

### 5. Wall plug (expansion anchor for solid walls)
- **Description:** A plastic or fibre sleeve pushed into a drilled hole in brick, block or
  concrete. It swells and grips the hole as a screw is driven in, so the screw holds in material
  too brittle or crumbly to take a thread.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 221 *Internal tube anchors* (expansion plug) and 179 *Press-fit and expanding inserts* work on
    the same expanding principle, but in tubes and parts.
  - 222 *Blind threaded fixings* is for thin sheet.
  - No entry answers "how do I fix this to a wall?", yet four entries tell readers to use "screws
    and wall plugs" instead.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Split-ribbed (conical) plastic plug.
  - Universal plugs that knot or fold in hollow material.
  - For crumbly walls, resin (chemical) anchors.
  - Heavy-duty masonry and concrete anchors: wedge anchor, sleeve anchor, drop-in anchor and
    concrete screw (Tapcon (brand)). The Wikipedia "Anchor bolt" article has diagrams of their
    failure modes (pull-out, concrete cone, splitting).
  - Rawlplug and Fischer (brands).
- **Family:** `everyday fasteners & fixings` (fallback: clamping, magnetic & friction
  connections). **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:**
  - Check for hidden cables and pipes before drilling.
  - Wear a dust mask and eye protection when drilling masonry.
  - Overhead or heavy loads (shelves, TVs, tip-prone furniture) need anchors rated for the load
    and wall.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wall_plug (animation of a split-ribbed plug expanding; 1911
    patent drawing)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchor_bolt (Types_Of_Anchors.png; wedge-anchor cutaway;
    failure-mode diagrams)
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Wall_anchors (86 images)

### 6. Hollow-wall anchors (toggle, molly, self-drilling plasterboard anchor)
- **Description:** Fixings for plasterboard, hollow doors and panels, which spread behind the
  board or bite into it, because a plain screw or plug would pull straight out of the thin, soft
  board.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 222 *Blind threaded fixings*: a molly works like a rivet nut, and a well nut is a cousin.
    222's related list already names "Hollow-wall anchors (molly bolt, toggle bolt)" as if they
    were an entry.
  - 5 *Wall plug* (above) is for solid walls.
  - The types a beginner must tell apart:
    - Wings that open behind the board: toggle anchors.
    - A sleeve that crumples behind the board: molly / cavity anchor.
    - A coarse thread that cuts into the gypsum: self-drilling anchor.
    - Plastic wings or legs that open out.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Toggle anchors:
    - spring toggle (butterfly anchor);
    - gravity toggle;
    - pull-strap toggles that swing open and are pulled tight with a plastic strap (fischer
      describes these, and the Toggler (brand) type works the same way).
  - Molly bolt (cavity anchor). The screw can be taken out and refitted, which a toggle's
    screw can't.
  - Self-drilling (threaded) plasterboard anchors, in plastic or zinc.
  - Winged plastic plasterboard anchors.
  - Rule of thumb: screw heavy things into studs where possible (cited already in 42 and 43).
  - Popular printed versions: a threaded drywall anchor with an internal 8-32 thread, and pegboard
    anchors.
  - The owner could split toggle anchors into a separate entry, since they need a bigger hole and
    hold the most.
- **Family:** `everyday fasteners & fixings` (fallback: clamping, magnetic & friction
  connections; other family: quick-release & tool-free for removable fixings). **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:** ratings fall sharply in thin or damp board, and in ceilings. Art of Manliness gives
  rough loads of 10-30 lb for plastic anchors, 10-80 lb for threaded anchors and up to 100 lb for
  mollies, with toggles the strongest [uncertain: one guide, WebFetch summary]. Go by the maker's
  rating for the wall type.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toggle_bolt (photo of open and closed toggles)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molly_(fastener) (photo; patent drawing; "drywall anchor"
    covers mollies, toggles, plastic and self-drilling anchors)
  - https://fischer-international.com/en/products/cavity-fixings/toggle-plug-flip-plug/toggle-and-spring-fixing
    (gravity and spring toggles: "independently swing open behind the board")
  - https://www.artofmanliness.com/skills/homeownership/lets-hang-the-essential-guide-to-drywall-anchors/
    (illustrated beginner guide to the four types)
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Toggle_bolts and
    https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Molly_bolts
  - Printables 139109 "Threaded Drywall Anchor" (Printables API; page not opened)

### 7. Push-on edge trim (U-channel edging, grommet edging)
- **Description:** A rubber or plastic U-shaped strip with springy metal fingers or barbed ridges
  inside. It is pushed onto the edge of a sheet or panel and grips it with no glue, to cover a
  sharp edge, protect cables or hold a soft part onto a rim.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 173 *Spring-steel push-on fasteners* are single clips for a screw or shaft, not a continuous
    strip.
  - 134 *Cable gland* and 215 *Elastomer isolation mount* name round grommets in holes.
  - 131 *Gasket* is for sealing.
  - Run 3 question 20 (TPU reinforcement on a polypropylene basket rim) found that the best
    answer, "make a U channel… no glue needed", had no entry.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Steel-cored PVC or rubber edge trim, sold by grip range (for example 1-4 mm).
  - Trim with a seal bulb (car door seals).
  - Grommet edging (edge grommets) for long or odd-shaped cut-outs that cables pass.
  - Printed TPU U-channels.
  - Rubber cable grommets for round holes go into the notes of 134 (see section 3).
- **Family:** `snap & clip fits` (other families: clamping, magnetic & friction connections;
  seals & pass-throughs). **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:** this is a safety part in itself (it covers sharp sheet edges). Measure the panel
  thickness, because trim that is too loose falls off.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grommet (defines a grommet as a "ring or edge strip"; "grommet
    edging"; molded and continuous-strip edge grommets)
  - https://www.par-group.co.uk/health-and-safety/impact-protection/tfast-u-channel-pvc-edging/
    (maker's page: segmented metal core, grip ranges, fitted by hand with no adhesive)
  - Run 3 question 20 thread, already recorded in research/flowchart-and-format.md section 1.7

### 8. Metal brackets and plates (angle bracket, mending plate, joist hanger)
- **Description:** Stamped steel brackets, straps and flat plates screwed or nailed across a joint
  to reinforce it or carry its load. Examples are corner braces, flat and T mending plates, joist
  hangers and framing ties.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 113 *Aluminium-extrusion connectors* covers brackets for T-slot bars only.
  - 271 *Butt and lap wood joints* mentions "nail plates and ties" in passing.
  - 42 *Hang-on mounts* covers keyhole slots and French cleats, not shelf brackets.
  - Corner brackets are among the most-downloaded functional prints on Printables.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Angle bracket and corner brace (L).
  - Flat, T and cross mending plates.
  - Shelf brackets.
  - Joist hangers, face-mount or top-mount. The Wood Handbook explains how each passes load.
  - Hurricane ties, seismic ties and twist straps.
  - Toothed truss plates (metal plate connectors).
  - Corrugated fasteners (wiggle nails).
  - Printed brackets: model flat faces on every layer, and make holes suit countersunk or
    pan-head screws.
- **Family:** `everyday fasteners & fixings` (fallback: interlocking joints & joinery).
  **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:** connectors only reach their rated load with the fastener type, size and number the
  maker specifies. Simpson Strong-Tie shows a 15% loss from a nail only half an inch short, and
  says not to mix nails and screws in one connector. Seismic ties for furniture are a child-safety
  use.
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.fpl.fs.usda.gov/documnts/fplgtr/fplgtr282/chapter_08_fpl_gtr282.pdf (sections
    "Metal Plate Connectors" and "Joist Hangers", Figs. 8-26 and 8-28)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_tie (redirects to "Tie (engineering)": hurricane
    tie, seismic tie, joist hanger, twist strap; photos of ties and truss plates)
  - https://seblog.strongtie.com/2016/09/pick-connector-series-selecting-fasteners/
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Joist_hangers and
    https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Angle_brackets
  - Printables 420879 "Corner Bracket v2 (optimized for 3D printing)" (Printables API; page not
    opened)

### 9. Nails, brads and tacks (incl. ring-shank nails)
- **Description:** A smooth or ridged steel pin hammered or fired into wood, held by friction
  and by the wood fibres gripping it. It is quicker than a screw and takes sideways load well,
  but pulls out more easily.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:** only named as a fastener in 271. The beginner needs to
  choose between nail and screw, and between nail types.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Nail types:
    - common and box nails;
    - finish and lost-head nails, and brads and panel pins, for hidden fixing;
    - oval nails, which split wood less;
    - ring-shank (annular) nails, which resist pulling out;
    - spiral (helical) nails;
    - roofing (clout) nails;
    - masonry nails;
    - tacks and upholstery tacks;
    - duplex (double-headed) nails for temporary work.
  - From the Wood Handbook:
    - Clinching (bending the point over) adds 45-170% withdrawal resistance in dry or green
      wood.
    - Toe-nailing works best at about 30° with an end distance of about a third of the nail
      length.
    - Lead holes of about 90% of the shank diameter reduce splitting.
  - Nail guns only as a tool note: sequential-trigger tools are safer. NIOSH/OSHA found the injury
    risk "twice as high" with contact (bump) triggers.
- **Family:** `everyday fasteners & fixings` (fallback: interlocking joints & joinery).
  **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:** wear eye protection when hammering. Nail guns are a serious injury risk: use a
  sequential trigger and keep hands clear.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nail_(fastener) (labelled photo of nail types:
    File:Spijkers_(Nails).jpg; list of types)
  - https://www.fpl.fs.usda.gov/documnts/fplgtr/fplgtr282/chapter_08_fpl_gtr282.pdf
    ("Nails": withdrawal, toe-nailing, clinching, lead holes)
  - https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/6013 (NIOSH/OSHA "Nail Gun Safety: A Guide for Construction
    Contractors", 2011)
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Nails

### 10. Threaded rod and studs (all-thread, studding, hanger bolt)
- **Description:** A rod threaded all along, or at both ends, used with nuts as a long bolt, tie
  rod, axle or adjustable spacer. A stud screws into a tapped hole so a nut can clamp a part over
  it.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 257 *Ordinary screw joint* lists threaded rod and studs only as off-the-shelf parts.
  - Threaded rod is used as an axle or tie in more than 20 entries (spool holders, winches, printed
    frames, pendulums), with no entry on how to use it well.
  - The owner may prefer this as notes in 257 rather than a separate item.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - All-thread rod (ATR) and studding.
  - Tap-end, double-end and fully threaded studs.
  - Hanger bolt (wood thread at one end, machine thread at the other), also called the "wood hanger
    screw" in Wikipedia's "Furniture screw".
  - Coupling nuts.
  - Two nuts locked against each other to fix a position.
  - Threaded rod is designed to be used in tension.
  - Waisted (undercut) studs.
- **Family:** `everyday fasteners & fixings` (fallback: clamping, magnetic & friction
  connections). **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:** plain zinc-plated rod is usually low-strength steel, so don't use it for lifting
  without a rating [uncertain: general knowledge]. Cut ends are sharp and spoil the thread unless
  dressed.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Threaded_rod (photos of a metric threaded rod and a stud bolt;
    types of stud)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Furniture_screw (wood hanger screw)
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Threaded_rods and
    https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Hanger_bolts

### 11. Eye bolts, screw eyes and hooks (lifting eye, cup hook, screw hook)
- **Description:** A bolt or screw with a closed loop or a hook on the end, fixed into a part or
  wall so a rope, cable, spring or hanging object can be attached to it.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 262 *Basic latches* uses a hook and screw eye as a latch, not as an anchor point.
  - 156 *Spring-gate hook* and 155 *Quick-release hooks* are loose hooks, not fixed anchor
    points.
  - The safety distinction a beginner must learn has no entry: bent-wire eyes are light duty
    only, and shoulder or forged eyes are needed for angled loads.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Eye bolt with a nut.
  - Shoulder (collared) eye bolts and machinery eye bolts for angled loads up to 45°.
  - Ring bolt.
  - Screw eye and vine eye.
  - Eye lag (wood thread).
  - Pigtail eye.
  - Eye bolt with an expansion anchor for masonry.
  - Cup hooks, screw hooks, J-bolts and S-hooks.
- **Family:** `everyday fasteners & fixings` (other family: cord, strap, webbing & textile
  hardware). **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:**
  - "Eye bolts without a shoulder should not be used for angular loads" (Wikipedia). The
    Crosby-derived handout says "Never use regular nut eye bolts for angular lifts" and to reduce
    the working load at an angle.
  - Bent-wire eyes can open under heavy load.
  - Hardware-store eyes and hooks must never be used for lifting people, climbing or swings
    without a rating.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eye_bolt (photos of an eye bolt with nut, a forged shoulder
    eye bolt, an expansion-anchor eye bolt, screw eyes and a pigtail; forged versus bent eyes)
  - https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/stageriggingspring2015/files/2015/05/Hardware-Handout.pdf
    (CUNY City Tech stage-rigging handout reproducing Crosby eye-bolt and shackle instructions,
    with diagrams of in-line and angular loading)
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Eye_bolts,
    https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Screw_eyes and
    https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Cup_hooks

### 12. Staples for wood and fabric (staple gun, hammer tacker, cable staples)
- **Description:** A U-shaped wire fastener with two legs, driven by a staple gun or hammer
  tacker. Its crown bridges what it holds, which suits fixing fabric, mesh, film or cables flat
  onto wood.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - Nothing covers staples as a fixing. The word appears only for the latch keeper in 262.
  - Fabric and mesh onto a wooden frame (upholstery, canvases, screens, chicken wire) is a common
    maker job with no entry on the strap, cord and fabric path.
  - The rope and sewn joints agent left staples to this group.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Construction staples and upholstery staples.
  - Insulated cable staples.
  - Outward-clinch staples.
  - Hog rings (C-rings) for upholstery and mesh [no source opened; Commons has no category].
  - The Wood Handbook says staples behave like nails, with each leg about two-thirds the
    diameter of an equivalent nail.
- **Family:** `everyday fasteners & fixings` (other family: cord, strap, webbing & textile
  hardware). **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:** staple guns and pneumatic staplers fire like nail guns, so keep fingers clear and
  wear eye protection. Never staple over mains cable with plain staples: use insulated cable
  staples.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staple_(fastener) (labelled staple diagram; insulated staple;
    "In construction" section)
  - https://www.fpl.fs.usda.gov/documnts/fplgtr/fplgtr282/chapter_08_fpl_gtr282.pdf (section
    "Staples")
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Staple_gun
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Staples_(fasteners)

### 13. Shackle and quick link (D-shackle, bow shackle, maillon)
- **Description:** A U-shaped metal link closed by a screw pin or bolt, or an oval link closed by
  a threaded sleeve, used to join chain, rope eyes, straps and anchor points. It is stronger than
  a hook because it closes fully.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 67 *Clevis joint* lists shackle only as an alias, but a clevis is a pivot, while a shackle is
    a rigging connector.
  - 155 *Quick-release hooks* holds the snap shackle.
  - 156 *Spring-gate hook* (carabiner) has no screw-closed link.
  - Hardware choice and safety rules (pin type, side loading, mousing) need their own card.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - D (chain) and bow (anchor) shackles.
  - Screw-pin shackles, for temporary use (tighten before each lift), and bolt-type shackles, for
    long-term use.
  - Round-pin shackles, for in-line loads only.
  - Mousing (wiring the pin so it can't turn).
  - Twist shackles.
  - Quick link (maillon), whose threaded sleeve makes it stronger than a carabiner but slower.
  - Soft shackles go to the rope agent (in its splices item).
  - Snap shackles stay in 155.
- **Family:** `cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware` (other family: everyday fasteners &
  fixings). **Tier:** 2.
- **Safety:** use rated (working load limit marked) forged shackles for any load that could hurt
  someone. Crosby says round-pin shackles must not be side-loaded, and multiple sling legs should
  not be put on the pin. Never improvise with unrated parts for climbing or lifting people.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shackle (photos of shackle types; animated Moused_shackle.gif)
  - https://www.thecrosbygroup.com/wp-content/uploads/catalog/2022/en-US/362.pdf (Crosby
    shackle application and warning sheet, with diagrams)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maillon (photo of a maillon open and closed)
  - https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Shackles and
    https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Maillons

### 14. Tamper-resistant fasteners (security screws, one-way screws, breakaway-head bolts)
- **Description:** Screws and bolts with unusual drives that ordinary tools won't turn, or that
  can only be turned one way. They stop casual tampering with covers, battery boxes, signs and
  public fittings.
- **Nearest items and why it's distinct:**
  - 160 *Mechanical interlock* mentions one-way and security screws as a way to make interlocks
    hard to defeat.
  - 161 *Pin-tumbler lock* is a key lock.
  - Nothing covers "how do I stop people undoing it?" for a plain screwed joint.
- **Notes for the entry:**
  - Pin-in-socket hex and Torx.
  - Tri-wing.
  - Pentalobe.
  - Pentagon head (utilities).
  - Clutch head.
  - One-way slotted screws.
  - Breakaway-head (shear) nuts and bolts, which Wikipedia calls the truly tamper-proof types.
  - Tamper-resistant only slows people down: bits are widely sold.
- **Family:** `locks, latches & catches` (other family: everyday fasteners & fixings).
  **Tier:** 2.
- **Sources:**
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Security_screw (redirects to "List of screw drives",
    "Tamper-resistant types" section, with drawings of each drive)

## 4. Near-misses rejected
- **Separate entries for each wall-anchor type** (spring toggle, gravity toggle, strap toggle,
  molly, self-drilling, winged plastic): kept as variants of *Hollow-wall anchors*. The owner can
  split toggles off if the finder needs it.
- **Masonry and concrete anchors** (wedge, sleeve, drop-in, chemical, concrete screw): variants in
  *Wall plug*.
- **Cable ties as a separate fastener entry:** already covered by 8 *Linear ratchet*, so they get
  notes instead.
- **Washers as an entry:** split across 257, 260 (lock washers, already there), 131 (sealing) and
  264 (thrust). There are too many unrelated jobs for one type.
- **U-bolts:** covered by the mount_adjust agent's suggestion 2 (clamp-on mount) and by 20 and 21.
- **Standoffs and spacers:** covered by the mount_adjust agent's suggestion 3 (shims and spacers).
- **P-clips, saddle clips and pipe clips** (fixing tubes and cables to a surface): borderline.
  They belong with cable management (electrical agent) or clamp-on mounts (mount_adjust). No page
  opened this session describes P-clips (Commons has no category).
- **Nail guns and staple guns:** tools, not connection types. They are a safety note in *Nails*
  and *Staples*.
- **Screw drive types** (Phillips, Pozidriv, Torx, hex): a catalogue of tool fits, not a
  connection type. They could go in the glossary. Only the tamper-resistant drives become an
  entry.
- **Screw, nail and washer sizes, grades and standards** (DIN, ISO, SAE, penny sizes, gauges):
  catalogue, not type. Link to them.
- **Coach (lag) screw, Confirmat, drywall, deck and chipboard screws as separate entries:**
  variants in *Wood screw*.
- **Carriage bolt, hanger bolt and sex bolt:** notes in 257, *Threaded rod* and 250. The Chicago
  screw goes to the rope and sewn joints agent.
- **Corrugated fasteners and toothed truss plates:** variants in *Metal brackets and plates*.
- **Hog rings:** a note in *Staples*. No source opened.
- **Captive panel screws:** already an alias in 261 *Tool-free fasteners*.
- **Clip-on nuts (U-nut, J-nut):** already in 173.
- **Solid rivet:** already noted in 274.
- **Wire-rope clips, thimbles, swaged sleeves, fabric eyelets and grommets, leather rivets,
  Chicago screws:** the rope and sewn joints agent's group.
- **Pipe threads:** the heat and pipe agent's group.
- **Shims and levelling feet:** the mount_adjust agent's group.
