## 325 Wood screw (including coach screw and Confirmat screw)

*everyday fasteners & fixings.* A pointed, coarse-threaded screw that cuts its own thread in wood or board and pulls the parts together under its head; the ordinary way to fix wood to wood

*Also covers:* Baseline entry for the family. Also covers pilot and clearance holes, countersinking, coach (lag) screws, chipboard, drywall and deck screws, and the Confirmat screw used in flat-pack furniture.

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A pointed metal screw with a coarse, sharp thread that you drive into wood with a screwdriver or drill; it cuts its own groove in the wood as it goes in, and its head pulls the top piece down tight
- **term**: Wood screw (screwed wood joint), including the coach screw (lag screw) and the Confirmat screw
- **aliases**: 
  - wood screw; flat-head (countersunk), oval-head (raised countersunk) and round-head wood screws (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8; Wikipedia 'Screw')
  - coach screw (UK, Australia, New Zealand), lag screw or lag bolt (US), French wood screw (Scandinavia): a large hex-head wood screw turned with a spanner; metric hex-head ones follow DIN 571 (Wikipedia 'Screw', Lag screw section)
  - Confirmat screw, cabinet-connecting screw: a fat, blunt-tipped screw for chipboard and MDF furniture (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw')
  - chipboard screw, particle board screw (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
  - drywall screw (bugle head; the fine-thread kind often has a twin-start 'Twinfast' thread) (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
  - deck screw, decking screw: corrosion-resistant, bugle head, auger-type cutting tip (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
  - tapping screw, self-drilling screw (Tek screw) for fixing wood to steel (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8; Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
  - pocket screw (screwed at an angle from a drilled pocket; see Butt and lap wood joints)
  - hanger bolt, dowel screw, double-ended screw (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
  - standards: DIN 95, 96 and 97 (slotted oval, round and countersunk wood screws), ANSI B18.6.1 for US inch wood screws, DIN 571 for coach screws (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'; 'Screw')
- **family**: everyday fasteners & fixings
- **other_families**: interlocking joints & joinery, clamping, magnetic & friction connections
- **one_liner**: A sharp, coarse-threaded screw that forms its own thread in wood or board and clamps the parts together under its head; with the right pilot and clearance holes it is the everyday way to fix wood to wood, and the coach screw (big, hex-headed) and Confirmat screw (fat, for chipboard furniture) are its heavy and flat-pack versions.
- **complexity**: Single piece: one screw with a point, a threaded part, often a plain (unthreaded) shank, and a head with a drive recess or hex. It is usually driven into a pre-drilled pilot hole, with a clearance hole and sometimes a countersink in the top piece (Wikipedia 'Screw', 'Pilot hole'; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
- **how_it_works**: As the screw turns, its sharp, coarse thread cuts or presses a spiral groove into the wood fibres, so the wood itself becomes the nut (Wikipedia 'Screw'). The top piece has a clearance hole, or the screw has a plain shank there, so the thread grips only the bottom piece; the head then bears on the top piece and the screw pulls the two together and clamps them (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types', wood screw). How hard it holds depends on the fibres trapped between the threads: withdrawal strength rises with the square of the wood's density (specific gravity), with the screw diameter and with the length of thread in the wood (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). A correctly sized pilot hole lets the thread form without the screw acting as a wedge that splits the wood or binds and snaps (Wikipedia 'Pilot hole').

### Finder attributes

- **job**: join and hold: fixes wood to wood, or metal, plastic and 3D-printed parts to wood, quickly and strongly
- **motion**: none: a driven screw holds the parts still.
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 once tightened.
- **special_behaviour**: none: it simply holds by its thread in the wood and clamps under its head.
- **holding_principle**: 
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the thread sits in the spiral groove it has cut or pressed into the wood (Wikipedia 'Screw')
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the head clamps the top piece onto the bottom piece as the screw is tightened (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: removable: it can be unscrewed, but each removal wears the thread it cut. In chipboard and MDF, drywall screws 'cannot be removed and re-inserted', while a Confirmat screw presses rather than cuts its thread and 'can therefore be removed and replaced dozens of times' (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw').
- **reuse_cycles**: a few times: in solid wood a screw can usually be refitted into its own hole a few times before the thread strips ⚑ [uncertain]; a Confirmat in chipboard dozens of times, provided the same thread is used (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'); a drywall screw in particleboard not at all (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **tools_needed**: Screwdriver, or drill-driver or impact driver, with the bit that matches the drive (Phillips, Pozidriv, Torx or star, square, hex or slotted) (Wikipedia 'Furniture screw'; 'List of screw drives'); a spanner, socket or special bit for the hex head of a coach screw (Wikipedia 'Screw'); a drill with pilot, clearance and countersink bits, or a combined bit that drills the whole profile in one go (Wikipedia 'Pilot hole', 'Countersink'); a stepped drill bit and hex key for Confirmat screws (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'); soap or wax on the thread for dense wood and long coach screws (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
- **seal**: none: the screw does not seal the joint; add glue or sealant if needed.
- **load**: Medium; heavy for coach screws. Strong sideways (shear) and in straight withdrawal from side grain: the Wood Handbook's withdrawal formula p = 108.25 G² D L (N, mm) gives about 2.8 kN ultimate for a #8 (4.17 mm) screw with 25 mm of thread in wood of specific gravity 0.5 (calculated here; safe design loads are far lower) (USDA Wood Handbook eq. 8-10a, Table 8-9). Weak points: pulling out of end grain (averages about 75% of the side-grain value, and erratic), stripping in chipboard and MDF, the head pulling through the top piece, which often limits screwed joints (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8 'Fastener Head Embedment'; woodgears.ca), and brittle hardened screws snapping: 'in hardwoods, a drywall screw will usually fail before the wood fails' (woodgears.ca). Coach screws threaded into the wood about 7 x their shank diameter in dense wood and 10-12 x in light wood develop about the full tensile strength of the screw (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
- **adjustable**: no: the position is fixed once the screw is driven; to move a part, unscrew it and drill new holes (slotted brackets give adjustment, see Slotted adjustment with a jacking screw).
- **play_and_precision**: Before tightening, the clearance hole lets the top piece shift a little; once tight there is no play. Screws are not locating parts: holes drilled by hand wander and a screw driven without a pilot hole drifts, so add dowels or use a jig where parts must line up exactly ⚑ [uncertain]. A Confirmat screw's plain shoulder 'helps hold the screw in position' (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): the screw is driven from the face of the top piece into the hidden bottom piece; coach screws are used 'where it would be difficult to fasten a bolt or where a nut on the surface would be objectionable' (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
- **materials**: Screws: usually steel, also brass, stainless steel and other alloys, with finishes such as nickel, blued, chromium or cadmium (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8); coach screws are usually zinc-galvanised carbon steel (Wikipedia 'Screw'); Confirmats are steel, galvanised steel, nickel or aluminium-zinc alloys (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). Joins: solid softwood and hardwood, plywood, chipboard (particleboard) and MDF (needing chipboard or Confirmat screws and gentle tightening), and fixes thin metal, plastic and 3D-printed parts onto wood (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8; Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). 'Ordinary wood screws do not hold well in particleboard ... They tend to tear out' (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). Not for: brick or concrete (use a Wall plug or a concrete screw), hollow plasterboard on its own (use Hollow-wall anchors), thick metal (use machine screws or self-drilling screws). Outdoors and in preservative-treated wood use stainless, hot-dip galvanised or coated screws: treated wood corrodes plain steel fasteners in the presence of moisture, and steel stains some woods (USDA Wood Handbook 'Corrosion and Staining', written for nails); deck screws have 'improved corrosion resistance' (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types').
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: buy the screws and drive them with a drill-driver; the skill is choosing the right screw and drilling the right pilot and clearance holes.

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: wood screws, coach screws and Confirmat screws are bought, not made
  - woodwork: the main use, fixing boards, frames and furniture parts together
  - 3D print (FDM): printed brackets, feet, holders and jigs fixed to wood with wood screws through printed clearance holes
  - laser cut: plywood and MDF parts joined at butt joints with screws or Confirmats ⚑ [uncertain]
  - CNC: routed sheet-material furniture assembled with screws or Confirmats ⚑ [uncertain] *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **print_in_place**: no: the screw is a bought metal part; only the parts it holds, and jigs for drilling, are printed.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) To fix a print onto wood, give the print a clearance hole the screw passes through freely, so the thread grips only the wood and the head pulls the print down, just as with a top board; this is what the plain shank of a wood screw is for (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'). 2) Countersunk heads wedge outward as they seat ⚑ [uncertain] and can split thin printed walls; on prints prefer pan, round or washer heads, or add a washer, and keep a wall at least one screw diameter thick round any hole (Hubs 'How to assemble 3D printed parts with threaded fasteners'). 3) If you do model a countersink, match the screw: 82° for US inch screws, 90° for ISO metric (Wikipedia 'Countersink'); Engineering ToolBox lists countersink diameters by screw gauge. 4) Don't overtighten into or onto plastic: the plastic under the head can creep and the joint loosens over time ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) Driving a wood screw into a print is really a Self-tapping screw into plastic (see that entry for hole sizes); Hubs suggests a pilot hole giving 75-80% thread engagement if the screw maker gives none. 6) Printable workshop aids help with wood screws: drill guides that keep pilot holes square, countersink depth stops, and screw-size gauges (see make_it_yourself). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Wood screws (countersunk/flat, raised/oval, round and pan heads; Phillips, Pozidriv, Torx/star, square or slotted drives; part-threaded or fully threaded); chipboard (particle board) screws; drywall screws (coarse or fine, twin-start); deck screws; coach (lag) screws with hex heads (DIN 571); Confirmat screws with their stepped drill bits and cosmetic cover caps; pocket-hole screws; tapping and self-drilling (Tek) screws; hanger bolts and dowel screws; combined pilot-and-countersink drill bits (Wikipedia 'Screw', 'List of screw and bolt types', 'Confirmat screw', 'Pilot hole'). For hole sizes by screw gauge, use a table rather than guessing: Engineering ToolBox 'US Wood Screws - Pilot Holes' gives hardwood and softwood pilot holes, shank clearance holes and 82° countersink diameters.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Pilot (lead) hole for the threaded part of a wood screw: about 70% of the thread's root diameter in softwoods and about 90% in hardwoods; the root diameter of most wood screws is about two-thirds of the shank diameter (USDA Wood Handbook 2021, ch. 8, pp. 8-10 to 8-11).
  - For the best sideways strength, drill the shank part of the hole the same diameter as the shank or slightly smaller, and the threaded part the same as the thread root in dense woods or slightly smaller in light woods (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-12).
  - Coach (lag) screws: shank hole = shank diameter; thread hole 40-70% of the shank diameter in low-density softwoods (cedars, white pines), 60-75% in Douglas-fir and Southern Pine, 65-85% in dense hardwoods such as oak; use the larger figure for larger screws, slightly larger holes for long screws, and soap on the thread (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-12).
  - Coach screw thread penetration of about 7 x the shank diameter in dense woods (specific gravity over 0.61) and 10-12 x in light woods (under 0.42) develops about the screw's full tensile strength (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-12).
  - Sideways (lateral) loads: screw into the piece holding the point at least 7 x the shank diameter; make the top piece about half that thickness; keep end distance at least the top piece's thickness and edge distance at least half of it; screwing a metal plate to wood raises the load about 25% (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, pp. 8-11 to 8-12).
  - End grain: wood screws and coach screws withdraw at about 75% of their side-grain load, and erratically, if the wood doesn't split (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
  - Long screws are wasted in dense hardwood: the screw breaks in tension before the wood lets go, at a shorter length the denser the wood (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-10).
  - 'Screws should always be turned in. They should never be started or driven with a hammer', which tears the fibres and damages the thread (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-12).
  - Chipboard: tighten modestly; withdrawal hardly changes for lead holes of 50-90% of the root diameter, and the stripping torque for an 8-gauge screw 15.9 mm deep is T = 3.16 + 0.0096 x board density (N·m, kg/m³), about 9 N·m for a 650 kg/m³ board (calculated here) (USDA Wood Handbook eq. 8-11a, p. 8-11).
  - In MDF furniture joints the screw's depth mattered most, then the hole diameter, and the screw type least (2019 study cited in Wikipedia 'Furniture screw').
  - Type A tapping (sheet-metal) screws hold about 10% more in wood than wood screws of the same size (ratio 1.05 in redwood to 1.16 in oak) (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-10).
  - Confirmat screws have about twice the shank diameter of ordinary wood screws, go into stepped pre-drilled holes, and must always be refitted with a screw of the same thread or the hole is ruined (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw').

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Flat-pack (ready-to-assemble) chipboard furniture held together with Confirmat screws turned with a hex key (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw', 'Furniture screw')
  - Decking boards fixed with coated deck screws (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
  - Plasterboard (drywall) screwed to wooden or metal studs with drywall screws (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
  - Heavy timber framing, and machinery feet fixed to wooden floors, with coach screws (Wikipedia 'Screw', Lag screw section)
- **when_to_use**: Use wood screws whenever you fix wood to wood, or fix brackets, hinges and printed or metal parts onto wood: they are fast with a drill-driver, need access from one side only, pull the joint tight as they go in, and can be undone. Choose a part-threaded wood screw to pull two boards together, a chipboard screw for chipboard and MDF, a deck screw outdoors, a coach screw for heavy timber and big loads where a through-bolt is awkward (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8), and a Confirmat screw for chipboard cabinet corners that may be taken apart and rebuilt (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). Screws add strength and clamp the work while glue sets in glued joints ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid screws alone in end grain, which holds poorly (use dowels, a pocket screw into side grain, or a bracket) (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8; Butt and lap wood joints). Don't use ordinary wood or drywall screws in chipboard or MDF that must be taken apart (use Confirmats, Knock-down cam-lock and cross-dowel connectors, or threaded inserts) (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). Don't use brittle drywall screws in hardwood or where they bend or carry a load (woodgears.ca). For structural metal connectors such as joist hangers use only the fasteners the connector maker specifies (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog). For parts taken apart often, use machine screws into threaded inserts or nuts (Ordinary screw joint, Screw-in threaded insert). For masonry and hollow walls use a Wall plug or Hollow-wall anchors. For fine furniture where looks matter, hidden joinery or glue is often preferred ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **environment_limits**: Moisture: in damp conditions plain steel corrodes, especially in preservative-treated wood, and steel reacts with some wood extractives to leave dark stains; use stainless, hot-dip galvanised or coated screws outdoors (USDA Wood Handbook 'Corrosion and Staining', written for nails). Mixed metals: a fastener and a metal connector of different metals corrode each other faster, so match them (USDA Wood Handbook; Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog). Wood moving: wetting and drying cycles cut nail withdrawal resistance to as little as 25% of its initial value (USDA Wood Handbook, for nails; screws are probably affected less ⚑ [uncertain]). Chipboard and MDF swell and lose grip when wet ⚑ [uncertain]. Plastic or 3D-printed parts under a screw head creep and loosen in heat ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **safety_notes**: Before screwing into a wall, check for hidden cables and pipes: an electrician quoted by Homebuilding says that in newer homes cables usually run in 'cable zones', e.g. horizontally about 150 mm below the ceiling or vertically to and beside switches, and recommends a detector with voltage sensing (Homebuilding 'How to avoid drilling into wires'). Hardened screws, especially drywall screws, can snap in hardwood and leave a stub buried in the wood (woodgears.ca). Structural and load-bearing joints (decks, joist hangers, things people stand on or under) need the fastener type and number the maker specifies to reach their design load (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog). Screw points that come through the far side are sharp ⚑ [uncertain]. Wear eye protection when drilling ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **common_failures**: Wood splits: no pilot hole, too small a pilot, or a screw too close to an end or edge, so the screw acts as a wedge; drill a pilot hole and keep end and edge distances (Wikipedia 'Pilot hole'; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Screw snaps while driving: too small a pilot hole lets the core bind (Wikipedia 'Pilot hole'), or a hardened drywall screw in hardwood (woodgears.ca). Hole strips and the screw spins: pilot hole too big, which gives a 'rough, shallow' thread (USDA Wood Handbook Fig. 8-8), or overtightening in chipboard (USDA Wood Handbook eq. 8-11). Joint won't pull tight: the thread is gripping the top piece too; drill a clearance hole or use a part-threaded screw (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'). Head pulls into or through the wood: soft or thin top piece, countersunk head; use a bigger or flat head or a washer (USDA Wood Handbook 'Fastener Head Embedment'; woodgears.ca). Weak joint in end grain (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Rust streaks and stains outdoors from uncoated steel (USDA Wood Handbook). Drive recess stripped by the wrong bit or too little pressure ⚑ [uncertain]. A refitted Confirmat with the wrong thread ruins the hole; plug it with a dowel and re-screw (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **related**: 
  - Nails, brads and tacks: faster to drive and cheaper, but hold less in withdrawal and can't be undone neatly
  - Butt and lap wood joints: the joints wood screws usually hold, including pocket-hole screws
  - Ordinary screw joint (bolt and nut, machine screw): a machine thread into a nut or tapped hole, for joints taken apart often
  - Self-tapping screw into plastic: the same self-threading idea for plastic and 3D prints
  - Knock-down cam-lock and cross-dowel connectors: flat-pack alternatives to the Confirmat that can be swapped and rebuilt
  - Screw-in threaded insert and Threaded rod and studs (hanger bolt): give wood a machine thread
  - Wall plug and Hollow-wall anchors: what a screw needs to hold in masonry or plasterboard
  - Metal brackets and plates: steel angles and plates screwed or nailed on to strengthen wood joints
  - Dowel and biscuit joints: wooden pins that align and glue parts with no metal showing
  - PVA wood glue: often used together with screws, which clamp the joint while the glue sets
  - Staples for wood and fabric: fast light fixing for thin sheet and fabric onto wood

## 365 Drive-in fixing pins (roll pin, coiled spring pin, dowel pin, taper pin)

*everyday fasteners & fixings.* A short steel pin tapped into a hole drilled through both parts and held by its own springiness or a tight fit, so the parts can't turn or slide apart, as when pinning a gear or hub to a shaft (cross-pinning)

*Also covers:* Also covers grooved and knurled pins, pin punches and choosing between solid and spring pins. Safety: wear eye protection when driving pins.

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A short steel pin hammered into a hole drilled right through two parts, such as a gear and its shaft, so they can't turn or slide apart; it stays put because it is springy and presses on the hole, or because it is a tight fit
- **term**: Drive-in fixing pins (roll pin, coiled spring pin, dowel pin, taper pin; cross-pinning)
- **aliases**: 
  - spring pin, tension pin (Wikipedia 'Spring pin')
  - slotted spring pin, roll pin, sellock pin, 'C' pin (Wikipedia 'Spring pin'); slotted pin (SPIROL)
  - coiled spring pin, spiral pin, coiled pin, coil pin, in light, standard and heavy duty (Wikipedia 'Spring pin'; SPIROL)
  - solid pin, press-fit pin; dowel pin, parallel pin, ground dowel (SPIROL 'How to Select the Proper Pin'); unhardened parallel pins to ISO 2338 and hardened dowel pins to ISO 8734 (globalfastener standards tables)
  - grooved pin, parallel grooved pin with full-length diamond grooves (ISO 8740 abstract, Standard Norge); knurled pin, barbed pin, headed barbed solid pin (SPIROL)
  - taper pin (1:50 metric, 1:48 inch), threaded taper pin, clock pin (Wikipedia 'Taper pin'); taper pins to ISO 2339 (Wikimedia Commons file description); split taper pin (Bharatskills notes)
  - cross pin, cross-pinning, pinned hub, pinned gear, pinned collar (SPIROL 'How to Properly Pin a Shaft and Hub Assembly')
  - pin punch, roll pin punch, starter punch, drift or lineup punch, pin driving chuck (Wikipedia 'Punch (tool)'; SPIROL 'How to Install a Coiled Spring Pin')
- **family**: everyday fasteners & fixings
- **other_families**: locating & alignment, clutches & couplings
- **one_liner**: A drive-in fixing pin is a short metal pin driven into a hole that passes through two parts, locking them together so they can't rotate or slide; spring pins (slotted roll pins and coiled pins) grip by springing outwards against the hole, while solid dowel, grooved, knurled and taper pins grip by a tight or wedging fit.
- **complexity**: Single piece: one pin, plus the matching drilled (or, for taper and precision dowel pins, reamed) hole through both parts. Slotted spring pin: a tube rolled from spring-steel strip with a slot along it; coiled spring pin: strip rolled into 2-1/4 coils; both have chamfered ends to start them in the hole (Wikipedia 'Spring pin'). Solid pins: a plain ground dowel, or a pin with grooves, knurls or barbs for grip (SPIROL; ISO 8740 abstract). Taper pin: a solid rod tapering 1:50 (metric) along its length, sometimes threaded at one end (Wikipedia 'Taper pin').
- **how_it_works**: The hole is drilled through both parts in line, and the pin is tapped in with a hammer or pressed in. A spring pin is slightly bigger than its hole, so it squeezes smaller as it goes in and then presses outwards on the hole wall; 'The force exerted by the pin against the hole wall retains it in the hole' (Wikipedia 'Spring pin'). A solid pin instead holds 'by displacing/deforming the host material', helped by grooves, knurls or barbs (SPIROL 'How to Select the Proper Pin'); a taper pin wedges tight in a matching tapered (reamed) hole and can be knocked out from its small end (Wikipedia 'Taper pin'; Bharatskills notes). Once in place, the pin crosses the joint line, so any force trying to turn or slide one part relative to the other has to shear the pin, usually across two faces at once (double shear), e.g. where it passes through both walls of a hub and the shaft inside (SPIROL 'How to Properly Pin a Shaft and Hub Assembly').

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - join and hold: pins a gear, pulley, collar, lever or handle to a shaft, or two parts to each other, so they can't turn or slide (SPIROL: 'Hub/shaft', 'Joining', 'Stop' functions)
  - line parts up exactly: dowel and taper pins locate parts precisely and repeatably (SPIROL: 'Ground Dowels are preferred for highly critical alignment applications'; Bharatskills: taper pins for 'self-alignment/location of components')
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): the same pins are used as hinge pins and axles, with a free fit in one part and a press fit in the other (SPIROL 'Hinge (free fit)'; Wikipedia 'Spring pin')
- **motion**: none: a fixing pin locks the parts; when used as a hinge pin or axle, rotate (one part turns on the pin) (SPIROL; Wikipedia 'Spring pin').
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 when fixing (the pin stops both turning and sliding); 1 (turning) when used as a hinge pin.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - self-aligning or self-centring: a taper pin pulls the parts into line as it is driven into its tapered hole and puts them back in exactly the same place when refitted; 'Taper pins help in assembling and dismantling of components without disturbing the location' (Bharatskills notes)
  - none for spring, dowel and grooved pins: they simply lock the parts. Coiled spring pins also keep flexing after fitting, absorbing shock and vibration so the holes aren't hammered bigger (Wikipedia 'Spring pin'; SPIROL)
- **holding_principle**: 
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the pin crosses the joint line, so the parts can only move apart by shearing it (SPIROL 'How to Properly Pin a Shaft and Hub Assembly')
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): slotted and coiled spring pins 'retain themselves by exerting a radial force (tension) against the hole wall' (SPIROL 'How to Select the Proper Pin')
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): solid dowel, grooved and knurled pins are press fits that 'rely on material displacement between the pin and the host components' (SPIROL); taper pins wedge in their tapered hole (Wikipedia 'Taper pin')
- **input_output**: When a pin fixes a hub, gear or coupling to a shaft: continuous rotation -> the same rotation, 1:1, in line, with no slip (a rigid link, so the output can drive the input backwards: back-drivable yes). Otherwise not applicable.
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: removable for spring pins and taper pins in through-holes: spring pins 'are serviceable in the same hole' and 'can be removed with no damage to the host components - if done properly' (SPIROL 'How to Select the Proper Pin', 'How to Remove a Coiled Spring Pin'); a taper pin is 'driven from LARGE end, removed from SMALL end with pin punch' (AIMS Industrial taper pin page), and some have a thread to pull them out with a nut (Wikipedia 'Taper pin'). Semi-permanent for solid press-fit pins: 'Solid Pins provide superior retention when an axial load is applied and are not removable / serviceable', and they 'immediately damage the hole upon installation' (SPIROL). Any pin in a blind hole is very hard to remove: SPIROL suggests spark erosion (EDM) or drilling it out, or leaving the pin standing proud so pliers can grip it.
- **reuse_cycles**: a few times for spring pins: a coiled pin removed properly 'can be installed back into the assembly after the components are serviced' (SPIROL 'How to Remove a Coiled Spring Pin'); many times for taper pins, which 'can be removed from the small end and reinserted repeatedly without losing accuracy' (PTS-UK taper pin page; Bharatskills notes); once for solid press-fit, grooved, knurled and barbed pins (SPIROL).
- **tools_needed**: A drill (ideally a drill press with a V-block or vice to drill straight through the centre of a round shaft) and a drill bit the size of the pin; for taper pins a pilot drill at the pin's small-end diameter followed by a matching taper-pin reamer, and a reamer for precision dowel holes (Wikipedia 'Taper pin'; AIMS Industrial; SPIROL; Model Engineer forum). A hammer, or for small pins a pin driving chuck in a hand press or air hammer (SPIROL 'How to Install a Coiled Spring Pin'). Punches: a pin punch to drive pins flush and out, a starter punch to loosen a stuck pin first, a roll pin punch (with a small centre pilot) for slotted roll pins, and a drift or lineup punch to bring holes into line (Wikipedia 'Punch (tool)'). Safety glasses (OSHA 3080).
- **seal**: none: spring pins are hollow; a solid pin can plug a hole 'to restrict passage of liquids' but is not a seal (SPIROL 'How to Select the Proper Pin').
- **load**: Medium to heavy in shear, light to medium in axial pull-out. A cross pin carries torque or sideways load in double shear; even SPIROL's extra-light-duty 1/8 in (3.2 mm) coiled pin is rated at a minimum of 400 lb (about 1.8 kN) double-shear strength in carbon steel (SPIROL Series 500 data sheet), and heavier duties and solid pins are stronger (SPIROL). The weak point is often the parts, not the pin: a hole bigger than 1/3 of the shaft diameter weakens the shaft, and a hub wall thinner than 1.5 x the pin diameter 'will not match the shear strength of the pin' (SPIROL 'How to Properly Pin a Shaft and Hub Assembly'). Against being pushed or pulled out along their axis, solid pins are better than spring pins ('Superior resistance to axial load (push out / pull out)', SPIROL table). Under repeated shocks, slotted pins elongate their holes and crack, while coiled pins absorb impact (SPIROL 'Slotted Pin Inflexibility Leads to Failure').
- **adjustable**: no: once driven, the parts are fixed in one position; to move them you drill a new hole.
- **play_and_precision**: Ground dowel pins in reamed holes give no play and the most precise location, at the cost of high insertion force and precision-machined holes (SPIROL). Coiled spring pins conform to the hole and allow 'wider hole tolerances', with good alignment from a 'light' press (SPIROL). For hub and shaft, the holes in both parts should match within 0.05 mm (0.002 in), or the parts can move slightly and the pin gets hammered (SPIROL). Taper pins have no play and relocate parts repeatably (Bharatskills notes). Misalignment tolerance: none; the holes in both parts must line up, and a drift (lineup) punch can be used to bring holes into line before the pin goes in (Wikipedia 'Punch (tool)').
- **access_needed**: both sides for through-holes: the pin is driven in from one side and punched out from the other (SPIROL 'How to Remove a Coiled Spring Pin'). One side only is possible with a blind hole, but such pins are very hard to remove later (SPIROL).
- **materials**: Pins: high-carbon steel, stainless steel and alloy 6150 for coiled pins (Wikipedia 'Spring pin'); SPIROL light-duty coiled pins in high-carbon steel, 302/304 austenitic (nickel) stainless and 420 martensitic (chrome) stainless; dowel pins in hardened steel or martensitic stainless (ISO 8734) or unhardened steel and austenitic stainless (ISO 2338) (globalfastener tables); grooved pins in steel and stainless steel (ISO 8740 abstract). Parts joined: steel shafts and hubs (standard-duty coiled pins for mild steel and non-ferrous parts; heavy-duty only in hardened parts; 'austenitic (nickel) stainless steel pins should never be used in hardened components', SPIROL); soft, brittle or thin materials such as plastics, aluminium, die castings and ceramics with light-duty coiled pins (SPIROL light-duty page); plastic housings with barbed solid pins (SPIROL). Wood uses wooden dowels instead (Wikipedia 'Dowel'; see the joinery entries).
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate: the pins are cheap and fitting them is just hammering, but the hole must go straight through the centre of a round shaft and line up exactly with the hub, which needs a drill press with a V-block or a drilling jig (Model Engineer forum; SPIROL), and taper pins need a taper reamer (Wikipedia 'Taper pin').

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: pins and punches are bought
  - CNC: drilling and reaming matched cross holes through shafts, hubs and plates (a drill press with a V-block is the hand-tool version) (SPIROL; Model Engineer forum)
  - 3D print (FDM): printed hubs, hinges, levers and housings fixed or pivoted with steel spring or dowel pins; printed drill guides for cross-drilling round stock (Printables models in make_it_yourself)
  - woodwork: wooden dowels are the woodworking relative (Wikipedia 'Dowel'); metal pins are also used as hinge pins in wooden boxes and toys ⚑ [uncertain] *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **print_in_place**: no: the pin is a bought steel part pressed into a printed or machined hole after printing.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Pin choice for plastic: use light-duty coiled spring pins, which SPIROL recommends 'for soft, brittle or thin materials such as plastics' and close to edges; a medical-device maker cured cracking of a plastic yoke this way (SPIROL light-duty page). Slotted roll pins and solid press-fit pins push harder on the hole and are more likely to split a printed boss ⚑ [uncertain: inferred from SPIROL's advice, not tested on prints]. Barbed solid pins grip plastic but can 'walk out of the hole' in hinges (SPIROL light-duty page case study). 2) Hole size: printed holes usually come out 0.1-0.3 mm undersize (about 0.24 mm for a 5 mm hole in PLA with a 0.4 mm nozzle) (Creative3DP), but a print service that uses industrial printers says its printers 'USUALY print a bit big'; either way, print the hole a little small and drill or ream it to size after printing (Forerunner 3D rule 5: undersize by 0.025 in and ream). Print a fit-test coupon first (Printables 547983). 3) Drill through both parts together once they are assembled, so the holes line up; SPIROL asks for hub and shaft holes matched within 0.05 mm. 4) Avoid cracking along layer lines: round interference holes 'concentrate hoop stress and crack along layer lines' (Creative3DP); give the boss thick walls (SPIROL's metal rule is a hub wall at least 1.5 x the pin diameter; use more in plastic ⚑ [uncertain]) and, where possible, orient the part so the pin's outward push acts along the layers rather than prising them apart ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) Lead-in: Forerunner 3D suggests chamfering printed dowel holes to help alignment, but SPIROL advises against countersinks on hub and shaft holes because they leave part of the pin unsupported and bend it; use at most a tiny chamfer on the entry side only ⚑ [uncertain]. 6) Creep: plastic relaxes under the constant push of a press fit, so a pin in PLA can loosen over months, faster when warm; PLA 'gets soft and deforms at temperatures over 60 °C' (Creative3DP; Prusa knowledge base). 7) Use steel pins rather than printed ones for anything carrying load; a printed pin is weakest across its layers ⚑ [uncertain]. 8) Support the printed part on a solid block right under the hole while tapping a pin in, so the blow doesn't crack it ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search catalogues for: 'slotted spring pin' or 'roll pin' (ISO 8752 heavy duty), 'coiled spring pin' or 'spiral pin' in light (ISO 8751), standard (ISO 8750) and heavy (ISO 8748) duty (Wikipedia 'Spring pin'); 'dowel pin' or 'parallel pin' (hardened to ISO 8734, unhardened to ISO 2338; size tables at globalfastener.com); 'grooved pin' (ISO 8740); 'knurled pin', 'barbed pin'; 'taper pin' (1:50, ISO 2339) and 'taper pin reamer' (Wikipedia 'Taper pin'); 'roll pin punch set', 'pin punch set', 'starter punch', 'drift punch' (Wikipedia 'Punch (tool)'); 'pin driving chuck' (SPIROL). For shear strengths and recommended hole sizes, use the maker's tables (e.g. SPIROL's coiled pin data sheets) rather than guessing.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - The pin's nominal diameter is the hole size to drill: a coiled spring pin is made bigger than its nominal size and its chamfer smaller, so it starts easily and compresses into the hole (SPIROL 'How to Install a Coiled Spring Pin').
  - Cross hole in a shaft: no more than 1/3 of the shaft diameter; heavy-duty pins only pay off if the hole is under 1/4 of the shaft diameter or the shaft is hardened (SPIROL 'How to Properly Pin a Shaft and Hub Assembly'). Indian vocational notes give a general proportion for taper pins of pin diameter = 1/6 of the shaft diameter (Bharatskills notes).
  - Hub wall around the pin at least 1.5 x the pin diameter (SPIROL).
  - Match the holes: hub and shaft hole diameters within 0.05 mm (0.002 in) of each other, the hole centred in both parts, no countersinks, and no more than 0.13 mm (0.005 in) between shaft and hub (the gap between the two shear faces) (SPIROL).
  - Drilling into a curved surface: put a small flat at the hole entry so the pin bears all round instead of at two points (SPIROL Fig. 3); to find the centre of a round bar, spot-drill or file a small flat first, or hold the bar in a V-block centred under the drill (Model Engineer forum).
  - Stop pins: keep at least 60% of a spring pin's length in the fixed part (SPIROL 'How to Select the Proper Pin').
  - Duty: standard-duty coiled pins for most jobs in mild steel and non-ferrous parts; heavy duty only in hardened parts; light duty for plastics, soft or brittle materials and holes near an edge (SPIROL; Wikipedia 'Spring pin').
  - Taper pins: 1:50 taper on diameter for metric pins, 1:48 for inch pins; a metric taper-pin reamer is named by its small end, e.g. a 2 mm reamer runs from 1.9 mm to 2.86 mm (Wikipedia 'Taper pin'). Drill a pilot hole at the pin's small-end diameter through both parts, ream progressively with the matching taper reamer and test-fit: one supplier says the pin 'should engage about 70-80% before becoming tight', and is then tapped home gently, ending flush or slightly proud (AIMS Industrial taper pin page).
  - Punches: for removing a coiled pin, use a punch with a flat, square end the same nominal diameter as the hole, never one smaller than the pin's outer coil, and don't use ball-end punches on coiled pins (SPIROL 'How to Remove a Coiled Spring Pin'); for slotted roll pins use a roll pin punch no larger and no smaller than the pin's compressed diameter (Wikipedia 'Punch (tool)'). Wikipedia says standard pin punches 'should never be used on a roll pin', while SPIROL says round ball-end punches are 'effective for Slotted Pins'; both agree the punch must match the pin.

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - A gear, pulley or collar pinned to a motor or gearbox shaft with a pin straight through hub and shaft (SPIROL 'How to Properly Pin a Shaft and Hub Assembly')
  - Hinge pins in cosmetic cases, car door handles, locks and latches, and a lever pinned to a valve rod (Wikipedia 'Spring pin', with photos)
  - Small roll pins used as shelf-support pegs in cabinets (Printables model 'Cabinet Shelf Pegs Supports 3mm Roll Pin', title from the Printables API), and a spring pin with a washer holding a shaft in place (Wikimedia Commons labelled photo)
  - Taper pins locking a collar, coupling or handwheel to a machine shaft (Bharatskills notes; PTS-UK taper pin page; Wikimedia Commons diagram of a taper pin in a shaft joint)
- **when_to_use**: When a gear, pulley, collar, knob or lever must not turn or slide on a shaft and a set screw keeps working loose: a pin through hub and shaft is a positive lock (SPIROL 'How to Properly Pin a Shaft and Hub Assembly'). Coiled spring pins are the general choice for hub-and-shaft joints, especially with shock or vibration, because they 'prevent hole damage' and accept wider hole tolerances (SPIROL). Slotted roll pins when cost matters more than life, in static, non-critical steel parts (SPIROL). Ground dowel pins for 'highly critical alignment', e.g. two machined plates that must go back together in exactly the same place (SPIROL). Taper pins where parts must be taken apart and put back exactly, or to lock a collar or handwheel (Bharatskills notes). Grooved, knurled and barbed solid pins where the pin must never come out, or to plug a hole, carry bending, give a smooth bearing surface for a pawl or provide a head as a stop (SPIROL). Spring pins also make cheap hinge pins and axles (SPIROL; Wikipedia).
- **when_not_to_use**: Not where you want to slide or adjust the part along the shaft, or undo it often by hand: use a clamp hub, shaft collar or set screw (see Shaft-to-hub joints) or a Quick-release pin (ball-lock pin). Not in thin or hollow shafts or small hubs where the hole would take more than 1/3 of the shaft diameter: use a key, spline or Keyless locking bush (SPIROL; see Shaft-to-hub joints). Not when the pin is meant to break on overload: that is a Shear-pin coupling, sized to fail. Not for locating parts that must lift off easily: use Locating pins with a hole-and-slot layout. Not solid press-fit pins in parts that need servicing, because they damage the hole (SPIROL). Not heavy-duty or austenitic stainless pins in hardened parts, or standard pins in brittle plastic (SPIROL). For hinge pins you want to remove by hand, a clevis pin with a clip or R-clip is easier (see Clevis joint; Tool-free fasteners).
- **environment_limits**: Corrosion: plain carbon-steel pins rust; stainless grades (302/304 austenitic, 420 martensitic) are available (SPIROL light-duty page; Wikipedia 'Spring pin'). Shock and vibration: coiled pins keep flexing after fitting and 'providing excellent performance to counter fatigue in dynamic applications' (Wikipedia 'Spring pin'), while slotted pins act like a rigid tube once fitted, so impacts elongate the hole until the pin cracks or falls out (SPIROL 'Slotted Pin Inflexibility Leads to Failure'). Heat: the steel pin is not the limit; in plastic parts the press fit relaxes over time and faster when warm (Creative3DP), and PLA softens above about 60 °C (Prusa knowledge base). Spring pins in soft plastic may slowly lose grip as the plastic creeps ⚑ [uncertain: no test data opened]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **safety_notes**: EYE PROTECTION: wear safety glasses when driving or punching out pins; OSHA says safety goggles 'must be worn to protect against hazards that may be encountered while using hand tools', and that punches, chisels and drift pins with 'mushroomed heads' may 'shatter on impact, sending sharp fragments flying' (OSHA 3080 'Hand and Power Tools'). Discard punches with mushroomed heads, and discard a roll pin punch whose tip is damaged, because it can't be reground properly (OSHA 3080; Wikipedia 'Punch (tool)'). Hold the punch in line with the pin and strike squarely, or the shank can bend or slip (Wikipedia 'Punch (tool)'). Small pins are hard to hold while hammering, so use a pin driving chuck or pliers rather than fingers (SPIROL 'How to Install a Coiled Spring Pin'). A pin can fly out of the far side of a hole when punched out; cover it or point it away from people ⚑ [uncertain]. Burrs on cut or drilled shafts are sharp ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **common_failures**: Hole damage and loosening: solid pins 'immediately damage the hole upon installation', and slotted pins under impact elongate the hole until the pin 'cracks, falls out of the hole, or does both' (SPIROL white papers). Pin bending or early fatigue from a gap or countersink between shaft and hub, which leaves part of the pin unsupported (SPIROL). Parts moving and hammering the pin because the holes in hub and shaft don't match closely (SPIROL: within 0.05 mm). Pin peeling back on a curved surface, because it only touches at two points; spot-face a small flat first (SPIROL Fig. 3). Cracked plastic or thin housings from too stiff a pin; use light-duty coiled pins (SPIROL light-duty page). Barbed solid pins walking out of plastic hinges (SPIROL case study). Holes drilled off-centre or not straight through a round shaft, so the pin won't go through or the part wobbles (Model Engineer forum). Roll pin ends collapsed or a punch jammed inside the pin from using a standard pin punch or the wrong size (Wikipedia 'Punch (tool)'). Pins stuck in blind holes that can't be removed (SPIROL). Wrong duty: heavy-duty or austenitic stainless pins in hardened parts (SPIROL).
- **related**: 
  - Shaft-to-hub joints and rigid couplings (key, set screw, D-flat, clamp hub): the other ways to fix a hub to a shaft; keys and splines carry more torque, set screws and clamp hubs can be adjusted
  - Locating pins and hole-and-slot: dowel pins used only to line parts up, with one round hole and one slot so the parts don't jam, rather than to fix them
  - Press fit (interference fit): the general principle behind solid dowel, grooved and knurled pins
  - Shear-pin coupling (breakaway link): a deliberately weak pin that breaks to protect the machine
  - Taper fit (Morse taper): a cone-in-cone fit, the same wedging idea as a taper pin but between a shaft and its socket
  - Quick-release pin (ball-lock pin) and Tool-free fasteners (hitch pin and R-clip): pins removed by hand, for joints taken apart often
  - Clevis joint (fork-and-pin joint): a pin through a fork as a pivot, held by a clip rather than a tight fit
  - Thread-locking hardware (split pin): a soft split pin through a hole stops a nut or clevis pin coming out, not for carrying load
  - Retaining ring (circlip, E-clip): snaps into a groove to stop sliding, but doesn't stop turning
  - Temporary sheet fastener (Cleco-type spring pin): a reusable spring-loaded pin that holds sheets while drilling and riveting
  - Plain pivot and rolling bearing (pin in hole, shoulder bolt): when the pin is meant to let a part turn
  - Wedge dowel (click-in grooved dowel) and wooden dowels: the woodworking relatives

## 366 Wall plug (expansion anchor for solid walls)

*everyday fasteners & fixings.* A plastic or fibre sleeve pushed into a drilled hole in brick, block or concrete that swells and grips the hole as a screw is driven in, so the screw holds in material too brittle or crumbly to take a thread

*Also covers:* Also covers matching plug, drill and screw sizes, and masonry anchors as variants (wedge, sleeve, drop-in, chemical anchors and concrete screws). Rawlplug (brand) as an alias. Safety: check for hidden cables and pipes before drilling; heavy, overhead and tip-prone loads need rated anchors.

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A small plastic sleeve you tap into a hole drilled in a brick or concrete wall; when you drive a screw into it, the sleeve swells and grips the sides of the hole, so the screw holds in a wall too hard and crumbly to take a thread
- **term**: Wall plug (plastic expansion anchor, screw anchor); its heavy-duty relatives are masonry anchors: wedge anchor, sleeve anchor, drop-in anchor, chemical (bonded) anchor and concrete screw
- **aliases**: 
  - wall plug (UK); anchor or plastic anchor (US); rawlplug or 'rawl plug' (UK everyday name taken from the Rawlplug brand; the plug was invented in 1911) (Wikipedia 'Wall plug')
  - Rawlplug (brand) and Fischer plug (brand, the plastic plug Artur Fischer designed in the late 1950s) (Wikipedia 'Wall plug')
  - expansion plug; plastic expansion anchor; nylon plug; screw anchor; masonry plug; 'dowel' (a common translation of the German 'Dübel', used on Printables model pages)
  - split-ribbed plastic anchor; conical screw anchor (Wikipedia 'Wall plug')
  - universal plug; all-purpose plug: a plug that expands in solid walls and folds or knots in hollow ones (fischer UX and DuoPower pages)
  - masonry anchor; concrete anchor; post-installed anchor (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
  - wedge anchor; stud anchor; expansion bolt; torque-controlled expansion anchor (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Eugene Fasteners)
  - sleeve anchor; Rawlbolt (brand; the first mechanical anchor, made by Rawlplug in the 1930s) (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'; Eugene Fasteners) ⚑ [uncertain: Wikipedia does not say which modern anchor type the original Rawlbolt was]
  - drop-in anchor; internally threaded expansion anchor; displacement-controlled anchor (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Simpson Strong-Tie setting-tool page)
  - chemical anchor; bonded anchor; adhesive anchor; resin anchor; injection anchor (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; fischer)
  - concrete screw; masonry screw; screw anchor; self-tapping concrete screw; Tapcon (brand) (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Confast) *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **family**: everyday fasteners & fixings
- **other_families**: clamping, magnetic & friction connections, adhesives & bonding
- **one_liner**: A wall plug is a plastic (once fibre or wood) sleeve that you push into a hole drilled in brick, block, stone or concrete; driving a screw into it spreads the sleeve against the hole so the screw can hold a shelf, bracket or cabinet, and bigger metal or resin anchors do the same job for heavy loads.
- **complexity**: A few parts. Plain wall plug: one moulded plastic sleeve (split along its length, with ribs, fins or teeth on the outside and a small collar at the front), used with your own wood or chipboard screw (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'; Rawlplug R-OLD data sheet). Universal plugs such as the fischer DuoPower are moulded from two plastics in one piece (fischer). Heavy-duty variants are small assemblies that act as one unit: wedge anchor = threaded stud with a cone on the end, a wedge clip (expansion clip), washer and nut; sleeve anchor = bolt or stud, cone, full-length split sleeve, washer and nut; drop-in anchor = internally threaded shell with an expander plug inside, set with a setting tool; chemical anchor = two-part resin cartridge, threaded rod and, in hollow masonry, a mesh sleeve; concrete screw = a single hardened screw with a sharp, cutting thread (Eugene Fasteners; Simpson Strong-Tie setting-tool page; fischer FIS H N; Confast).
- **how_it_works**: You drill a hole the same size as the plug, tap the plug in until its collar is flush with the wall, then drive a screw through your bracket into it. The screw is fatter than the hole in the middle of the plug, so as it goes in it forces the two halves of the plug apart; the soft plastic squeezes into every bump of the hole and presses hard on its walls, and ribs and fins stop it spinning or sliding out (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'; Rawlplug). In hollow bricks and blocks, universal plugs instead fold or tie themselves into a knot behind the thin brick walls, so they hold by shape rather than by squeezing (fischer UX: 'expands in solid building materials and knots in hollow building materials'). Heavy-duty metal anchors work the same way with a steel cone pulled into a split sleeve or clip; chemical anchors glue a threaded rod into the hole with resin, and concrete screws cut their own thread into the concrete (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Eugene Fasteners; Confast).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - join and hold: fixes shelves, brackets, cabinets, rails, hooks and machines to brick, block, stone and concrete walls, floors and ceilings (Rawlplug R-OLD applications; fischer DuoPower applications; Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
  - glue or bond: chemical (bonded) anchors glue a threaded rod or bar into the hole with epoxy, polyester or vinylester resin (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
- **motion**: none: once the screw is tight the plug and fixture can't move
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0: the plug is locked in the hole and the screw clamps the fixture to the wall. Before the screw goes in, a loose plug can still turn or slide in the hole, which is why plugs have anti-rotation fins and why the hole size matters.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - none of the listed behaviours: a wall plug is simply a permanent anchor point for a screw
  - Its own trick: universal plugs change how they hold to suit the wall: they expand in solid material, and fold or knot behind the webs of hollow bricks and blocks, so one plug can be used when you don't know what the wall is made of (fischer UX; fischer DuoPower)
- **holding_principle**: 
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the expanded plug, or the steel clip or sleeve of a wedge or sleeve anchor, presses outward on the walls of the hole and holds by friction (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt': 'friction mechanical interlock guaranteed by expansion forces'; 'Plastic anchors... similar to mechanical expansion anchors')
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): ribs and fins key into the hole; universal plugs knot behind the webs of hollow bricks; resin pushed through a mesh sleeve forms a key in perforated brick; undercut anchors bear on an undercut in the hole; and a concrete screw's thread bears on the concrete it has cut (fischer UX; fischer FIS H N; Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
  - material bond (glue, solvent or melting): chemical anchors are held by resin bonded to the hole wall and the rod (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed). A drill, often with hammer action, is only used to make the hole (Confast; Anchor It).
- **removability**: semi-permanent: the screw can be undone and the fixture taken down, but the plug normally stays in the wall (it can be pulled or drilled out, leaving a hole to fill). Wedge and sleeve anchors are 'permanent installations': the nut or bolt can be removed and refitted but the anchor body stays in the hole (Eugene Fasteners). Chemical anchors are permanent. Concrete screws unscrew; a hole that is too shallow can leave the screw 'bound in the hole and unable to be removed' (Confast).
- **reuse_cycles**: a few times: the screw can usually be taken out and put back into the same plug a few times, but each plug is set once; a plug that has been pulled out is normally replaced ⚑ [uncertain: no source gives a number of re-uses]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **tools_needed**: Drill with a masonry bit of the size marked on the plug; hammer action for concrete, hard brick and concrete screws ('The hole must be drilled using a hammer drill with a carbide tipped bit' for Tapcon screws, Confast; 'a masonry bit, a heavy-duty hammer drill, concrete screw anchors, and an impact driver' for brick, Anchor It). A hammer to tap the plug flush, a screwdriver or drill-driver, a pencil, tape measure and level, and a cable, pipe and stud detector before drilling (Electrical Safety First). Heavy anchors add a blow-out pump or compressed air to clean the hole, a spanner or torque wrench for wedge anchors, a setting tool for drop-in anchors and a dispensing gun and brush for chemical anchors (Eugene Fasteners; Simpson Strong-Tie setting-tool page).
- **seal**: none: a wall plug doesn't seal anything
- **load**: Light to heavy, and it depends far more on the wall than on the plug. fischer's stated maximum loads for a single DuoPower 6 x 50 mm universal plug are 165 kg in concrete, 55 kg in solid brick, 17 kg in vertically perforated brick, 15 kg in aerated concrete and 15 kg in 12.5 mm plasterboard (fischer DuoPower 6 x 50 page). Strongest against a downward (shear) load close to the wall, weaker in straight pull-out (tension), as on a ceiling or a shelf bracket's top screw: Wikipedia's table gives typical figures of 10-20 lb (4.5-9 kg) tension against 20-30 lb (9-14 kg) shear for plastic expansion anchors, and 300-600 lb (140-270 kg) tension for concrete screws (Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor') ⚑ [uncertain: the table cites no test source and doesn't say whether these are safe or breaking loads]. Wedge anchors carry the highest, structural-rated loads but only in solid concrete (Eugene Fasteners). Loads held away from the wall (deep shelves, swing-arm TV mounts) lever on the top fixings and greatly reduce what each anchor can take (TOGGLER FAQ, written for its hollow-wall anchors). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **adjustable**: no: the position is fixed once the hole is drilled. Slotted holes in the bracket, or a French cleat, give some adjustment (see 'Hang-on mounts').
- **play_and_precision**: not applicable as a moving joint: once tight there is no play. Position accuracy depends on marking and drilling; a hole drilled oversize lets the plug spin and holds less (Rawlplug: 'Anti-rotation fins prevent spinning'; Simpson Strong-Tie: 'Oversized holes will make it difficult to set the anchor and will reduce the anchor's load capacity').
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): everything is done from the face of the wall; the back of the wall is never reached.
- **materials**: Plugs: polypropylene or polyethylene (Rawlplug R-OLD: 'impact resistant co-polymer of polypropylene'), nylon (fischer UX and DuoPower), once jute fibre bonded with glue, lead, wood or white bronze (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'). Metal anchors: zinc-plated or stainless carbon steel; chemical anchors: polyester, vinylester or epoxy resin with steel threaded rod (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Eugene Fasteners). Walls they suit: concrete, solid brick, sand-lime brick, natural stone and solid block; universal plugs and chemical anchors with mesh sleeves also suit perforated brick, hollow blocks and aerated concrete (fischer DuoPower; fischer FIS H N). Wedge anchors suit solid concrete only, not hollow block, brick or mortar joints; sleeve anchors suit concrete, brick, hollow block and (at low load) mortar joints (Eugene Fasteners). Not for plasterboard on its own: 'if fixing into plasterboard, Rawlplugs will not work' (Homebuilding); see 'Hollow-wall anchors'.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy for plastic plugs and concrete screws: you need the right drill, bit and screw, and a detector to check the wall first. Moderate for wedge, drop-in and chemical anchors, which need exact hole sizes, hole cleaning, a torque wrench or setting tool, and time for resin to cure (Eugene Fasteners; Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt').

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: wall plugs and masonry anchors are bought; the maker's part is the bracket, holder or shelf fixed with them
  - 3D print (FDM): printed brackets and holders are screwed to walls through clearance holes into plugs; emergency printed plugs exist, printed in PETG at 100% infill, for solid masonry and light loads only (Printables models by Petr Vacek, Skovik, Mape and Marc Herter, read through the Printables API)
  - woodwork: battens, cleats and cabinets fixed to masonry walls with plugs and screws or concrete screws (Anchor It)
- **print_in_place**: no: a commercial plug is moulded, and the printed versions on Printables are single plugs that need a screw; nothing is assembled when printed.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) The plug goes into the wall, not into the print: give the printed bracket a plain clearance hole for the screw (a little bigger than the screw's outer diameter) and let the screw thread grip only the plug ⚑ [uncertain: general practice; not stated by a cited source]. 2) Count the print as the 'fixture' when choosing screw length: screw length = plug length + fixture thickness + 1 x screw diameter (fischer DuoPower and UX), so a 50 mm plug and a 10 mm thick printed bracket with a 5 mm screw needs at least a 65 mm screw. 'The threadless part of the screw must not be longer than the fixture' (fischer), or the plain shank ends up inside the plug and doesn't expand it. 3) Make the area round each screw hole solid (extra perimeters or 100% infill) and use a pan or washer-head screw, or a washer, so the clamping force bears on solid plastic; a countersunk head acts as a wedge and can split printed layers ⚑ [uncertain: general printing advice]. 4) Orient the bracket so the load presses layers together rather than peeling them apart, and print hangers for heavy or permanent loads in PETG, ABS or ASA rather than PLA, which creeps under constant load ⚑ [uncertain: general printing advice]. 5) Printed plugs are for emergencies: Petr Vacek's online HmoždinkApp generator suggests PETG (also PLA), 0.3 mm layers or lower, 100% infill, and says the plug 'is best fitting to solid materials, such as solid bricks or concrete' and 'has no official certificates. Experiment with low loads first'; Skovik says for his 10 x 45 mm plug 'PETG is appropriate material', ABS also, 'PLA není vhodný' (PLA is not suitable); Marc Herter printed his 6 x 30 mm plug in PETG at 100% infill; Mape found that thinner walls and a larger gap between the two halves 'works much better', and recommends a large brim (Printables API). Don't use printed plugs for anything overhead, heavy or that could fall on someone. 6) If you want a thread inside the print itself, use a heat-set insert, printed thread or captive nut instead (see those entries); wall plugs are made for masonry. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search for: 'wall plugs' / 'plastic wall plugs' / 'nylon wall plugs' (often colour-coded by size in the UK: yellow, red, brown, then grey or blue for large sizes, though 'colour coding can vary between brands', Homebuilding), 'universal plugs' or 'all-purpose plugs' (expand in solid walls, knot in hollow ones), 'frame fixings' for push-through installation through a batten or frame (fischer DuoPower is 'suitable for pre-positioned and push-through installation'), 'hammer fixings' or 'nail plugs' (pre-assembled plug and nail-screw that is hammered in) ⚑ [uncertain: not described by an opened source], 'concrete screws' / 'masonry screws' / 'Tapcon (brand)' with their matching drill bit, 'wedge anchors' / 'through bolts', 'sleeve anchors' / 'shield anchors', 'drop-in anchors' with 'setting tool', 'chemical anchor' / 'injection resin' / 'resin anchor kit' with 'threaded rod' (studding) and 'mesh sleeve' or 'anchor sleeve' for hollow masonry (fischer FIS H N), and 'furniture anti-tip kit' / 'TV anchoring kit' (Anchor It). Match the 'masonry drill bit' or 'SDS drill bit' to the plug; for screws, wood or chipboard screws of the diameter range printed on the plug pack (e.g. 4.0-5.0 mm for a 6 mm DuoPower, fischer). Link to a maker's size table rather than copying it: e.g. Rawlplug's R-OLD data sheet and fischer's product pages. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Drill bit = plug diameter: 'a 6mm Rawlplug commonly fits a hole drilled with a 6mm bit', and the plug 'should fit snugly into the hole without being forced in, and it should sit flush with the wall surface' (Homebuilding). Rawlplug's R-OLD plugs: yellow = 5 mm hole for 3.0-5.0 mm screws, red = 6 mm hole for 3.5-5.5 mm screws, brown = 7 mm hole for 5.0-6.0 mm screws (Rawlplug R-OLD data sheet), but 'colour coding can vary between brands' (Homebuilding).
  - Match the screw to the plug: use a screw diameter inside the range printed on the pack, e.g. 4.0-5.0 mm wood or chipboard screws for a 6 mm fischer DuoPower (fischer). In UK screw gauges: 5 mm plugs take No. 6-8, 6 mm take No. 8-10, 7-8 mm take No. 10-14 (Homebuilding).
  - Screw length = plug length + fixture thickness + 1 x screw diameter (fischer DuoPower and UX).
  - Drill deeper than the plug: fischer gives a 60 mm minimum hole depth for a 50 mm DuoPower; Homebuilding's tip is to line the plug up against the bit, add 5 mm and mark the depth with masking tape.
  - Get through the plaster: the plug must grip the masonry, not the plaster on top; fischer's longer DuoPower plugs are 'especially suited to... bridge plaster' (fischer DuoPower).
  - Keep away from edges: 'The edge distance must be at least one plug length' (fischer UX). Wedge anchors need even more edge distance and concrete thickness because 'the high local pressure at the wedge can crack thin or close-to-edge concrete' (Eugene Fasteners).
  - Choose the anchor by the wall: wedge anchors in solid concrete only; sleeve anchors in concrete, brick, hollow block and (low load only) mortar joints (Eugene Fasteners); chemical anchors with a mesh sleeve in perforated brick, where 'the mortar is pressed through the grid structure and creates a form fit' (fischer FIS H N); a resin or gypsum-pad fix in crumbling walls where an expanding plug could crack the wall (Wikipedia 'Wall plug').
  - Concrete screws need their own bit size and depth: a 3/16 in screw needs a 5/32 in hole and a 1/4 in screw a 3/16 in hole; embed at least 1 in and at most 1-3/4 in; drill the hole 1/2 in deeper than the screw goes so the dust has somewhere to go (Confast, for Tapcon screws).
  - Wedge anchors: drill exactly the anchor diameter and 'at least 1/4" deeper than the published minimum embedment', blow the dust out, and tighten to the published torque with a torque wrench; sleeve anchors are tightened 'snug plus a defined turn count (commonly 3 to 5 turns past snug, per brand spec)' (Eugene Fasteners).
  - Chemical anchors: clean the hole: tests showed up to 60% less capacity in a dirty hole, and 20% less in wet concrete with polyester resin (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt').
  - Anchor furniture that can tip: 'Anchoring all furniture with drawers, doors, and shelves to the wall prevents tip-over injuries and deaths' (US CPSC, Anchor It); on brick, use concrete screw anchors set with a masonry bit and hammer drill (Anchor It).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - The plugs behind almost every shelf bracket, curtain rail, bathroom cabinet, mirror and picture hook on a brick or concrete wall (Rawlplug R-OLD and fischer DuoPower application lists)
  - Radiators, TV mount brackets, kitchen cabinets, gutter brackets and house numbers on brick (Homebuilding)
  - A furniture or TV anti-tip strap screwed to a brick wall with concrete screws (Anchor It)
  - Bolts holding a handrail, machine or steel post to a concrete floor, which use wedge, sleeve or chemical anchors (Eugene Fasteners; Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
- **when_to_use**: Use a wall plug whenever you need to screw something to brick, block, stone or concrete: shelves, brackets, rails, hooks, cabinets and printed holders. Plastic plugs and universal plugs cover light and medium loads in most solid walls, and universal plugs also cope with perforated brick and unknown walls (fischer UX and DuoPower). Use concrete screws for quick, strong fixings in concrete, brick or block that can be unscrewed later, such as anti-tip kits and battens (Anchor It; Confast). Use wedge anchors for heavy and structural loads in solid concrete, sleeve anchors for medium loads in mixed or weaker masonry, drop-in anchors where you want a flush threaded socket for a bolt, including overhead, and chemical anchors for hollow or crumbly masonry, near edges, or for the heaviest loads (Eugene Fasteners; Simpson Strong-Tie setting-tool page; fischer FIS H N; Wikipedia 'Wall plug').
- **when_not_to_use**: Not for plasterboard (drywall), hollow doors or thin panels: a plain plug just pulls out; use a hollow-wall anchor or screw into a stud (Homebuilding: 'if fixing into plasterboard, Rawlplugs will not work'). Not for wood: screw straight into timber (see 'Wood screw'). Don't put a wedge anchor in hollow block, brick or a mortar joint: 'the clip will simply break out the base material rather than expand against it' (Eugene Fasteners). Don't put an expanding plug in a crumbling wall, where 'the force of the expanding plug may be enough to cause cracking'; use a resin fix instead (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'). For structural, safety-critical or code-required fixings (balustrades, heavy overhead loads, anything people stand on or under), use anchors with a published, approved rating installed exactly to the maker's instructions; 'most sleeve anchors are not code-listed for structural applications' (Eugene Fasteners). For light items on smooth walls where you can't drill, consider stretch-release adhesive strips or double-sided foam tape instead.
- **environment_limits**: Plastic plugs are used indoors and out, but a steel screw left wet outdoors will rust; use stainless or coated screws outside ⚑ [uncertain: general advice, not from an opened source]. Published plastic-anchor loads assume normal room temperature: for example fischer's nylon plasterboard anchor GK is rated only 'for temperatures in the substrate up to +24 °C (resp. short term up to +40 °C)' (fischer GK load table). Bonded (chemical) anchors lose capacity in wet holes (20% less in wet concrete with polyester resin) and have known issues with 'high temperature behavior and creep response' (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'). Crumbly or damp masonry holds much less, and an expanding plug may crack it (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'). Anchors in cracked concrete and under vibration or earthquake loads need specially rated types (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt': seismic and crack-cycling loads). Plastic plugs may creep or loosen under constant heavy load or vibration ⚑ [uncertain: no opened source gives data]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **safety_notes**: Check for hidden cables and pipes before drilling: 'A common error is accidentally drilling, nailing, or screwing into cables hidden inside your walls. A quality cable detector can help track buried cables before you start work', and cables buried in walls are required to run 'either horizontally or vertically from electrical accessories like socket-outlets or switches', so avoid those lines (Electrical Safety First, UK). A damaged gas pipe 'can result in a gas leak, fire or explosion'; take care when drilling (Cadent Gas). Drilling concrete, brick and mortar makes fine silica dust (respirable crystalline silica), which causes lung disease; HSE says FFP3 is 'the most advisable type' of mask for work that 'does or could create high dust levels or involves silica' (HSE construction dust FAQ). Wear eye protection when drilling masonry ⚑ [uncertain: general advice]. Heavy, overhead and tip-prone loads need anchors rated for the load and the wall, fitted to the maker's instructions; furniture with drawers, doors and shelves should be anchored to the wall to prevent tip-overs (US CPSC, Anchor It). Ceiling fixings carry the full load in pull-out, the direction anchors are weakest (Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor' table), and a failure drops the load on whoever is below. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **common_failures**: 1) Plug spins in the hole and the screw won't tighten: hole too big (wrong or worn bit, or a wobbly drill) or soft, crumbly wall; drill the size on the pack and use plugs with anti-rotation fins (Rawlplug; fischer UX). 2) Plug pulls out with the screw: screw too thin or short to expand it, plug sitting in plaster instead of masonry, or overloaded; use the screw range and length rule on the pack and a long enough plug (fischer). 3) Plug won't go in flush, or the screw bottoms out: hole too shallow or full of dust; drill deeper than the plug and clear the dust (fischer; Homebuilding; Confast). 4) Wall cracks or a corner breaks off: fixing too close to an edge or wall too weak; keep at least one plug length from edges (fischer UX) and use resin anchors in crumbly walls (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'). 5) Plug disappears into a void in hollow brick or block: use a universal plug that knots, or a chemical anchor with a mesh sleeve (fischer). 6) Concrete screw jams or snaps: hole too shallow so dust packs the bottom, or wrong bit size (Confast). 7) Chemical anchor pulls out: hole not cleaned (up to 60% loss) or resin not cured (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'). 8) Masonry anchor failure modes in tension: pull-out, concrete cone break-out, splitting near an edge, or the steel itself breaking (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'). 9) Drilled into a cable or pipe: check with a detector first (Electrical Safety First).
- **related**: 
  - Hollow-wall anchors (toggle, molly, self-drilling plasterboard anchor): for plasterboard, hollow doors and panels, where a plain plug would pull straight out
  - Wood screw (incl. coach screw): what usually goes into a wall plug, and what you screw directly into a timber stud or batten
  - Internal tube anchors (star nut, expansion plug): the same expanding-sleeve idea used inside the end of a tube
  - Press-fit and expanding inserts (tap-in insert): expanding threaded inserts for holes in parts rather than walls
  - Blind threaded fixings (rivet nut, well nut): blind threads for thin sheet; a rubber well nut can also anchor in a blind hole by squashing outward, like a plug
  - Hang-on mounts (keyhole slot, French cleat): the hanging hardware usually screwed to the wall with plugs
  - Modular grid mounting systems (pegboard, Gridfinity-style grids): boards fixed to walls with plugs and screws
  - Metal brackets and plates (incl. furniture anti-tip ties): steel brackets screwed to walls through plugs
  - Threaded rod and studs: the rod set in resin in a chemical anchor
  - Eye bolts, screw eyes and hooks: often screwed straight into a wall plug
  - Stretch-release adhesive strips and double-sided foam tape: no-drill alternatives for light items on smooth walls
  - Two-part epoxy and epoxy putty: related resins; chemical anchors are a purpose-made version for holes in masonry
  - Heat-set threaded insert: the way to put a strong thread into a 3D print, not a wall

## 367 Hollow-wall anchors (toggle, molly, self-drilling plasterboard anchor)

*everyday fasteners & fixings.* Fixings for plasterboard, hollow doors and panels that spread out behind the board or bite into it, because a plain screw or plug would pull straight out of the thin, soft board

*Also covers:* Also covers spring, gravity and strap toggles, winged plastic anchors and finding a stud instead. Safety: ratings fall sharply in thin or damp board and in ceilings; go by the maker's rating for the wall type.

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A fixing for hollow walls such as plasterboard: you push it through a hole (or screw it straight into the board) and it opens out, spreads or digs in behind the thin board, so a screw can hold where on its own it would pull straight out
- **term**: Hollow-wall anchor (cavity fixing, drywall anchor): toggle bolt (spring, gravity and strap toggles), molly bolt (metal cavity anchor), self-drilling plasterboard anchor and winged plastic anchor
- **aliases**: 
  - drywall anchor (US); plasterboard fixing; cavity fixing; cavity anchor; hollow-wall anchor; hollow-door anchor (Wikipedia 'Molly (fastener)'; Eugene Fasteners)
  - toggle bolt; butterfly anchor; spring toggle; wing toggle; spring-wing anchor (Wikipedia 'Toggle bolt'; Eugene Fasteners; fischer KD)
  - gravity toggle: a toggle bar that swings into place by its own weight (fischer KM 10; Ramset GravityToggle (brand); Indexfix)
  - strap toggle; snap toggle; channel toggle; SNAPTOGGLE and TOGGLER (brands) (TOGGLER data sheet; Eugene Fasteners)
  - molly; molly bolt (often misspelt 'moly'); MOLLY was registered as a trademark in 1934 and is now used generically (Wikipedia 'Molly (fastener)')
  - metal cavity fixing; metal hollow wall plug; hollow wall anchor (fischer HM; Eugene Fasteners)
  - self-drilling drywall anchor; threaded drywall anchor; screw-in plasterboard fixing; self-drill fixing; 'EZ anchors' or 'Zip-It anchors' (trade names) (Art of Manliness; fischer GK); SnapSkru (brand, TOGGLER)
  - winged plastic anchor; plastic toggle; pop toggle; nylon toggle (Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor'; Art of Manliness; fischer DuoTec)
  - plasterboard plug; 'Rigipsdübel' or 'Gipskartondübel' (German names, from a Wikimedia Commons file description)
- **family**: everyday fasteners & fixings
- **other_families**: clamping, magnetic & friction connections
- **one_liner**: A hollow-wall anchor gives a screw something to hold on to in plasterboard, hollow doors and panels, either by opening out behind the board (toggles, mollies, winged anchors) or by cutting a coarse thread into it (self-drilling anchors), because a plain screw or wall plug would pull straight out of the thin, soft board.
- **complexity**: A few parts that act as one unit. Spring toggle: a machine screw and a pair of spring-loaded folding wings (Art of Manliness; Wikipedia 'Toggle bolt'). Gravity toggle: a single bar pivoting on a threaded rod (fischer KM 10; Indexfix). Strap toggle: a steel channel, two plastic straps with a ratchet, a sliding plastic cap and a bolt (TOGGLER SNAPTOGGLE data sheet). Molly: a slotted metal sleeve with a front flange and anti-turn spikes, plus its machine screw (Wikipedia 'Molly (fastener)'; fischer HM). Self-drilling anchor: one nylon or zinc-alloy piece with a drill point, a coarse thread and a screw hole, used with your own screw (fischer GK; Art of Manliness). Winged plastic anchor: one moulded plastic piece whose legs fold out when the screw goes in (Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor').
- **how_it_works**: Plasterboard is only about 10-15 mm of soft gypsum between paper, so a screw thread or an ordinary plug crushes it and pulls out. Hollow-wall anchors avoid this by bearing on the back of the board over a wide area: a toggle's wings or bar are pushed through the hole folded and then spring, swing or are pulled open behind the board, and tightening the bolt clamps the board between the wings and the screw head (Wikipedia 'Toggle bolt'; Eugene Fasteners). A molly's sleeve is slit along its length; turning its screw pulls the far end towards you so the strips between the slits buckle outward into 'petals' that press on the back of the board, while spikes under the flange stop it turning (Wikipedia 'Molly (fastener)'; fischer HM). A self-drilling anchor is screwed straight into the board and its wide, deep thread spreads the load through more gypsum than a screw could (Art of Manliness; fischer GK).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - join and hold: fixes pictures, shelves, rails, lights, TV brackets and cabinets to plasterboard walls and ceilings, hollow doors, panels and hollow block where there is no stud behind (Wikipedia 'Molly (fastener)'; fischer HM and KD; Art of Manliness)
- **motion**: none: once tightened the anchor and fixture can't move
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0: the board is clamped between the anchor and the screw head. A loose or spinning anchor (hole too big, board crushed) is a failure, not a feature.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - none of the listed behaviours: it is a fixed anchor point
  - Its own tricks: it is fitted entirely from the front, opening out in a cavity you can't see; a molly or strap toggle keeps its anchor in the wall when the screw is removed, so a fixture can be taken down and refitted (Wikipedia 'Molly (fastener)': 'permit repeated withdrawals and reengagements of the associated bolt'; TOGGLER data sheet: 'Reusable in the same hole')
- **holding_principle**: 
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): toggle wings, a toggle bar or channel, molly petals and the legs of winged plastic anchors open out behind the board and bear on its back face; the board is trapped between them and the screw head (Wikipedia 'Toggle bolt'; fischer HM: 'the expanding arms swing open and press onto the reverse side of the board'); a self-drilling anchor's thread gives 'a secure, positive fit fixing' in the gypsum (fischer GK)
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): plain plastic expansion anchors in plasterboard 'expand out when you drive a screw in them' and press on the sides of the hole (Art of Manliness); tightening any anchor also clamps the board
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): spring toggles open by a spring; a gravity toggle drops open by its own weight (Art of Manliness; fischer KM 10)
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: semi-permanent. Molly: the screw comes out and goes back in; the sleeve stays in the wall (Eugene Fasteners; fischer HM: 'Removal and installation of the attachment can be repeated without reducing load-carrying properties'). Spring (wing) toggle: 'when you remove the bolt, the wings drop into the wall cavity and are not retrievable' (Eugene Fasteners). Strap toggle: the bolt can be removed and the channel stays (TOGGLER data sheet). Self-drilling anchors unscrew; fischer's GK 'can also be screwed out like a screw' (fischer GK), and TOGGLER says ordinary self-drilling anchors 'tend to remove a chunk of drywall with them' (TOGGLER FAQ). Plastic toggles are removed by cutting off the flange and pushing the rest into the cavity, then filling the hole (TOGGLER FAQ).
- **reuse_cycles**: Depends on type: molly and strap toggle: many times for the screw, once for the anchor; spring toggle: once (the wings are lost when the screw comes out); self-drilling and plastic anchors: a few times, as the gypsum wears each time ⚑ [uncertain: no source gives a number of re-uses]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **tools_needed**: A stud and cable/pipe detector first (Anchor It; Electrical Safety First). A drill and bit of the size printed on the pack: about the sleeve diameter for a molly, much bigger for toggles (Eugene Fasteners: 'typically 2-3x bolt diameter'), e.g. 12 mm for an M3 fischer spring toggle and 30 mm for a fischer KM 10 gravity toggle (fischer). Self-drilling anchors need only a screwdriver or a cordless driver with limited torque (fischer GK; Art of Manliness). A screwdriver; optional molly setting pliers (fischer HM-Z 1); a knife and filler to remove anchors and patch holes (TOGGLER FAQ).
- **seal**: none: hollow-wall anchors don't seal, except that fischer's KM 10 gravity toggle has a rubber washer that 'seals the drill hole against moisture' (fischer KM 10).
- **load**: Light to medium, limited by the board rather than the anchor. Maker's recommended loads (safety factors already applied) for one anchor in 12.5 mm plasterboard: about 0.08 kN (8 kg) for a nylon self-drilling anchor, 0.15-0.18 kN (15-18 kg) for steel spring toggles in pull-out, and 0.20 kN (20 kg) for a metal cavity anchor (molly) in any direction (fischer GK, KD and HM load tables); another maker gives 24-30 kg for M4-M8 spring and gravity toggles in 12.5 mm board (Indexfix). Published 'ultimate' figures are breaking loads and are much higher: e.g. 265 lb (120 kg) pull-out for a 1/4-20 strap toggle in 1/2 in drywall, with 'Failure measured as breakage of drywall portion'; 'Industry standards recommend 1/4 of ultimate test load' (TOGGLER data sheet). Beginner guides give rough holding figures of 10-30 lb for plastic expansion anchors, 10-80 lb for threaded (self-drilling) anchors, up to 100 lb for mollies, with toggles the strongest (Art of Manliness); Wikipedia's table gives typical pull-out values of 50-150 lb for toggle bolts and 25-50 lb for mollies (Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor') ⚑ [uncertain: these guide figures don't say whether they are safe or breaking loads, so trust the maker's rating]. Stronger against downward (shear) load than straight pull-out (Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor' table), and 'Capacity is reduced in thinner drywall (3/8" or 1/4") and in ceiling applications' (Eugene Fasteners). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **adjustable**: no for position. Strap toggles adjust to the wall thickness with a ratcheting cap ('smaller ratchet interval adjusts more precisely', TOGGLER data sheet) and toggles on long threaded rods suit a range of board and fixture thicknesses (fischer KD).
- **play_and_precision**: not applicable as a moving joint. Holding strength falls as the hole gets bigger: it 'varies... inversely with variations in hole diameter' (TOGGLER data sheet), so drill the exact size. Big toggle holes can be hidden by the fixture or a washer.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): fitted from the front, but there must be enough clear space behind the board for the anchor to open, e.g. at least 27 mm for fischer's smallest spring toggle and 1-7/8 in (48 mm) for a SNAPTOGGLE (fischer KD; TOGGLER data sheet). Short self-drilling anchors need 'only a small amount of space' behind the board (fischer GK).
- **materials**: Anchors: zinc-plated steel (mollies, spring toggles, strap-toggle channels; stainless versions exist), zinc alloy or nylon (self-drilling anchors), nylon or glass-fibre-reinforced nylon (plastic toggles), high-impact polystyrene straps (Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor' table; TOGGLER data sheet; fischer DuoTec and GK). Boards they suit: gypsum plasterboard (9.5, 12.5, 15 mm and double layers), gypsum fibreboard, chipboard, OSB, plywood, hardboard, fibre-cement board, hollow-core doors, plaster, and hollow concrete block (fischer HM and KD; TOGGLER data sheet; Wikipedia 'Molly (fastener)'). fischer's nylon GK self-drilling anchor is 'Not suitable for gypsum fibreboard and tiled plasterboard' (fischer GK).
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: self-drilling and plastic anchors go in with a screwdriver. Moderate for toggles and mollies: you need the right board-thickness size, a bigger hole, and a feel for when to stop tightening so the gypsum isn't crushed (fischer HM; Art of Manliness).

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: hollow-wall anchors are bought; the maker's part is what is hung on them
  - 3D print (FDM): printable drywall anchors exist, such as a compliant (flexure) anchor whose braces swing out behind the board, threaded self-drilling anchors and pegboard anchors, printed in PETG (Printables models by Ptarmigan, Tennessee Mats, marcvivet, DP design and JM3D, read through the Printables API)
  - woodwork: shelves, cleats and cabinets hung on stud walls, ideally screwed into the studs (Anchor It)
- **print_in_place**: partly: a printed compliant anchor (Ptarmigan) or threaded anchor (Tennessee Mats) prints as a single working piece but needs a metal screw or bolt; steel toggles, mollies and springs can't be printed.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Printable anchors: Ptarmigan's compliant drywall anchor uses 'crossing compliant hinges' that swing braces against the back of the board; print it in PETG ('PLA is too brittle and will break during installation'), on its side with no supports, at 0.2 mm layers, with 'At least 3, but preferably 4 perimeters' (infill matters little because the stress is in the perimeters). It is made for 1/2 in (12.7 mm) drywall, needs a 1/2 in (13 mm) hole and a bolt that goes 3/4 in (19 mm) into the anchor (Printables 730522, via API). Tennessee Mats' threaded anchor needs a 1/4 in hole and takes an 8-32 bolt; JM3D suggests PETG, at least 3 walls and 0.16 mm layers for a SnapSkru-style self-drilling anchor (Printables API). 2) Printed anchors are not rated: Ptarmigan's held 'over 75 pounds (34 kg)' in a vertical wall in his test but he 'would not recommend putting anywhere near that amount of weight on a single drywall anchor', and 'Installed in a ceiling... the anchor will support even less weight' (Printables 730522). Use them only for light things that can't hurt anyone if they fall. 3) For printed items hung on hollow walls, design the mounting hole for the anchor's machine screw (8-32, M4, M5 or similar), leave room for the molly flange or toggle-bolt head, and use a washer; fischer's HM needs the fixture (or a plate up to 6 mm thick) as a 'turning stop' while the anchor is expanded (fischer HM). 4) Spread heavy loads: a printed French cleat or rail screwed to several anchors or into studs is stronger than one bracket on one anchor ⚑ [uncertain: general advice; see 'Hang-on mounts']. 5) Pegboard and modular grids: parametric OpenSCAD anchors (DP design) let you match the hole and board thickness exactly. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search for: 'spring toggle' / 'toggle bolt' / 'butterfly anchor' / 'wing toggle' (with machine screw, hook or eye), 'gravity toggle', 'strap toggle' / 'snap toggle' / 'channel toggle' / SNAPTOGGLE or TOGGLER (brands), 'molly bolt' / 'hollow wall anchor' / 'metal cavity fixing' (sized by board thickness, e.g. fischer HM in several lengths) and 'molly setting tool' or 'setting pliers', 'self-drilling plasterboard fixing' / 'self-drill drywall anchor' in nylon or zinc ('speed plug' is a UK trade name) ⚑ [uncertain: 'speed plug' not checked on an opened page], 'plastic toggle' / 'nylon toggle' / 'winged plastic anchor', 'plasterboard plug' and 'universal plug' (fischer DuoPower also folds or knots in plasterboard), 'hollow door anchor', and a 'stud finder' (with live-wire detection). Link to a maker's load table rather than copying it: e.g. fischer's HM, KD and GK load tables and TOGGLER's technical flyers. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Find a stud first for heavy or tip-prone items: 'For really heavy items, you want the added security of fastening them with a screw to the wooden studs behind the wall' (Art of Manliness); 'In modern homes, studs are spaced 16 or 24 inches apart' (Anchor It); in Norway and much of Europe studs are typically at 600 mm (or 450 mm) centres (Wikipedia 'Wall stud').
  - Go by the maker's rating for your board type and thickness: fischer's recommended loads per metal cavity anchor (HM) are 0.15 kN in 9.5 mm board, 0.20 kN in 12.5 mm, 0.25 kN in 2 x 9.5 mm and 0.30 kN in 2 x 12.5 mm ('Required safety factors are considered'; valid for 'tensile load, shear load and oblique load under any angle') (fischer HM load table).
  - If a maker lists 'ultimate' (breaking) loads, divide by four: 'Industry safety standards recommend 1/4 of that ultimate load as a reasonably safe working load for each installed anchor' (TOGGLER FAQ); 'Apply a 4:1 safety factor for design loads' (Eugene Fasteners).
  - Load held away from the wall counts for more: 'Moving objects away from the wall (e.g., the far end of a shelf or using a TV mount that swings away from the wall) significantly increases the effective weight of that object, greatly reducing the allowable load of that anchor' (TOGGLER FAQ).
  - Figures side by side for toggles in about 12.5 mm (1/2 in) plasterboard: fischer KD spring toggle 0.15-0.18 kN recommended (axial pull); Indexfix spring and gravity toggles 23.7-30.2 kg maximum recommended pull-out; SNAPTOGGLE 1/4-20 265 lb ultimate pull-out (about 66 lb, 30 kg, at the 1/4 rule); Wikipedia 'typical' toggle bolt 50-150 lb pull-out; Eugene Fasteners wing toggle about 80 lb for 1/4 in (fischer KD load table; Indexfix FT BASC; TOGGLER data sheet; Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor'; Eugene Fasteners).
  - Thin board and ceilings hold less: 'Capacity is reduced in thinner drywall (3/8" or 1/4") and in ceiling applications' (Eugene Fasteners); avoid mollies in drywall thinner than 3/8 in (Eugene Fasteners); fischer's GK nylon self-drilling anchor drops from 0.08 kN in 12.5 mm to 0.07 kN in 9.5 mm board (fischer GK load table).
  - Keep anchors apart and away from board edges: Rawlplug's spring toggle needs at least 100 mm spacing and 100 mm edge distance (Rawlplug SPO).
  - Hole size: a molly's hole matches its sleeve; a wing toggle's hole must clear the folded wings, 'typically 2-3x bolt diameter' (Eugene Fasteners); a 1/4 in wing toggle needs a 3/4 in hole against 1/2 in for a 1/4 in strap toggle (TOGGLER data sheet).
  - Bolt length for toggles: at least wall thickness + fixture thickness + 1/2 in (TOGGLER data sheet); check the minimum cavity depth behind the board (e.g. 27 mm for fischer KD 3; fischer KD).
  - Choose a molly for the board thickness: 'The fixing should be selected based on the thickness of the board building material, to allow the very best expansion in the cavity' (fischer HM).
  - Self-drilling anchors: don't over-tighten ('the installation torque should be limited when using a battery operated screwdriver'); pre-drill with the setting tool in boards thicker than 15 mm (fischer GK).
  - Spring toggles: 'While tightening the screw, pull the fixture out from the wall or ceiling to prevent the 'wings' spinning freely' (Rawlplug SPO).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Towel rails, curtain rails and light fittings on plasterboard walls (fischer HM applications)
  - Pictures, lamps and light shelves hung on stud walls with spring toggles (fischer KD applications)
  - The plastic anchors that come in the box with a coat hook or towel rail (Art of Manliness)
  - A large mirror, heavy shelf or TV bracket on a hollow wall held with toggle bolts (Art of Manliness; Eugene Fasteners)
- **when_to_use**: Use a hollow-wall anchor when you must fix something to plasterboard, a hollow-core door or a thin panel where there is no stud or batten behind the spot you need (Wikipedia 'Molly (fastener)'). Self-drilling and plastic anchors are quick for light items such as pictures, coat hooks, towel rods and small shelves (Art of Manliness); mollies for medium loads and anything you may take down and refit, because the anchor stays and the screw can be removed (Wikipedia 'Molly (fastener)'; fischer HM); toggles, especially strap toggles, for the heaviest hollow-wall loads such as large mirrors, shelves and TV brackets, and for hollow concrete block (Art of Manliness; TOGGLER data sheet). For shallow cavities or unknown board thickness, short self-drilling anchors need little space behind (fischer GK).
- **when_not_to_use**: For heavy, tip-prone or safety-critical items (wall cabinets, TVs, bookcases, anything children could climb), screw into studs or noggins, or fit a timber batten across several studs, rather than relying on anchors in the board alone (Art of Manliness; Anchor It). Not for solid brick, block or concrete walls: use a wall plug or masonry anchor there. Avoid plastic and self-drilling anchors in ceilings and for loads that pull straight out; a printed anchor's designer warns that 'Installed in a ceiling where the force is directed straight away from the drywall the anchor will support even less weight' (Printables 730522), and Eugene Fasteners says capacity is reduced in ceilings. Avoid mollies for loads over about 100 lb or in drywall under 3/8 in (Eugene Fasteners). Don't use fischer's nylon GK in gypsum fibreboard or tiled plasterboard (fischer GK). Don't fix into wet or damaged board: 'Gypsum will soften with exposure to moisture' (Wikipedia 'Drywall'). If a stud is right behind the spot, a plain wood screw into the stud is stronger than any anchor (Wikipedia 'Wall stud').
- **environment_limits**: Gypsum board weakens when damp: 'Gypsum will soften with exposure to moisture' (Wikipedia 'Drywall'); fischer's nylon GK loads are 'Valid for installation and use in dry base material for temperatures in the substrate up to +24 °C (resp. short term up to +40 °C)' (fischer GK load table). Strap-toggle makers note that 'The age, condition, and capacity of the substrate must be considered' (TOGGLER data sheet). Zinc-plated steel anchors can rust in wet rooms; stainless versions are sold (TOGGLER data sheet lists 304 stainless channels, and gives 350 hours to red rust in salt spray for its zinc plating). Plastic anchors may creep or loosen under a constant heavy load or vibration ⚑ [uncertain: no opened source gives data]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **safety_notes**: Check for hidden cables and pipes before drilling: 'A common error is accidentally drilling, nailing, or screwing into cables hidden inside your walls. A quality cable detector can help track buried cables before you start work', and buried cables must run 'either horizontally or vertically from electrical accessories like socket-outlets or switches' (Electrical Safety First, UK); a damaged gas pipe 'can result in a gas leak, fire or explosion' (Cadent Gas). Ratings fall sharply in thin or damp board and in ceilings: 'Capacity is reduced in thinner drywall (3/8" or 1/4") and in ceiling applications' (Eugene Fasteners); use only anchors the maker lists for overhead use (e.g. Rawlplug's spring toggle is 'Suitable for overhead applications', Rawlplug SPO), since a ceiling failure drops the load on whoever is below. Go by the maker's rating for your wall type and thickness, and remember a published 'ultimate' load is the breaking load, not the safe load (TOGGLER FAQ). Anchor tip-prone furniture and TVs, ideally into studs (US CPSC, Anchor It). A strong anchor can tear a large piece out of the board when it fails ⚑ [uncertain: general observation; SNAPTOGGLE failures were measured as breakage of the drywall]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **common_failures**: 1) Plastic anchor spins or pulls out: hole too big, board crumbly, or anchor overloaded; use a toggle or molly, or a stud. 2) Over-tightening crushes the gypsum or strips the anchor: 'Keep turning until you feel resistance, but don't overtighten — you don't want to strip it or break the drywall' (Art of Manliness); limit driver torque on self-drilling anchors (fischer GK). 3) Spring toggle wings spin instead of tightening: pull the fixture outward while turning the screw (Rawlplug SPO). 4) Toggle wings lost in the wall: with a wing toggle, 'when you remove the bolt, the wings drop into the wall cavity and are not retrievable' (Eugene Fasteners), so the bolt has to pass through the item before the wings go into the hole ⚑ [uncertain: inferred from how it works]; strap toggles avoid this because they 'Pre-installs without the bolt' (TOGGLER data sheet), and mollies keep their sleeve in the wall. 5) Molly won't expand or the flange spins: wrong length for the board thickness, or no turning stop; choose by board thickness and use the setting pliers or the fixture as a stop (fischer HM). 6) Anchor won't open: cavity too shallow, or it hits a stud, pipe or insulation behind (fischer KD minimum cavity depth). 7) Self-drilling anchor jams on a stud or metal channel behind the board: screw into the stud instead ⚑ [uncertain: general advice]. 8) Whole patch of board pulls out: load too high or held too far from the wall; SNAPTOGGLE test failures were 'breakage of drywall' (TOGGLER data sheet; TOGGLER FAQ). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **related**: 
  - Wall plug (expansion anchor for solid walls): for brick, block and concrete; universal plugs also fold or knot in plasterboard
  - Wood screw: screwing into a timber stud or batten behind the board is stronger than any hollow-wall anchor
  - Blind threaded fixings (rivet nut, well nut): the same blind-fixing idea for thin sheet and tube; Wikipedia compares the well nut to a molly bolt
  - Hang-on mounts (keyhole slot, French cleat): a cleat spreads one heavy item over several anchors or studs
  - Modular grid mounting systems (pegboard, Gridfinity-style grids): wall boards often fixed with hollow-wall anchors or into studs
  - Metal brackets and plates (incl. furniture anti-tip ties): brackets and anti-tip ties fixed to studs or anchors
  - Eye bolts, screw eyes and hooks: toggles are sold with hooks and eyes for ceiling hooks and lamps
  - Stretch-release adhesive strips and double-sided foam tape: no-hole alternatives for light items
  - Compliant mechanisms & flexures: printed anchors such as Ptarmigan's use flexure hinges to open behind the board

## 369 Metal brackets and plates (angle bracket, mending plate, joist hanger)

*everyday fasteners & fixings.* Stamped steel brackets, straps and flat plates screwed or nailed across a joint to reinforce it or carry its load, such as corner braces, flat and T mending plates, joist hangers and framing ties

*Also covers:* Also covers corrugated fasteners, toothed truss plates and furniture anti-tip ties. Safety: connectors only reach their rated load with the fastener type, size and number the maker specifies.

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A piece of bent or flat steel with holes in it that you screw or nail across a corner or a joint, so the metal takes the load and stops the joint pulling apart, folding or sagging
- **term**: Metal bracket and plate connectors (angle bracket, corner brace, mending plate, joist hanger, framing tie, truss plate)
- **aliases**: 
  - angle bracket, L-bracket, corner bracket, corner brace, angle brace, angle tie: an L-shaped bracket screwed into the inside or outside of a corner (DT Online 'Knock Down Fittings, Brackets and Plates'; Wikipedia 'Tie (engineering)', Angle tie)
  - flat corner plate, flat angle bracket, stretcher plate (an L made from flat strip, screwed across the face of a corner) (DT Online; Wikimedia Commons 'Flat and bent angle brackets.png')
  - mending plate, repair plate, flat repair bracket, straight plate, T-plate, cross (X) plate (DT Online; Wikipedia 'Truss connector plate')
  - shelf bracket, heavy-duty shelf bracket (an angle bracket with a long arm, often with a diagonal web, that holds a shelf off a wall) (Wikimedia Commons 'Gray metallic shelf bracket')
  - shrinkage bracket, shrinkage plate, table-top fastener: a bracket with slotted holes that lets a solid-wood top swell and shrink (DT Online)
  - joist hanger: face-mount hanger, top-mount (top-flange) hanger, concealed-flange hanger (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8; Wikipedia 'Tie (engineering)')
  - framing ties and straps: hurricane tie (hurricane clip or strap), seismic tie, twist strap, rafter tie, perforated strap (Lochband in German) (Wikipedia 'Tie (engineering)'; Wikimedia Commons 'AnkerNagel&Lochband.JPG')
  - toothed truss plate, metal plate connector, gang plate, gang nail plate, nail plate, stud tie: punched steel plate with teeth on one side, pressed into wood (Wikipedia 'Truss connector plate'; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Gang-Nail was the name of the inventor's company (Wikipedia 'Truss connector plate')
  - corrugated fastener, wiggle nail (also wriggly nail, dog in UK trade use): a strip of corrugated sheet steel with one sharpened wavy edge, hammered across a mitre or butt joint (Isaac Kremer glossary, after Ching 1995)
  - furniture anti-tip kit, tip restraint, furniture anchor, wall strap: a bracket on the furniture and a bracket on the wall joined by a strap or cable (CPSC Anchor It; Consumer Reports)
  - seismic tie: the same idea for cabinets, bookcases, appliances and machines in earthquake areas (Wikipedia 'Tie (engineering)')
  - box corner (suitcase corner), mirror plate (hanging plate) as decorative or hanging relatives (DT Online)
  - Simpson Strong-Tie (brand) is the best-known maker of structural wood connectors; Strong-Tie is a common generic term in North America ⚑ [uncertain] *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **family**: everyday fasteners & fixings
- **other_families**: interlocking joints & joinery
- **one_liner**: A stamped steel bracket, strap or flat plate fixed across a joint with screws, nails or teeth so that the metal carries the load the joint itself can't, from a small corner brace that stiffens a wobbly frame to a joist hanger or truss plate that holds up a floor or roof.
- **complexity**: A few parts: the steel bracket or plate itself (one stamped piece with round fastener holes, sometimes slotted or triangular holes, ribs or teeth) plus the screws or nails that fix it to each part; a furniture anti-tip kit has two brackets and a strap or cable between them; a toothed truss plate is one piece whose teeth replace the fasteners (Wikipedia 'Truss connector plate'; Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes; Consumer Reports).
- **how_it_works**: The bracket bridges the joint: each leg is fixed to one part, so a force that tries to open, fold or slide the joint has to pull the metal along its length (tension) or across its fasteners (shear), which steel and a row of fasteners resist much better than end grain or glue alone (Wikipedia 'Tie (engineering)'). A joist hanger works differently from a flat plate: the joist sits in a steel seat and bears on it directly, while the hanger passes the load into the beam through its nails or screws (face-mount) or by hooking over the top of the beam (top-mount) (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, pp. 8-25 to 8-26). A toothed truss plate is pressed in so all its teeth enter the wood at once, and the wood squeezed between neighbouring teeth is less likely to split (Wikipedia 'Truss connector plate'). In every case the connector is only as strong as its fasteners and the wood or wall they are in, which is why makers specify the exact fastener type, size and number (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: join and hold: reinforces a corner, butt or mitre joint, carries a joist, shelf or rafter, or ties a tall piece of furniture to the wall so it can't tip
- **motion**: none: the bracket holds the parts still (slotted shrinkage brackets let a wooden top slide a little as it swells and shrinks, by design) (DT Online).
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 once fixed (a slotted shrinkage bracket deliberately leaves about 1 sliding direction so solid wood can move with humidity) (DT Online).
- **special_behaviour**: none: it simply holds. (Its one clever variant is the slotted shrinkage plate, which holds a top down while letting it move across the grain.) (DT Online)
- **holding_principle**: 
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the joist sits in the hanger's seat, and the plate's holes lock round the nails or screws that pass through them (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-25)
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): screw heads clamp the plate to the wood, and nails and truss-plate teeth are gripped by the wood fibres (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8)
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: removable when fixed with screws (unscrew it); nailed connectors are semi-permanent (nails can be pulled, damaging the wood), and pressed-in toothed truss plates are in practice permanent ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **reuse_cycles**: a few times: a screwed bracket can be moved and refitted, but each refit wears the screw holes in the wood; nailed connectors and truss plates are once ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **tools_needed**: Screwdriver or drill-driver (with a pilot drill) for screwed brackets; hammer, palm nailer or nail gun for nailed connectors; a pencil and stud finder, drill and screws for furniture anti-tip kits (CPSC Anchor It); a hydraulic press or roller for most toothed truss plates, although some can be fitted by hand (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-25; Wikipedia 'Truss connector plate'); hammer (or a special driving tool) for corrugated fasteners (Isaac Kremer glossary; Wikimedia Commons patent 'Tool For Driving Corrugated Fasteners').
- **seal**: none: brackets don't seal the joint.
- **load**: Light (small zinc-plated corner braces and mending plates for furniture) to heavy (structural joist hangers, hurricane ties and truss plates with published design loads). Strong: pulling along a strap or plate (tension) and sliding across its fasteners (shear); a joist resting in a hanger seat (bearing) (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, pp. 8-25 to 8-26). Weak: a flat plate on one face resists the joint folding about that face poorly, and an L bracket can bend open at its corner ⚑ [uncertain]. Usually the fasteners fail first: screws pull out of end grain or chipboard, or heads pull through the wood (woodgears.ca shelf bracket test: 'the screw often pull through the wood before the threads let go'); for shelf and wall brackets the wall fixing usually fails before the bracket ('Your mounting solution will probably fail before the bracket does!', Tom, Printables model 4623). Published loads are only reached with the specified fasteners: a hanger nailed with 0.148 x 2.5 in nails instead of the specified 0.148 x 3 in nails loses 15% of its capacity (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog). Design loads for joist hangers are set at the lower of the load at 3.2 mm (0.125 in) deflection and the average ultimate load divided by 3 (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-26). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **adjustable**: no for most brackets (position is fixed by the screw holes); yes, a little, for slotted shrinkage plates and brackets with slotted holes, which can be shifted before the screws are tightened (DT Online; see Slotted adjustment with a jacking screw).
- **play_and_precision**: Not a locating part: clearance in the screw holes lets parts shift before the screws are tight, so square the joint first and then fix the bracket ⚑ [uncertain]. A rigid bracket does help hold a frame square once fixed ⚑ [uncertain]. Joist hangers need the joist cut to fit: the gap between joist end and header should be no more than 1/8 in (3 mm) unless noted (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): fixed from the face of the parts with screws or nails; truss plates are pressed onto both faces of a truss joint (Wikipedia 'Truss connector plate'); furniture anti-tip kits need access to the back of the furniture and to a wall stud (CPSC Anchor It).
- **materials**: Usually galvanised (zinc-coated) or zinc-plated steel; stainless steel for corrosive or outdoor places; brass or decorative finishes for box corners (Wikipedia 'Tie (engineering)'; DT Online). Truss plates are structural-quality steel to ASTM A653, A591, A792 or A167 with zinc or zinc-aluminium coatings or stainless (Wikipedia 'Truss connector plate'). Fasteners must match the connector's corrosion protection and metal: hot-dip galvanised nails with heavily galvanised connectors, stainless nails with stainless connectors, because mixed metals corrode each other faster (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-5). Joins: solid wood, plywood, engineered wood and (with the right fixings) walls; small brackets also fix to chipboard, MDF and 3D prints, where the screws, not the bracket, limit strength ⚑ [uncertain]. Printed brackets: PLA for indoor static loads; PETG or other materials with good layer adhesion are also used (Printables models 4623 and 1549159). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: buy the bracket and the fasteners it specifies and screw or nail it on; the skill is choosing the right size and fasteners, and fixing into sound wood or a wall stud.

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: stamped steel brackets, plates, hangers, ties and truss plates are bought, not made
  - woodwork: the main use, reinforcing frames, shelves, decks, sheds and furniture
  - 3D print (FDM): printed corner brackets, mending plates and shelf brackets for light loads and for joining printed parts (Printables models 420879, 1549159, 4623)
  - sheet metal: home-made brackets cut from flat bar or sheet and bent, or ordered laser-cut and bent ⚑ [uncertain]; don't re-bend structural connectors that aren't designed to be bent (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes)
  - laser cut / CNC: plywood or acrylic gusset plates screwed across joints as a cut-from-sheet alternative ⚑ [uncertain] *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **print_in_place**: no: a printed bracket is a single solid part with nothing to assemble; it is fixed with bought screws.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Orientation decides strength: FDM layers bond at about half the in-plane strength (CNC Kitchen measured PLA at 63 MPa flat versus 31 MPa standing). For an L bracket, printing it on its side lines the layers up with the load, but then a countersunk screw hole can split along the layer lines; a three-sided corner bracket has no safe orientation, and printing it on its tip (about 45°) balances its legs (CNC Kitchen 'Stop Printing Flat'). 2) Designs 'specially designed for FDM' give every layer a wide area; one popular corner bracket comes in versions for countersunk screws and '_flat' versions for pan, round or truss heads, because overtightened screws break normal printed brackets (Printables 420879). 3) Prefer pan, round or washer heads to countersunk heads on prints, because a countersunk head wedges into the plastic and splits it (CNC Kitchen; Printables 420879; Printables 264348 note 'Use flat head screws so you don't split the layers' shows makers disagree ⚑ [uncertain]). 4) For mending plates, 100% infill or at least 4-5 perimeters round the bolt holes, and PETG or ASA for mechanical stress (Printables 1549159). 5) For shelf brackets, add a modifier with more infill round the top mounting hole, and fix to a stud or proper wall plugs (Printables 4623). 6) Plastic creeps under constant load, so for permanent or heavy loads use steel brackets ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) Never print safety brackets such as furniture anti-tip restraints: even moulded plastic tip restraints were recalled because they 'can become brittle and break over time' (Consumer Reports, 2024). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Angle brackets and corner braces (light and heavy-duty, with or without a stiffening rib); flat corner plates; mending plates (straight, T and cross); shelf brackets; shrinkage plates (slotted table-top fasteners); joist hangers (face-mount, top-flange, concealed-flange, skewed or sloped); hurricane ties, twist straps and perforated strap (Lochband); seismic and furniture anti-tip kits (metal brackets with a nylon strap or steel cable); toothed truss plates (gang nail plates); corrugated fasteners; connector nails and connector screws made for these products (e.g. Strong-Drive SCN/SCNR connector nails and SD connector screws (brand)) (DT Online; Wikipedia 'Tie (engineering)', 'Truss connector plate'; Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog; Consumer Reports). Catalogues name the variants and give loads for each fastener schedule; read the load table rather than guessing (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog).
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Install every required fastener: use the right type and quantity, fill all fastener holes, don't over- or under-drive nails (including with nail guns), and drive screws fully home (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes, rule 3).
  - A connector reaches its full design value only if you use the fastener type and number in the maker's table; a 0.148 x 2.5 in nail used where a 0.148 x 3 in nail is specified cuts face-mount hanger capacity by 15%, and box or sinker nails cut it more (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog).
  - Specify nails by length AND diameter: '10d' can mean 0.148 x 3 in common, 0.128 x 3 in box or 0.120 x 2.875 in sinker nails (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog); the Wood Handbook also calls penny sizes obsolete (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-2).
  - Don't mix nails and screws in one connector unless the load table allows it, and substitute screws only with the connector screws the maker lists, never ordinary screws (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog).
  - In double-shear nailing (a nail through the hanger, the joist and the hanger again) always use full-length common nails (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog).
  - Only bend products that are designed to be bent, and bend them once; field-bent steel may crack at the bend, and cracked steel 'will not carry load and must be replaced'; don't weld connectors unless the maker allows it (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes, rule 4 and notes i and r).
  - Cut joists to length: no more than a 1/8 in (3 mm) gap between joist end and header in a hanger, unless noted (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes, rule 5).
  - If nails split the wood, pre-bore holes no bigger than 0.75 of the nail diameter; a fastener that splits the wood won't take the design load (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes, note j).
  - Hanger and fastener should be the same metal type, or galvanic corrosion speeds up (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-5); use hot-dip galvanised nails with heavily galvanised connectors and stainless nails with stainless connectors (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog).
  - Joist-hanger design loads come from tests (ASTM D7147): the lower of the average load at 3.2 mm deformation and the average ultimate load divided by 3; truss-plate design values are the lower of the load at 0.38 mm slip divided by 1.6 and the ultimate load divided by 3 (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, pp. 8-25 to 8-26).
  - Face-mount hangers pass load to the header only through the sideways (lateral) loading of their nails or screws; top-mount hangers also bear on top of the header (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-26).
  - Solid wood tops need slotted (shrinkage) brackets so the wood can move with humidity (DT Online).
  - For 3D-printed corner brackets, angling the print at 45° raised layer-direction strength by 27% over upright (40 vs 31 MPa in PLA); aim for at least 45° because the gain follows an S-curve (CNC Kitchen 'Stop Printing Flat').

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Small L-shaped corner braces screwed inside a wobbly chair, table or drawer frame (DT Online: 'to repair broken items of furniture')
  - Steel shelf brackets holding a wall shelf (Wikimedia Commons 'Gray metallic shelf bracket')
  - Joist hangers holding deck or floor joists against a beam (Wikipedia 'Tie (engineering)'; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8)
  - Toothed gang-nail plates at the joints of factory-made roof trusses (Wikipedia 'Truss connector plate')
  - An anti-tip strap behind a bookcase, dresser or TV (CPSC Anchor It)
- **when_to_use**: Use a bracket or plate when a joint would otherwise rely on screws or nails in end grain, or on glue alone, and you need it stiffer or stronger quickly: corner braces and flat plates to fix or strengthen frames and boxes, T and cross plates where pieces meet in a T or cross, shelf brackets to hold shelves, joist hangers instead of toe-nailing joists to a beam ('toe nailing and end nailing is nowhere near as effective as using hangers', Wikipedia 'Tie (engineering)'), hurricane ties and twist straps to tie rafters to walls against uplift, slotted shrinkage plates for solid wood tops (DT Online), and anti-tip kits for any tall or heavy furniture or TV (CPSC; Wikipedia 'Furniture screw'). They need no joinery skill, work from one side, and can be added to an existing joint as a repair (DT Online). Printed brackets suit light loads and joining printed or thin parts (Printables 420879, 1549159).
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid visible brackets where looks matter; hidden joinery (Dowel and biscuit joints, Mortise and tenon, Pocket screws in Butt and lap wood joints) or glue usually look better ⚑ [uncertain]. Don't rely on a bracket whose screws only grip chipboard, MDF or crumbly plaster: fix to wall studs or use proper anchors (Wall plug, Hollow-wall anchors) (Printables 4623; CPSC Anchor It). Don't use light zinc-plated furniture brackets for structural jobs such as decks, floors or anything people stand on or under: use rated structural connectors with the specified fasteners (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog). Don't use rigid steel brackets across solid wood that will swell and shrink; use slotted shrinkage plates (DT Online). Don't 3D-print brackets for heavy, permanent or safety-critical loads, or anti-tip restraints (Consumer Reports; CNC Kitchen). For T-slot aluminium frames use Aluminium-extrusion connectors instead. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **environment_limits**: Moisture and outdoor use: plain zinc-plated steel rusts outdoors; in damp conditions treated wood corrodes fasteners and connectors, so use hot-dip galvanised or stainless parts and match the fastener metal to the connector (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-5; Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog). Wood movement: wood shrinks and swells across the grain, which can loosen connections and nuts on tie rods (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes, note k); nails in wood that goes through wet-dry cycles can lose up to 75% of their withdrawal resistance (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-3). Allowable loads in catalogues assume continuously dry Douglas fir-larch unless noted (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes, note f). Printed brackets: PLA softens with heat and creeps under constant load ⚑ [uncertain]. Plastic anti-tip restraints can become brittle and break over time (Consumer Reports). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **safety_notes**: Connectors only reach their rated load with the fastener type, size and number the maker specifies; fill every hole, don't mix nails and screws, and never use drywall or other substitute screws in structural connectors (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog and General Notes). Anything people stand on, sit under or rely on (decks, floors, roofs, child-climbable furniture) needs rated connectors fixed into sound wood. Furniture and TV tip-overs injure and kill children: anchor tall furniture to a wall stud with a metal-bracket anti-tip kit; CPSC credits its Anchor It campaign with a nearly 50% fall in tip-over injuries and deaths (CPSC 2025); Consumer Reports advises replacing plastic anchors with metal ones after millions of plastic tip-restraint kits were recalled in 2024. Check for hidden cables and pipes before drilling walls ⚑ [uncertain]. Cut steel brackets and strap ends have sharp edges and burrs ⚑ [uncertain]. Wear eye protection when hammering nails into connectors (CCOHS 'Hand Tools - Hammers'). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **common_failures**: Screws or nails pull out or the wood splits: too few, too short or wrong fasteners, screws in end grain or chipboard, or nails too near an edge; use the specified fasteners and pre-bore if the wood splits (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog and General Notes). Screw heads pull through the wood or bracket hole: use washers or bigger heads (woodgears.ca shelf bracket test). The wall fixing fails before the bracket: fix into a stud or use proper wall plugs (Printables 4623). Joist drops or squeaks in its hanger: joist cut short (gap over 1/8 in), hanger nails missing or too short (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes and SE blog). Bracket cracked from being bent on site (Simpson Strong-Tie General Notes, note i). Rust and corrosion from mismatched metals or plain steel outdoors (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Solid-wood top splits because rigid brackets stop it moving (DT Online). Printed bracket breaks along a layer line or splits round a countersunk screw (CNC Kitchen; Printables 420879). Plastic anti-tip strap or bracket breaks with age (Consumer Reports).

## 370 Nails, brads and tacks (ring-shank nail)

*everyday fasteners & fixings.* A smooth or ridged steel pin hammered or fired into wood and held by friction and the wood fibres gripping it; quicker than a screw and good for sideways load, but it pulls out more easily

*Also covers:* Also covers ring-shank and twisted nails, panel pins, brads and tacks, toe-nailing and clinching. Safety: eye protection when hammering; nail guns are a serious injury risk.

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A thin steel spike with a head that you hammer (or fire from a nail gun) straight into wood, where the squashed wood grips it; ridged (ring) or twisted ones grip much harder, and tiny ones (panel pins, brads, tacks) fix thin boards, trim, felt and fabric
- **term**: Nail (driven fastener), including ring-shank (annular) and twisted (helical) nails, panel pins, brads and tacks
- **aliases**: 
  - nail, wire nail, round wire nail, French nail (Wikipedia 'Nail (fastener)'; DT Online 'Nails and Pins')
  - common nail (smooth shank, heavy flat head, 'the typical nail for framing'), box nail (thinner shank and head), sinker (cement-coated framing nail) (Wikipedia 'Nail (fastener)')
  - ring-shank nail, ring nail, annular (annular ring) nail, annularly threaded nail, improved or jagged shank nail (Wikipedia; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8)
  - twisted nail, helical or helically threaded nail, spiral nail, screw nail, helix or decking nail (Wikipedia; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8)
  - panel pin and deep-drive panel pin (thin nails with a small cone-shaped head for cabinet work), veneer pin, hardboard pin (DT Online; Wikipedia)
  - brad: a small thin nail with a lip or very small head; in nail-gun sizes an 18-gauge (1.02 mm) fixing; pin nail or micro pin: 23 gauge (0.64 mm); floor brad: a flat stamped nail for floorboards (Wikipedia 'Nail (fastener)', 'Nail gun'; DT Online)
  - finish nail, lost-head nail, bullet-head nail and casing nail: small heads made to be sunk below the surface and filled (Wikipedia)
  - oval wire nail, oval brad: an oval shank that splits wood less (DT Online; Wikipedia)
  - tack, tin tack, carpet tack, upholstery tack (large domed brass or black head), brass tack, thumbtack, drawing pin or push pin (Wikipedia; DT Online)
  - clout nail (large-headed roofing felt nail), masonry nail (hardened, for concrete or block), duplex or double-headed (scaffold, formwork) nail for temporary work, drywall nail, roofing nail, cut nail, horseshoe nail (Wikipedia; DT Online)
  - spike: a large nail, usually over 4 in (100 mm) long (Wikipedia; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8)
  - toe-nailing, toenailing, skew-nailing or slant nailing: driving a nail at about 30 degrees through the end or edge of one piece into another (Wikipedia 'Toenailing'; USDA Wood Handbook)
  - dovetail nailing: nails driven in at opposing angles to improve their hold (DT Online)
  - clinching or clenching (clench nail, canoe tack): bending the point over where it comes out of the far side so it bites back into the wood (USDA Wood Handbook; Wikipedia). Not the same as sheet-metal clinching (press joining), which is a separate entry
  - nail gun, nailer, brad nailer, pin nailer, finish nailer, framing nailer, palm nailer (Wikipedia 'Nail gun')
  - sizes: metric '50 x 3.0' means 50 mm long and 3 mm diameter; US penny sizes such as 8d; the Wood Handbook calls penny sizes obsolete and recommends giving length and diameter (Wikipedia; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8)
- **family**: everyday fasteners & fixings
- **other_families**: interlocking joints & joinery
- **one_liner**: A pointed steel pin hammered or fired into wood, where the squashed wood fibres grip its shank by friction, used to fix wood to wood (and thin board, felt, fabric or leather to wood) quickly and cheaply; it carries sideways (shear) loads well but pulls out more easily than a screw unless it has rings or a twist, is driven at an angle, or is bent over (clinched).
- **complexity**: Single piece: a nail has a head, a shank (smooth, ringed, twisted, barbed or coated) and a point (USDA Wood Handbook Fig. 8-1; Wikipedia 'Nail (fastener)'). The tools are separate: a hammer (claw hammer, or a light pin hammer for pins and tacks), a nail punch (nail set) to sink heads, pincers or a claw to pull nails, a bradawl for small lead holes, and optionally a nail holder or a nail gun loaded with collated strips or coils (DT Online 'Nails and Pins'; Wikipedia 'Nail gun').
- **how_it_works**: Driving a nail pushes the wood fibres aside; they press back on the shank, so it holds along its length by friction ('The holding power of Nails depends upon Friction', DT Online; 'friction in the axial direction and shear strength laterally', Wikipedia). How hard it resists being pulled straight out (withdrawal) depends mainly on the wood's density, the nail's diameter and how deep it goes into the second piece (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Rings on the shank lock behind the fibres and twisted shanks turn as they go in, so both grip far better than smooth nails, especially in wood whose moisture content changes later (USDA Wood Handbook). Sideways (shear) loads are carried by the stiff steel shank pressing on the wood and bending, which depends much less on grip, so nails are good sideways; bending the point over on the far side (clinching), angling nails in opposing pairs (toe-nailing, dovetail nailing) or using a slightly undersized lead hole adds pull-out resistance (USDA Wood Handbook; DT Online).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - join and hold: fixes wood to wood, and thin board, roofing felt, fabric or leather to wood; small nails or pins also hold a glued joint while the glue sets, so no clamps are needed (Wikipedia 'Toenailing')
- **motion**: none: a nailed joint holds the parts still, though it slips slightly under sideways load (USDA Wood Handbook, load-slip curve Fig. 8-4).
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 when two or more nails are used. A single nail acts like a pin, so one nail alone can let a part swing round it; use at least two nails, or nails at opposing angles, to stop that ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - none: it simply holds by friction and by the stiffness of its shank. Ring-shank nails resist 'working back out once driven in', and duplex (double-headed) nails are made to be pulled out easily for temporary work (Wikipedia), but neither is a mechanism
- **holding_principle**: 
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the squeezed wood fibres grip the shank (DT Online; Wikipedia)
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): rings and twists lock behind the fibres; a clinched point hooks round the far side, 'forming a rivet-like fastening' (Wikipedia, canoe tack); in sideways load the shank bears on the wood like a dowel (USDA Wood Handbook yield model)
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed) for the joint itself. It is driven by hand with a hammer or by a powered nail gun (compressed air, electric, gas or a powder-actuated cartridge) (Wikipedia 'Nail gun').
- **removability**: semi-permanent: smooth nails can be pulled with a claw hammer or pincers (DT Online), usually leaving holes and dents; ring-shank nails resist backing out and twisted square-shank (helix) nails are 'very difficult to pull out' (Wikipedia); toe-nailing 'makes later dismantling difficult or destructive' (Wikipedia 'Toenailing'). Duplex nails and casing nails are made to come out more easily (Wikipedia).
- **reuse_cycles**: once: a pulled nail is usually bent and its hole is enlarged, so re-nail in a fresh spot; duplex nails can be pulled and re-driven a few times ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **tools_needed**: A hammer (claw hammer for general nailing, a light pin hammer for panel pins and tacks), a nail punch (nail set) to sink heads below the surface, wood filler, a bradawl or small drill for lead holes, and pincers or a claw hammer to pull nails out (DT Online 'Nails and Pins'); safety glasses or goggles (CCOHS 'Hand Tools - Hammers'). Optional: a nail holder that keeps fingers away from the blow (Printables models below), or a nail gun with the matching collated nails plus hard hat, ANSI Z87.1 eye protection and hearing protection (OSHA/NIOSH 'Nail Gun Safety').
- **seal**: none: a nail leaves an open hole. Exception: some roofing nails for metal roofs have an umbrella-shaped head with a rubber gasket that seals the hole (Wikipedia, convex head roofing nail).
- **load**: Light to medium per nail; many nails together carry heavy loads, as in house framing. Strongest sideways (shear): nails 'bend under pressure, and rarely snap' where a screw may snap (Art of Manliness), and the wood's condition affects pull-out far more than sideways strength (USDA Wood Handbook). Weakest pulled straight out (withdrawal), especially smooth nails, nails in end grain (50-75% of the side-grain value), in plywood (15-30% less than solid wood) and in wood that has been wetted and dried (as low as 25% of the first value) (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Ring-shank nails can roughly double a smooth nail's withdrawal load, and give about four times as much where wood moisture changes (USDA Wood Handbook). Worked example, calculated here from the Wood Handbook's equations for short-term maximum loads (not safe loads): a smooth 3.33 mm common nail going 40 mm into softwood of specific gravity 0.42 gives about 0.8 kN in withdrawal, and about 0.3 kN sideways at 0.38 mm slip, rising to roughly 1 kN at failure (softwood ultimate up to 3.5 times the slip load); the Handbook takes one-sixth of the average maximum withdrawal load as the long-term allowable load, about 0.14 kN (14 kgf) here ⚑ [uncertain]. Twisting: one nail is a pivot, so use several. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **adjustable**: no: once driven a nail can't be moved; pull it and re-nail. Duplex nails suit temporary jigs, formwork and scaffolding that will be changed or taken down (Wikipedia).
- **play_and_precision**: Not a locating joint. Nailed joints slip a little under side load: the Handbook's older design loads are taken at 0.38 mm slip, and the slip at maximum load is more than 20 times that (USDA Wood Handbook). A hammered nail can also wander off line in hard or knotty wood, and knots can deflect a nail out of the side of the work (OSHA/NIOSH 'Nail Gun Safety'), so use dowels or locating pins where parts must line up exactly.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind) for ordinary nailing, with the work backed up solidly; clinching needs the far side reachable to bend the point over, or a hard backing such as a clinching iron for canoe tacks (Wikipedia; USDA Wood Handbook).
- **materials**: Nails: mostly soft low-carbon (mild) steel of about 0.1% carbon; masonry nails are tempered, higher-carbon steel (Wikipedia). Finishes: bright (uncoated, 'not recommended for weather exposure or acidic or treated lumber'), cement or resin coated, electro-, mechanically or hot-dip galvanised, phosphate coated; also 300-series stainless steel, copper, silicon bronze, aluminium and brass nails and tacks (Wikipedia; USDA Wood Handbook). Joins: softwoods (usually driven directly) and hardwoods (pre-drill), plywood (resists splitting near edges better than solid wood), hardboard and thin ply (hardboard pins), roofing felt (clout nails), fabric, carpet and leather (tacks), plasterboard to wood framing (drywall nails), and steel connector plates to wood with the maker's specified nails (DT Online; Wikipedia; USDA Wood Handbook; Simpson Strong-Tie). Not for: 3D-printed plastic as the material being nailed, glass, or metal to metal; into concrete or block only with hardened masonry nails or cut nails (Wikipedia; DT Online).
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: buy them; the skills are choosing the type and length, hitting squarely, and keeping away from edges and ends so the wood doesn't split.

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: nails, pins, brads and tacks are bought
  - woodwork: the main use, for boxes, crates, frames, cabinet backs, framing and jigs
  - 3D print (FDM): printed clips, hooks and hangers with holes for a nail, and printed nail holders that keep fingers clear (see make_it_yourself); printed plastic is not a material to nail into
  - laser cut: plywood and hardboard parts can be pinned together with panel pins or brads, usually with glue ⚑ [uncertain] *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **print_in_place**: no: nails are bought steel parts; printed parts only provide holes or guides for them.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Don't hammer a nail through solid printed plastic: it has no fibres to grip the nail and the blow can split it along a layer ⚑ [uncertain]. Print the nail hole into the part instead: printed nail-in cable clips are designed round a 2 mm nail (Printables 'Nail-in Cable Clip [3-12mm Assortment]', akimakes), and a printed wall hook takes a two-inch finishing nail 'with a girth of 2mm or less' through a bore in the part (Printables 'finishing nail wall hook', FixedWithPlastic). 2) Hole size: make the hole a little bigger than the nail so it slides in without forcing the plastic apart; an Original Prusa is accurate to about 0.2 mm and moving fits start at about 0.3 mm clearance (Prusa 'Modeling with 3D printing in mind'), and small holes tend to print undersize, so test first ⚑ [uncertain]. 3) Angle the hole: a hook designed so the nail goes in at an upward slope, with the flat side pressed to the wall, uses the nail's sideways strength and lets the nail reinforce the hook 'to prevent bending and breakage' (Printables 'finishing nail wall hook'). 4) Orientation: 'FFF prints are stronger in one direction than another' (Prusa), so print clips and hooks so the load doesn't peel layers apart; one cable clip maker says 'Print flat on the bed for best results' (Printables 'Cable Wall Clip for Nails'). 5) Material for printed nail holders: PLA is fine, 'but PETG or ABS are recommended for better durability if you accidentally hit the plastic with the hammer', with at least 20% infill and 3 walls (Printables 'Safety Nail Holder | Finger Saver'). 6) Before nailing a printed hook or clip into a wall, check for hidden electrical wires and metal plates behind the board (Printables 'finishing nail wall hook'). 7) To fix printed parts to each other, use screws, heat-set inserts or snap fits rather than nails. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search by type and finish: round wire (common) nails, box nails, oval wire nails, lost-head nails, panel pins and deep-drive panel pins, veneer pins, hardboard pins, ring-shank (annular ring) nails, twisted (helical, screw-shank) nails, clout nails, masonry nails, cut nails and floor brads, duplex (double-headed) nails, drywall nails, upholstery and carpet tacks, joist-hanger (connector) nails, and collated nails for nail guns (stick or coil; pin, brad, finish and framing sizes); hot-dip galvanised or stainless versions for outdoor and treated wood (Wikipedia 'Nail (fastener)', 'Nail gun'; DT Online 'Nails and Pins'). Tools: claw hammer, pin hammer, nail punch (nail set), nail puller, pincers, bradawl, magnetic push-pin tool for small panel pins (DT Online). Specify nails by length and diameter rather than penny size (USDA Wood Handbook; Simpson Strong-Tie). Link to a supplier's nail catalogue page for sizes instead of copying tables.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Nail length: the piece being fixed (side member) should be about half as thick as the depth the nail goes into the holding piece (USDA Wood Handbook 2021, ch. 8, p. 8-6), which makes the nail about three times as long as the fixed piece is thick (calculated here). For full sideways strength, go into the holding piece at least 10 nail diameters in dense wood (specific gravity over 0.61) and 14 diameters in light wood (under 0.42) (p. 8-6).
  - Edge and end distance: nail no closer to the edge of the side member than half its thickness, and no closer to the end than its thickness; smaller nails can go closer (USDA Wood Handbook pp. 8-7 to 8-8). Stagger nails instead of lining them up along the grain, to avoid splitting (DT Online 'Nails and Pins'; Wikimedia Commons 'Nail row unfortunate.svg').
  - Lead holes of about 90% of the shank diameter give 'somewhat greater withdrawal resistance' and reduce splitting, particularly in dense woods (USDA Wood Handbook p. 8-5); pre-drill hardwoods and near ends, or use a bradawl (DT Online).
  - Toe-nailing: use the largest nail that won't split the wood, start about one-third of the nail's length from the end of the attached piece, drive at 30 degrees to it, and bury the whole shank without mashing the wood with hammer blows (USDA Wood Handbook p. 8-5); drive toenails in opposing pairs where possible (Wikipedia 'Toenailing').
  - Clinching: a clinched smooth nail gives 45% to 170% more withdrawal resistance than an unclinched one in dry or green wood pulled soon after driving, and 250% to 460% more in green wood that then seasons; clinching across the grain gives about 20% more than along it; it is mainly used for boxes, crates and containers (USDA Wood Handbook pp. 8-5 to 8-6).
  - End grain: withdrawal is only 50% to 75% of the side-grain value (USDA Wood Handbook p. 8-5) and sideways resistance about two-thirds, with erratic results (p. 8-8), so don't rely on nails driven straight into end grain; toe-nail, or use a metal bracket or joist hanger instead (Wikipedia 'Toenailing').
  - Ring-shank nails: can double the immediate withdrawal value of smooth nails and give about four times as much where wood moisture changes, but then the head can pull into or through the wood, so head size can become the limit (USDA Wood Handbook pp. 8-4, 8-26).
  - Angled (stitched) nailing at alternating plus and minus 60 degrees gave 42% more peak withdrawal load per nail for 8d common nails and 24-48% more withdrawal energy for all the nail types tested (Luszczki, Clapp, Davids and Lopez-Anido 2013, Forest Products Journal 63(5/6), summary on the University of Maine Composites Center site).
  - Design load: the Wood Handbook has usually taken one-sixth of the average maximum withdrawal load as the allowable long-term load (USDA Wood Handbook p. 8-6).
  - Metal connectors: use exactly the nail the maker specifies, by length and diameter; in Simpson Strong-Tie's example a substituted nail cut the connector's capacity by 15%, and nails and screws must not be mixed in one connector unless the load table allows it (Simpson Strong-Tie Structural Engineering Blog, 2016).
  - Fine work: drive nails to within a few millimetres of the surface, then punch them below it with a nail punch and fill the hole (DT Online).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - House and shed framing: wall studs toe-nailed to the sole plate, and blocking between joists (Wikipedia 'Toenailing')
  - Wooden pallets and crates, often assembled with twisted (helical) nails or clinched nails (Wikipedia 'Nail (fastener)'; USDA Wood Handbook)
  - The thin hardboard back of a cupboard or the mouldings on a picture frame, fixed with panel pins or brads (DT Online; Wikipedia 'Nail gun')
  - Upholstery tacks with domed brass heads on a chair, carpet tacks on floorboards, and roofing felt held by large-headed clout nails (DT Online)
- **when_to_use**: Use nails to join wood to wood quickly and cheaply where the load is mainly sideways (shear): frames, sheds, crates, boxes, jigs, fences and studwork, and anywhere many fasteners are needed fast, since a hammer or nail gun is much quicker than driving screws (Wikipedia 'Nail gun'). Nails 'bend under pressure, and rarely snap' when loaded sideways (Art of Manliness), so they suit framing. Use panel pins and brads to fix thin boards, backs, beading and mouldings, and to hold glued joints while the glue sets (DT Online; Wikipedia 'Toenailing'); use ring-shank or twisted nails where the joint must not work loose, such as floors, decking and sheet materials (Wikipedia; USDA Wood Handbook); use tacks for fabric, carpet and leather; use toe-nailing where you can't nail through the face of a piece, such as fixing an upright to a base plate (Wikipedia 'Toenailing').
- **when_not_to_use**: Not where the main load pulls the fastener straight out, such as a shelf hanging from a ceiling or a tread that must not lift: use a wood screw or coach screw, which holds better in withdrawal. Not where the part must come apart again: use screws, knock-down fittings or bolts (or duplex nails for temporary work). Not straight into end grain for load (only 50-75% of the side-grain hold: USDA Wood Handbook); use toe-nailing, a metal bracket, a joist hanger or a joint such as a dowel or mortise and tenon. Not into fine hardwood near edges without pre-drilling, or it splits. Not for metal connector plates unless you use the exact nails the maker specifies (Simpson Strong-Tie). Not into 3D-printed parts, acrylic or other brittle plastics; give the printed part a hole for the nail, or use screws, heat-set inserts or glue. Not into masonry with ordinary nails: use masonry nails or a Wall plug and screw. Not to fix mains cables: use the fixings made for them and follow local wiring rules.
- **environment_limits**: Water and humidity: smooth nails in wood that is wetted and dried, or green wood that dries, can lose most of their grip, down to 25% of the value just after driving, and even in steady dry wood the grip falls slowly as the fibres relax; ring-shank and twisted nails hold about four times better under moisture changes (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Cement coatings help only at first: in softwoods the gain 'drops off significantly after a month or so' (USDA Wood Handbook). Corrosion: bright nails are not for weather or treated wood (Wikipedia); in wet treated wood use 300-series stainless, copper or silicon bronze (USDA Wood Handbook), hot-dip galvanised nails with galvanised connectors, and the same metal for nail and hanger to avoid galvanic corrosion (USDA Wood Handbook; Simpson Strong-Tie). Staining: steel reacts with some woods' extractives in the wet, so use stainless, aluminium or hot-dip galvanised nails on visible outdoor wood (USDA Wood Handbook); brass tacks resist sweat on furniture (Wikipedia). Drying after assembly can also loosen joints as the wood shrinks (USDA Wood Handbook, clinching).
- **safety_notes**: Wear safety glasses or goggles when hammering, strike squarely, avoid glancing blows, and never strike one hammer against another hammer or other hard metal (CCOHS 'Hand Tools - Hammers'). Masonry nails and hardened nails are brittle and can break and fly if not hit straight, so eye protection matters even more (DT Online; USDA Wood Handbook). Keep fingers clear of small nails, for example with a printed nail holder. Nail guns are a serious injury risk: they cause an estimated 37,000 emergency-room visits a year in the US (OSHA) or about 42,000 (CDC figure quoted by Wikipedia 'Nail gun'), and more than half of reported injuries are to hands and fingers (OSHA/NIOSH guide). The risk is 'twice as high' with a contact (bump) trigger as with a full sequential trigger, so use a sequential trigger; keep your other hand at least 12 inches (30 cm) from the nailing point, or use clamps; check for knots, nails and metal straps that cause blow-outs and ricochets; take extra care when toe-nailing, since the gun can slip; disconnect the air supply before clearing jams, passing the tool or leaving it; never disable the safety contact; and wear a hard hat, ANSI Z87.1 eye protection and hearing protection (OSHA/NIOSH 'Nail Gun Safety: A Guide for Construction Contractors', 2011). DT Online calls nail guns 'extremely dangerous and not suitable for school use'. Before nailing into a wall, check for hidden cables and pipes (Printables 'finishing nail wall hook').
- **common_failures**: Wood splits: nail too close to an end or edge, nails in a line along the grain, a sharp point in dense wood, or too big a nail; keep the edge and end distances, stagger the nails, pre-drill at about 90% of the shank diameter or blunt the point (USDA Wood Handbook; DT Online). Nail bends while driving: a dirty or glancing hammer face, or hard wood; keep the hammer face clean and strike squarely (DT Online; CCOHS), or pre-drill. Joint pulls apart: smooth nails in end grain or in wood that has been wet and dry; use ring-shank or twisted nails, toe-nail or dovetail-nail in opposing pairs, or clinch them (USDA Wood Handbook; DT Online). Head pulls through thin or soft material: ring-shank nails can out-hold their heads, so use nails with larger heads or a washer-headed or clout nail (USDA Wood Handbook p. 8-26). Rust streaks and stains outdoors: wrong finish (USDA Wood Handbook). Nail comes out of the side of the work: it deflected off a knot or missed the piece below (OSHA/NIOSH). Hammer dents round the head on show wood: stop a few millimetres short and use a nail punch (DT Online). Connector joint below rating: wrong or substituted nails (Simpson Strong-Tie).
- **related**: 
  - Wood screw (including coach screw and Confirmat screw): holds far better in pull-out and can be undone, but is slower to drive and can snap where a nail would bend
  - Staples for wood and fabric: two thin legs and a bridging crown, quicker for fabric, mesh, film and cables
  - Metal brackets and plates (angle bracket, mending plate, joist hanger): steel connectors nailed or screwed across a joint so the nails work sideways instead of in end grain; also corrugated fasteners (wiggle nails)
  - Butt and lap wood joints: the simple joints nails usually hold
  - Dowel and biscuit joints: wooden pins that align and glue a joint with no metal
  - Drive-in fixing pins (roll pin, dowel pin, taper pin): steel pins tapped into drilled holes in metal or plastic parts, not driven into raw wood
  - Wall plug (expansion anchor for solid walls): the way to fix into brick or concrete without masonry nails
  - Blind rivet (pop rivet): a permanent fastener whose tail is spread on the far side, like a clinched nail, for sheet metal
  - Clinching (press joining without rivets): a different meaning of 'clinching', where sheet metal is pressed into itself

## 371 Threaded rod and studs (all-thread, studding, hanger bolt)

*everyday fasteners & fixings.* A rod threaded all along, or at both ends, used with nuts as a long bolt, tie rod, axle or adjustable spacer; a stud screws into a tapped hole so a nut can clamp a part over it

*Also covers:* Also covers hanger bolts (wood thread one end, machine thread the other) and printer frames built on threaded rod. Safety: plain plated rod is low-strength, so don't lift with it unless rated; dress cut ends.

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A long metal (or nylon) rod with a screw thread all the way along it, or just at both ends, that you cut to length and put nuts on, so it works as an extra-long bolt, a tie that pulls two things together, a frame bar, an axle or an adjustable spacer; a stud is a short one screwed into a threaded hole so a part can be slipped over it and clamped with a nut, and a hanger bolt does the same in wood
- **term**: Threaded rod (all-thread rod, studding) and stud (stud bolt), including the hanger bolt
- **aliases**: 
  - threaded rod, all-thread, all-thread rod (ATR), fully threaded rod, redi-rod, continuously threaded rod, TFL rod (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'; Portland Bolt 'All Thread Rods')
  - studding, threaded bar (UK trade names) ⚑ [uncertain]
  - stud, stud bolt: fully threaded stud; tap-end stud (threads of unequal length, the shorter end screwed into a tapped hole); double-end stud (equal threads at both ends); flange stud (fully threaded with chamfered ends); full-bodied and undercut (reduced-shank, waisted) studs (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod')
  - wheel stud: the threaded studs on a car hub that the lug nuts screw onto (Wikipedia 'Lug nut')
  - hanger bolt, wood hanger screw, stud screw: a wood-screw thread at one end and a machine thread at the other (Wikipedia 'Furniture screw'; The Handyman's Daughter; Wikimedia Commons 'Hanger bolt.jpg')
  - dowel screw, double-ended screw dowel: wood-screw threads at both ends, for joining two pieces of wood (Wikimedia Commons 'Double screw dowel.png')
  - coupling nut, extension nut: a long nut that joins two male threads, usually two lengths of threaded rod; reducing, sight-hole and left-hand versions (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut')
  - jam nut: a thin nut tightened against another nut to lock it, or a pair used to give a rod something to turn it by (Wikipedia 'Jam nut')
  - grades and standards to look up, not copy: ISO strength classes 4.6, 8.8 and 10.9 and stainless A2 (304) and A4 (316) for metric rod, marked by end colour (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'); ASTM A307, F1554, A193 B7, A449 and SAE J429 for inch rod, with dimensions to ASME B18.31.3 (Portland Bolt)
  - M8 threaded rod frame: the rod-and-printed-corner frame of early RepRap 3D printers such as the Prusa Mendel (RepRap wiki 'Prusa Mendel') *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **family**: everyday fasteners & fixings
- **other_families**: clamping, magnetic & friction connections, adjusters, levellers & spacers
- **one_liner**: A rod threaded along its length (or at both ends) that, with nuts and washers, makes a bolt of any length, a tie rod that pulls parts together, a frame member, an axle or an adjustable spacer; a stud screws into a tapped hole and stays there so a part can be clamped over it with a nut, and a hanger bolt does the same job in wood.
- **complexity**: A few parts: the rod or stud, plus nuts and washers (often a nut on each side of every part it passes through, or two nuts jammed together), coupling nuts to join or extend lengths, and cap (acorn) nuts to cover ends. A hanger bolt is one piece with a wood thread at one end and a machine thread at the other. Example: the Prusa Mendel printer's frame is six lengths of M8 threaded rod for the side triangles plus front, rear and top rods, printed corner pieces, 83 M8 nuts and 93 M8 washers (RepRap wiki 'Prusa Mendel' bill of materials).
- **how_it_works**: With a nut on each end, or a nut on each side of each part, tightening the nuts stretches the rod slightly and squeezes the parts between them, just as a bolt does; because the rod is threaded all along, the nuts can sit anywhere, so the same rod both sets a distance and holds it (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly'; Portland Bolt). Threaded rod is 'designed to be used in tension' (Wikipedia): it is good at pulling, but weaker and more flexible when bent than a plain bar of the same size, because the thread cuts into its section ⚑ [uncertain]. A stud's short thread is screwed into a tapped hole and left there, the part goes over it and a nut clamps it, so the tapped thread in the part is not worn by every assembly ⚑ [uncertain]. A hanger bolt's coarse wood thread is screwed into wood, leaving a machine thread sticking out for a nut, knob, threaded insert or bracket (Wikipedia 'Furniture screw'; The Handyman's Daughter). A nut on an ordinary thread won't be pushed round by a pull along the rod, because most screws are self-locking (efficiency below 50%; Wikipedia 'Screw (simple machine)'), but vibration can still work it loose, which is why a second nut is jammed against it (Wikipedia 'Jam nut'). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - join and hold: through-bolting thick or odd-sized parts, clamping a stack of parts, anchoring into concrete with epoxy or cast-in, and suspending pipes and equipment (Portland Bolt); frames of rod and printed corners (RepRap)
  - tighten and hold tension: a tie rod pulled tight with nuts, or rods joined with a coupling nut that can 'tighten a rod assembly inward or ... press a rod assembly outward' (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut')
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): as an axle carrying ball bearings clamped between washers and nuts, as on the Prusa Mendel's belt idlers and on printed spool holders (RepRap; Printables); the bearing should do the turning, not the thread
  - change the kind of motion: turned by a motor, plain M8 threaded rod was used as a cheap lead screw on the Prusa Mendel's Z axis (RepRap); see Lead screw and nut for proper versions
- **motion**: none when used as a bolt, tie rod, frame or spacer; rotate when it carries bearings as an axle; rotate+slide (a turning rod drives a nut along it) when used as a crude lead screw.
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 when nuts are tightened on both sides of each part. Before locking, a nut has one combined freedom: turning it moves it along the thread. As an axle, the bearing on it has 1 (rotation).
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): an axial pull can't turn a nut on an ordinary thread, because 'A screw will be self-locking if and only if its efficiency η is below 50%' and most are (Wikipedia 'Screw (simple machine)'); vibration can still loosen it, so jam nuts or locking nuts are added (Wikipedia 'Jam nut')
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): turning the nuts or a coupling nut pulls a tie rod tight and the thread holds that tension (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut')
  - motion conversion (e.g. rotary to linear): only when the rod itself is turned as a lead screw (RepRap Prusa Mendel Z axis)
- **holding_principle**: 
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): nuts tightened along the rod clamp the parts between them, and preload and thread friction stop the nuts turning (Wikipedia 'Jam nut')
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the thread flanks interlock with the nut, the tapped hole or, for a hanger bolt, the wood fibres, and carry the pull
- **input_output**: Usually not applicable (it is a fixing). When turned as a crude lead screw: rotation -> straight-line motion of a nut, one thread pitch per turn (1.25 mm for M8 coarse thread ⚑ [uncertain]); low efficiency, so it is not back-drivable (self-locking) (Wikipedia 'Screw (simple machine)'); shaft in line with the motion. See Lead screw and nut. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: removable: nuts undo with spanners, and studs and hanger bolts can be backed out by gripping two nuts jammed together (Wikipedia 'Jam nut'; The Handyman's Daughter). Rod set in concrete with epoxy or cast in is permanent (Portland Bolt), and seized stainless threads may have to be cut off (Wikipedia 'Galling').
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: steel rod and nuts can be refitted many times if the threads are kept clean and undamaged; stainless and aluminium threads can gall and seize (Wikipedia 'Galling'); hanger bolts in wood loosen their hole if taken out and refitted often ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **tools_needed**: Two spanners (wrenches) or a socket set; to cut rod, a hacksaw, a chop saw (a cold-cutting blade leaves 'significantly less burring' than an abrasive one) or a threaded-rod shear; a grinder, file or cone-shaped chamfering tool (e.g. a Uniburr) to clean the cut end; gloves, since cut rod gets hot (Albany County Fasteners; Wikimedia Commons 'Gewindestangenschere Makita.jpg'). To fit studs and hanger bolts: two nuts jammed together, or a hanger-bolt driver bit in a drill (Wikipedia 'Jam nut'; The Handyman's Daughter); a drill for clearance and pilot holes, and a tap to thread a hole for a stud ⚑ [uncertain]. A ruler or a cut-to-length jig to set spacings (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly'). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **seal**: none: fluid can creep along the thread; where a rod passes through a wet or pressurised wall, seal it with sealing washers or thread sealant ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **load**: Medium to heavy in tension (a pull along the rod), set by the grade, diameter, nuts and the material being clamped; poor in bending and only moderate in shear, because it is 'designed to be used in tension' (Wikipedia) and its thread sits wherever it crosses a joint ⚑ [uncertain]. The grade matters hugely: unmarked metric rod is class 4.6 (tensile strength 400 N/mm2, yield 240 N/mm2), yellow-marked class 8.8 is twice as strong (800 and 640), and white-marked 10.9 stronger still (1000 and 900) (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'). The AISC allowable tension for ordinary threaded rod is P = 0.33 x Fu x pi x d^2 / 4 (Wikipedia); for M8 class 4.6 that gives about 6.6 kN (about 680 kgf) as a structural steel design value (calculated here), not a rating for lifting ⚑ [uncertain]. A tap-end stud in soft metal is limited by the tapped thread unless it goes in about 1 x its diameter in steel and 2 x in aluminium (Nord-Lock). A hanger bolt is limited by its wood thread's grip, like a coach screw ⚑ [uncertain]. Through printed plastic, the plastic crushes long before the rod fails, hence 'do not crush the plastic' (RepRap). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **adjustable**: yes: run the nuts along the rod to change a spacing, height, length or tension, then lock them with a second nut (Wikipedia 'Jam nut'); RepRap builders set each frame side to 290 mm this way (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly'). Coupling nuts join rods end to end and can draw them together or push them apart (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut').
- **play_and_precision**: Small: a loose nut on a thread has a little end play (backlash), but once nuts are tightened against both sides of a part there is none. Positions are only as accurate as you set them: RepRap builders use a cut-to-length jig 'until you can just barely fit the jig J1 between them' (RepRap). The rod doesn't locate parts sideways by itself, because it sits in clearance holes ⚑ [uncertain]. A rod that is not straight wobbles when turned: the Prusa Mendel notes wobbling 'if your threaded rods are not straight or your couplings are not aligned' (RepRap). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **access_needed**: both sides for a through-rod with a nut on each end; one side only (blind) for a stud or hanger bolt once fitted, and for rod epoxied into a drilled hole (Portland Bolt).
- **materials**: Made from galvanised, mild and stainless steel, nylon, brass, copper, aluminium and titanium (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'); finishes include plain black, zinc plated, hot-dip galvanised and stainless (Portland Bolt; Wikipedia). Joins or passes through: wood (through-bolting and hanger bolts), metal (tapped holes for studs), concrete (epoxy or cast-in anchors) (Portland Bolt), 3D-printed parts clamped between nuts and washers (RepRap), plywood and MDF. Stainless and aluminium threads gall easily, so lubricate them (Wikipedia 'Galling'); nylon rod is light and doesn't rust but is much weaker ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: cut it to length, clean the end and add nuts and washers; the points needing care are deburring the cut, keeping the rod straight, not over-tightening printed parts, and using a rated grade wherever a failure could hurt someone.

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: threaded rod, studs, hanger bolts, nuts, coupling nuts and washers are bought
  - 3D print (FDM): printed corners, brackets, knobs with nut pockets, spool holders and frame braces threaded onto rod (RepRap; Printables)
  - woodwork: through-bolting timber, and hanger bolts for legs, knobs and pegs (The Handyman's Daughter)
  - CNC: studs in a T-slot bed to hold down clamps, with printed knobs (Printables 'Knob for M5 threaded rod / stud')
  - laser cut: plywood or acrylic plates held apart at set distances by threaded rod and nuts ⚑ [uncertain] *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **print_in_place**: no: the rod and nuts are bought; printed parts are slid onto the rod, and nuts are pressed into printed pockets after printing (Printables 'Knob for M5 threaded rod / stud').
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Clamp printed parts between a washer and nut on each side, and tighten the nuts 'until they are firm, but do not crush the plastic' (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly'); plastic under constant clamping force slowly creeps, so re-check after a few days ⚑ [uncertain]. 2) Spread the load with washers, and large fender (mudguard) washers where needed; if you use serrated washers, put them only between nut and plastic (RepRap). 3) Clearance holes: make holes a little bigger than the rod so its thread doesn't chew the plastic as it slides through; printers are accurate to about 0.2 mm (Prusa 'Modeling with 3D printing in mind'), so test the fit ⚑ [uncertain]. 4) Set lengths with a jig: cut a piece of rod, wood or cardboard to the wanted distance and adjust the nuts until it just fits (RepRap). 5) Nut pockets: a printed knob or handle can hold a standard nut in a hex socket; one M5 knob uses a 'drafted hole designed for a snug fit', the nut pulled in with a screw and washer from the back, printed flat side down in ABS at 75% infill (Printables 'Knob for M5 threaded rod / stud'). 6) Orientation: 'FFF prints are stronger in one direction than another' (Prusa), so orient printed corners so the clamping force presses across the layers rather than prising them apart ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) Bearings on rod: let a ball bearing do the turning (a 608 bearing fits M8 rod), clamped between washers and nuts, instead of a printed part rubbing on the thread (RepRap Prusa Mendel idlers; Printables 'M8 threaded rod bearing wall spool holder': M8 rod, two 608 bearings, four M8 nuts, PETG). 8) Printed matching threads: BOSL2's threaded_rod() and threaded_nut() modules generate ISO/UTS threads to print (BOSL2 threading.scad). 9) Typical failures: cracked printed corners from over-tightening, nuts loosening with vibration (add a jam nut or nylon-insert nut), and wobble from a bent rod (RepRap). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search for: threaded rod, all-thread or studding, metric or inch, by grade (class 4.6, 8.8, 10.9, A2, A4; ASTM A307, F1554, A193 B7; SAE J429) and finish (zinc plated, hot-dip galvanised, stainless, nylon) (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'; Portland Bolt); fully threaded, tap-end and double-end studs; hanger bolts (wood hanger screws) and dowel screws; hex nuts, jam (thin) nuts, nylon-insert lock nuts, flange nuts, cap (acorn) nuts, and coupling (extension) nuts including reducing and sight-hole types (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut'); flat and fender washers; hanger-bolt driver bits (The Handyman's Daughter); threaded-rod cutters and cone chamfering tools (Albany County Fasteners). For hanging services from ceilings, buy hanger rod and fittings with a published load rating (Portland Bolt lists 'suspending pipe and mechanical systems' as a use). Link to a supplier's size and grade tables rather than copying them.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Use it in tension: threaded rod is 'designed to be used in tension' (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'), so use it to pull or clamp, not as a beam, lever or cantilever; for bending use a solid bar or tube.
  - Check the grade before trusting it: metric rod ends are colour-coded: unmarked class 4.6 (tensile 400 N/mm2, yield 240 N/mm2), yellow 8.8 (800/640), white 10.9 (1000/900), green A2 stainless, red A4 stainless (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod').
  - Allowable tension (AISC) for non-undercut threaded rod: P = 0.33 x Fu x pi x d^2 / 4, where d is the nominal diameter and Fu the ultimate tensile strength (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'); for M8 class 4.6 about 6.6 kN (calculated here).
  - Tap-end studs: thread engagement of about 1 x the nominal diameter in steel and 2 x in aluminium as a minimum (Nord-Lock 'The Experts: Thread engagement in a tapped hole').
  - Locking with two nuts: tighten the inner nut to about a quarter to a half of the outer nut's torque, hold it with a spanner and tighten the outer nut fully; a pair of jam nuts also gives a headless rod a place for a spanner, but jam nuts 'can be unreliable under significant loads' (Wikipedia 'Jam nut').
  - Joining rods: a coupling nut engages more threads than a standard nut, which 'helps to spread the force over a larger number of threads' and reduces stripping; sight-hole coupling nuts let you check how far each rod has gone in (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut').
  - Hanger bolts: drill a pilot 'slightly smaller diameter than the bolt (preferably the same size as the center shaft)', mark the depth with tape, and drive with two nuts locked together or a driver bit, never pliers, which strip the threads (The Handyman's Daughter).
  - Through-bolting wood: holes should let the rod or bolt be 'inserted by tapping lightly with a wood mallet'; a 1.6 mm (1/16 in) oversized hole gives slightly lower load, smoother holes bear better, and if the wood dries after assembly a snug fit loses much of its load because of shrinkage (USDA Wood Handbook 2021, ch. 8, pp. 8-16 to 8-17).
  - Cutting: a cold-cutting saw blade leaves much less burr than an abrasive one; grind off the burr and chamfer the end so a nut starts easily (Albany County Fasteners 'How To Properly Cut, Deburr and Chamfer Threaded Stud').
  - Rod-and-printed-part frames: a washer and nut on both sides of each printed part, set lengths with a jig, then tighten 'until they are firm, but do not crush the plastic' (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly').
  - Lifting: lifting equipment 'must be of adequate strength' and 'clearly marked to indicate their safe working loads' (HSE LOLER, for equipment used at work); ordinary threaded rod has no such rating, so don't make lifting points from it.

  - Pipes, ducts and equipment hung from a ceiling on lengths of threaded rod (Portland Bolt)
  - Table or stool legs and knobs screwed on with hanger bolts (The Handyman's Daughter)
  - Early RepRap 3D printers such as the Prusa Mendel, whose frame is M8 threaded rod and printed corners (RepRap wiki)
- **when_to_use**: Use threaded rod when you need a bolt longer than you can buy, or an odd length, since it 'can be cut to any length in the field and secured with nuts and washers' and can stand in 'when a headed bolt of the correct length is not available' (Portland Bolt); to clamp a thick stack of parts or tie two walls of a box, frame or bench together; to hang things at an adjustable height; as an adjustable spacer or levelling foot with nuts locked either side; and for quick, cheap, reconfigurable frames from rod and printed corners, as early RepRap printers did (RepRap). Use a stud where a soft or valuable part must be taken apart many times, such as a cover on an aluminium casting, so the tapped thread isn't worn ⚑ [uncertain], and a hanger bolt to give wood a machine thread for legs, knobs, pegs and brackets (The Handyman's Daughter). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **when_not_to_use**: Not for lifting, swings or anything holding people or heavy overhead loads unless every part is rated and marked for it (HSE LOLER); use rated eye bolts, shackles and rated hanger rod and fixings. Not where it will be bent or used as a lever (it is 'designed to be used in tension', Wikipedia); use a solid bar or tube. Not as a guide rod for sliding bearings or bushings: the Prusa Mendel uses separate smooth rods for its moving axes and recommends stainless or tool steel because 'manufacturing (straightness) standards are higher' (RepRap); see Linear guides. Not as a precise lead screw: use a trapezoidal Lead screw and nut or a Ball screw. Not as a bare axle where a part rubs on the thread: carry the load on a ball bearing or a smooth sleeve. Not in a joint where the bolt carries shear and must fit closely: use a bolt with a plain shank (see Ordinary screw joint). Not where something could catch on long exposed ends: cut it short or fit cap nuts.
- **environment_limits**: Corrosion: zinc-plated rod rusts outdoors and in damp places; use hot-dip galvanised or stainless steel (Portland Bolt offers plain black and hot-dip galvanised, and stainless 'when corrosion resistance is a priority'). Galling: 'Galling can often occur in screws and bolts, causing the threads to seize and tear free', especially in stainless steel and aluminium; grease or oil reduces it (Wikipedia 'Galling'). Mixed metals in the wet, such as steel rod in aluminium, can corrode each other ⚑ [uncertain]. Vibration loosens nuts; use jam nuts, lock nuts or threadlocker (Wikipedia 'Jam nut', 'Nut (hardware)'). Wood: through-bolted timber that dries after assembly loosens as it shrinks (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Printed parts: plastic clamped on rod creeps and softens with heat, so a PLA part in a hot place loses its clamp ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **safety_notes**: Plain zinc-plated hardware-store rod is usually a low-strength grade, and unmarked metric rod is class 4.6, half as strong as class 8.8 (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'). Don't use threaded rod to lift loads, hang swings or seats, or hold people or heavy things overhead unless every part, including the fixing it screws into, is rated and marked with a safe working load; UK law requires lifting equipment used at work to be strong enough and marked with its safe working load (HSE LOLER). Cut ends are sharp and burred: deburr and chamfer them; wear gloves, because rod gets hot when cut, and keep hands clear of the saw by holding the rod in a vice (Albany County Fasteners). Cover long sticking-out ends with cap nuts so they don't snag or cut ⚑ [uncertain]. Wear eye protection when cutting and grinding ⚑ [uncertain]. A turning rod (lead screw, mixer on a drill) can wind up hair, sleeves or cords ⚑ [uncertain]. When drilling walls or ceilings for anchors and hanger rods, check for hidden cables and pipes. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **common_failures**: Nut won't start on a cut end: burr from cutting; grind and chamfer the end (Albany County Fasteners). Printed part cracks: nuts over-tightened; tighten firm but 'do not crush the plastic', with washers (RepRap). Nuts work loose: vibration; add a jam nut, nylon-insert nut or threadlocker (Wikipedia 'Jam nut', 'Nut (hardware)'). Rod bends or sags: it is being used as a beam or is too thin; use a bigger rod or a tube (Wikipedia). Wobble when turned: bent rod or misaligned coupling (RepRap). Stainless threads seize: galling; lubricate before assembly (Wikipedia 'Galling'). Hanger bolt threads stripped: gripped with pliers instead of jammed nuts (The Handyman's Daughter); wood split or thread pulled out: pilot hole too small or too big ⚑ [uncertain]. Stud pulls out of aluminium: too little thread engagement (Nord-Lock). Rod stretches or snaps: a low grade overloaded (Wikipedia strength classes). Through-bolted timber goes slack: the wood dried and shrank (USDA Wood Handbook). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **related**: 
  - Ordinary screw joint (bolt and nut, machine screw): a headed bolt with a plain shank, better in shear; threaded rod is the cut-to-length version
  - Lead screw and nut (incl. anti-backlash nut): proper trapezoidal threads for turning rotation into straight-line motion; plain threaded rod is a crude substitute
  - Turnbuckle: a body with left- and right-hand threads that tensions rods, wires and ropes
  - Shims, spacers and standoffs: fixed spacers; threaded rod with nuts gives an adjustable one
  - Locking adjustment screws (push-pull screws, spring-loaded levelling screws): fine adjustment and locking of a position
  - Thread-locking hardware (nylon-insert nut, lock washer, split pin): stops nuts on rod loosening
  - Heat-set threaded insert and Screw-in threaded insert: give printed or wooden parts a machine thread for a stud or rod
  - Self-clinching fastener (press-in nut or stud): studs pressed permanently into sheet metal
  - Wood screw (including coach screw): the wood-thread end of a hanger bolt grips like a coach screw
  - Eye bolts, screw eyes and hooks: use rated eye bolts, not bent threaded rod, for hanging and lifting
  - Tripod and stand screw mount: a short 1/4-20 stud is the camera-mount version
  - Aluminium-extrusion connectors (T-slot nuts and brackets): the stiffer modern alternative to threaded-rod printer frames

## 372 Eye bolts, screw eyes and hooks (lifting eye, cup hook, screw hook)

*everyday fasteners & fixings.* A bolt or screw with a closed loop or a hook on the end, fixed into a part or wall so a rope, cable, spring or hanging object can be attached to it

*Also covers:* Also covers shouldered and plain eye bolts, and hook-and-eye pairs. Safety: plain eye bolts must not be loaded at an angle; bent-wire eyes open under load; never use unrated eyes or hooks for lifting people, climbing or swings.

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A screw or bolt with a metal loop (or an open hook) on the end, fixed into wood, a wall or a part, so you can tie, clip or hang something from it
- **term**: Eye bolt, screw eye and screw hook (fixed anchor eye or hook), including the shouldered lifting eye bolt and the hook-and-eye pair
- **aliases**: 
  - eye bolt: a bolt with a loop at one end, usually held with a nut (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
  - regular nut eye bolt or plain (shoulderless) eye bolt: for straight, in-line pulls only (Crosby 'Forged eye bolt warnings')
  - shoulder eye bolt, shoulder nut eye bolt, collared eye bolt: has a collar that bears on the surface so it can take angled pulls (Crosby; Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
  - machinery eye bolt, lifting eye bolt, lifting eye: fully threaded with a collar, screwed into a tapped hole in a motor, gearbox or cabinet; metric ones follow DIN 580, and the matching eye nut is DIN 582 (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'; Otto Ganter DIN 580 instructions)
  - forged eye bolt versus bent (turned) eye bolt or wire eye bolt (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
  - ring bolt: an eye bolt with a loose ring through the eye (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
  - screw eye, eye screw, lag eye screw, wire eye lag, screw thread eye bolt: a loop on a wood-screw thread (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'; 'List of screw and bolt types')
  - vine eye: a long-shanked screw eye for fence posts and garden wires (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
  - pigtail eye bolt, pigtail hook: an open spiral loop that a rope can be threaded into sideways (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
  - eye bolt with expansion anchor or shield anchor, for masonry (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
  - screw hook, cup hook (a small rounded hook with a shoulder, also called a half-ring screw), square-bend screw hook (L-hook), ceiling hook and swag hook (Wikimedia Commons categories 'Screw hooks', 'Cup hooks'; Bob Vila)
  - hook and eye (gate hook, cabin hook, safety hook and eye with a spring keeper): a hook on one part dropping into a screw eye on the other (gatelatchusa.com; see Basic latches)
  - S-hook and J-bolt (a bolt bent into a J): loose and bolt-on cousins of the screw hook ⚑ [uncertain: no opened page describes the J-bolt]
  - standards for rated lifting hardware: ASME B30.26 'Rigging Hardware' (covers eyebolts, eye nuts and hoist rings) and DIN 580 / DIN 582 (Hanford Site Hoisting and Rigging Manual ch. 2-26; Otto Ganter DIN 580 instructions) *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **family**: everyday fasteners & fixings
- **other_families**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware, locks, latches & catches
- **one_liner**: A screw or bolt that ends in a closed loop (eye) or an open hook, fixed into wood, masonry or a tapped hole to give a rope, wire, spring, chain or hanging object a fixed point to attach to; bent-wire eyes and hooks are for light loads only, while forged, shouldered and marked lifting eyes carry heavy, angled pulls.
- **complexity**: Single piece for a screw eye, vine eye, cup hook or screw hook (a loop or hook on a pointed wood thread). A few parts for an eye bolt (eye bolt, nut, washers), a masonry eye (eye bolt plus expansion shield) and a hook-and-eye pair (hook on a screw eye or plate, plus a separate screw eye) (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'; Crosby 'Forged eye bolt warnings'; gatelatchusa.com).
- **how_it_works**: The thread holds the eye or hook in place: a screw eye or screw hook cuts its own thread into wood like a wood screw, an eye bolt passes through a hole and is clamped by a nut, a machinery eye bolt screws into a tapped hole until its collar sits tight, and a masonry eye expands a shield in the hole (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'; Crosby). A rope, cable or clip then goes through the loop, which surrounds it so it can't fall off, or over the hook, which is quicker to use but lets things lift off. A pull straight along the shank only tries to pull the thread out, but a sideways pull also bends the shank and tries to open the loop, which is why plain eye bolts are only for straight pulls and why a shoulder or collar, which presses on the surface and takes the bending, is needed for angled loads (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'; Crosby). An eye bent from rod or wire has an open joint where the loop meets the shank, so a heavy load can straighten it, while a forged or welded eye is a closed ring (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt').

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - join and hold: gives a rope, wire, chain, spring, picture cord or hanging object a fixed point to tie, clip or hang from
  - lock, and release by hand: as a hook-and-eye pair, a hook dropped into a screw eye holds a gate, door or lid shut and is lifted out by hand (gatelatchusa.com; see Basic latches)
- **motion**: none: the eye or hook is fixed; whatever is clipped or tied to it can still swing and turn freely in the loop.
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 for the fitted eye or hook. A rope, clip or ring through the eye is loosely captured: it can slide round the loop and swing, but cannot leave a closed eye (it can lift off an open hook). A ring bolt's loose ring can pivot so it lies flat and reduces bending on the bolt (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt').
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - none: a fitted eye simply gives a fixed anchor point
  - quick-release (open hooks only): an open hook, cup hook or gate hook lets things be hung or lifted off in a second with no tools, which is also why things can fall off it
- **holding_principle**: 
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the thread sits in the groove it has cut in wood or in a tapped hole, and the closed loop surrounds the rope or clip (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8)
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the nut clamps a through-bolted eye bolt, and the shoulder of a shoulder or machinery eye bolt must be tightened flush against the surface (Crosby); a masonry eye's shield grips the hole by expansion (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: removable: screw eyes and hooks unscrew by hand or with a rod through the eye, and nut eye bolts come off with a spanner; each refit wears the thread a screw eye cut in wood ⚑ [uncertain]. Masonry eyes with expansion shields are semi-permanent ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **reuse_cycles**: many times for a bolt-and-nut eye bolt or a machinery eye bolt in a tapped metal hole, provided it is inspected and not bent or worn (Crosby: never use one whose eye or shank is bent or elongated); a few times for a screw eye in the same hole in wood ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **tools_needed**: Drill and bit for a pilot hole (wood) or clearance hole (through-bolting); a screwdriver or rod pushed through the eye as a lever to turn screw eyes and hooks, or a printed or bought drill-driver bit for eyes and hooks (see make_it_yourself); spanner for the nut of an eye bolt; tap and drill for machinery eye bolts in metal; masonry drill and the anchor's own tools for a masonry eye; washers or shims to seat the shoulder or line the eye up with the pull (Crosby; Otto Ganter DIN 580 instructions; Bob Vila).
- **seal**: none: nothing seals; seal the hole with sealant outdoors if water must be kept out ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **load**: Light to heavy, depending entirely on the type and its marking. Light: a 1-5/8 in hardware-store screw eye is sold for a 30 lb (about 14 kg) safe working load, and a 3/8 in x 6 in bent eye bolt with nut for 160 lb (about 73 kg), both marked 'Do not use for overhead lifting, support of human weight, athletic or playground equipment' (National Hardware). Heavy: a forged 3/8 in shoulder nut eye bolt is rated 1,550 lb (about 700 kg) and an M12 1,000 kg in-line (Crosby), while a DIN 580 M12 lifting eye bolt is rated 3,400 N (about 340 kg) along its axis (Otto Ganter). Strong when pulled straight along the shank in the plane of the eye; weak when pulled sideways, across the eye or at an angle: Crosby cuts a shoulder or machinery eye bolt to 30% of its rating at 45° and 25% at 90°, and plain (regular nut) eye bolts must not be loaded at an angle at all. In wood the usual limit is the thread pulling out, which grows with wood density squared, thread diameter and thread length, and is lower and erratic in end grain (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
- **adjustable**: Barely: a machinery eye bolt whose eye doesn't line up with the pull is turned into line by adding shims (washers) under the collar, not by backing it out (Crosby); otherwise the position is fixed once fitted.
- **play_and_precision**: not applicable: it does not locate parts; the eye is a loose loop and things clipped to it are free to move.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind) for screw eyes, screw hooks, masonry eyes and machinery eye bolts in a tapped hole; both sides for a through-bolted eye bolt with a nut (Crosby; Otto Ganter).
- **materials**: Usually zinc-plated or galvanised steel, stainless steel or brass for screw eyes, hooks and hook-and-eye latches (gatelatchusa.com; National Hardware), plastic-coated steel for cup hooks; forged carbon or alloy steel for rated lifting eyes; DIN 580 eye bolts are C15E steel or A2 / A4 stainless and are marked with maker, material and working load limit (Otto Ganter). Fixes into: solid wood and plywood (screw eyes and hooks), metal with a tapped hole or a through-hole and nut (eye bolts), brick and concrete with an expansion anchor (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'). Not into: hollow plasterboard or ceilings on its own (use a swag hook with a toggle, or find a joist: Bob Vila), chipboard or MDF for loads ⚑ [uncertain], or 3D-printed plastic as a screw thread for anything heavy (see making tips). Preservative-treated wood corrodes some fasteners, so match the hardware to the treatment (CPSC Outdoor Home Playground Safety Handbook). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to fit: drill a pilot hole and screw it in, or bolt it through; hard (in practice impossible at home) to make a load-rated lifting eye, so buy a forged, marked one for anything heavy or overhead.

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: screw eyes, hooks, eye bolts and lifting eyes are bought, not made
  - woodwork: screw eyes and hooks go into frames, posts, shelves and beams
  - 3D print (FDM): printed hooks, eye plates and hangers screwed onto a surface with metal screws, and printed drivers for turning screw eyes (see make_it_yourself)
  - CNC and sheet metal: a through-hole for an eye bolt and nut, or a tapped hole for a machinery eye bolt, in a plate or frame ⚑ [uncertain]
  - laser cut: plywood or acrylic parts with holes for eye bolts or screw eyes ⚑ [uncertain] *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **print_in_place**: no: metal eyes and hooks are bought; printed hooks and eye plates are single parts that are screwed or bolted on, so there is nothing to print assembled.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) For anything that matters, put a metal eye bolt through the print with a nut and a big washer on the far side, rather than screwing a screw eye into the plastic: the nut and washer spread the load, the same way Crosby and the DIN 580 instructions want a nut (and washer) on through-holes; screw threads cut into prints are 'not suited for applications where parts will regularly be assembled/disassembled' (Hubs). 2) If a machine-thread eye must screw into a print, fit a heat-set or press-in insert and keep at least one fastener diameter of wall round the hole (Hubs 'How to assemble 3D printed parts with threaded fasteners'). 3) Printed hooks and eyes are weakest where the layers are pulled apart: PLA test bars printed flat held 63 MPa but only 31 MPa printed standing, and layer adhesion is 'typically around 50% of the in-plane strength' (CNC Kitchen); so print hooks and eye loops lying flat, with the curve in the layer plane, so the bending runs along the lines of filament ⚑ [uncertain for every hook shape: CNC Kitchen found an angled test hook held no more than a standing one because hook loading is complex]. 4) Use plenty of perimeters (walls) and infill round the hook's bend and the screw holes, and PETG, ABS/ASA or nylon rather than PLA for anything warm or under constant load, because plastics creep and PLA softens in heat ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) Test a printed hook with more than the real load before trusting it, and never hang anything above people from a printed hook. 6) A printed driver bit or T-handle that fits over the eye or hook makes screwing eyes into wood much easier (see make_it_yourself). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Screw eyes (zinc-plated, stainless or brass), lag screw eyes, vine eyes, cup hooks (plastic-coated or brass), screw hooks and square-bend hooks, ceiling and swag hooks with toggle bolts, hook-and-eye latches and safety hook-and-eyes with a spring keeper (gatelatchusa.com), eye bolts with nut (bent or welded eye), ring bolts, pigtail eye bolts, eye bolts with masonry expansion shields, forged shoulder nut eye bolts and machinery eye bolts (ASME B30.26; e.g. Crosby G-277, S-279), DIN 580 lifting eye bolts and DIN 582 eye nuts, swivel hoist rings for angled lifts (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'; Crosby; Otto Ganter; Hanford ch. 2-26). For sizes and rated loads use the maker's table and the load marked on the part rather than a general table: Crosby's eye bolt warnings sheet and the DIN 580 operating instructions list them.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Plain (regular nut, shoulderless) eye bolts and bent-wire screw eyes are for straight, in-line pulls only: 'Never use regular nut eye bolts for angular lifts' (Crosby) and wire eye lags 'should not be used for angular loads' (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt').
  - Shoulder nut and machinery eye bolts for angled pulls (Crosby, 2023): in line 100% of the rated load, 15° 80%, 30° 65%, 45° 30%, 90° 25%; the pull must be in the plane of the eye, and the shoulder must sit flush on the surface (use washers or a countersink, never undercut the bolt).
  - DIN 580 lifting eye bolts give different figures (Otto Ganter DIN 580 instructions, 2012): an M12 is rated 3,400 N straight along its axis, 2,400 N per bolt in a two-leg lift at up to 45° in the plane of the eye, 1,700 N per bolt when pulled sideways at up to 45°, and must not be loaded at right angles to its axis. Sources disagree because the standards and test definitions differ; always use the maker's table for the part you have.
  - Thread engagement in metal: DIN 580 eye bolts need a screw-in depth of 1.5 x the thread diameter in steel above 340 N/mm², 2 x in aluminium alloys and 2.5 x in weaker light metals; on a through-hole fit a full nut (0.8 x d) and a washer (Otto Ganter). Crosby's drawings show at least 2 x the diameter of thread engaged in a tapped hole, and for machinery eye bolts a tap depth of the shank length plus half the diameter.
  - Bolted through a plate: if more than one bolt diameter of thread passes through, one nut tightened securely against the part is enough; if one diameter or less, use two nuts (Crosby drawings).
  - Don't thread a sling or rope from one eye bolt to another: it changes the angle and load on each eye (Crosby).
  - Screw eyes and hooks in wood hold like wood screws: withdrawal load rises with the square of the wood's specific gravity, with the thread diameter and with the length of thread in the wood; in end grain it averages about 75% of the side-grain value and is erratic; pilot holes of about 70% of the thread's root diameter in softwood and 90% in hardwood (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, pp. 8-10 to 8-11).
  - Hardware-store eyes and hooks come with a small safe working load and a caution against overhead lifting and human weight: e.g. 30 lb for a 1-5/8 in screw eye and 160 lb for a 3/8 x 6 in eye bolt (National Hardware), against 1,550 lb for a forged 3/8 in shoulder eye bolt (Crosby).
  - For rated lifting hardware, ASME B30.26 temperature limits for eyebolts are +30°F to +275°F (about -1°C to 135°C) (Hanford Site Hoisting and Rigging Manual ch. 2-26); DIN 580 eye bolts can be used from -20°C to +200°C without derating (Otto Ganter).
  - Close S-hooks and other hooks on play equipment so the gap is less than the thickness of a dime; open hooks are acceptable only at the uppermost attachment points of swings (CPSC Outdoor Home Playground Safety Handbook).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Two screw eyes and a picture wire on the back of a painting (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
  - Cup hooks screwed under a kitchen shelf for mugs, and a ceiling hook for a hanging plant (Bob Vila)
  - Vine eyes holding training wires along fence posts for vines and soft fruit (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt')
  - The hook and eye holding a shed door or garden gate shut, and the lifting eye screwed into the top of an electric motor or gearbox (gatelatchusa.com; Otto Ganter DIN 580 instructions)
- **when_to_use**: Use a screw eye or hook whenever you need a quick, cheap fixed point in wood: to hang pictures, lights, plants, tools and mugs, to anchor guy lines, springs, tension wires and garden wires, or to make a simple hook-and-eye catch. Choose a closed eye when the rope or clip must not come off, and an open hook (cup hook, screw hook) when things are hung and taken down often. Use an eye bolt with a nut and washer through a plate or frame when the load is bigger or the material is thin, a masonry eye for brick and concrete, and a forged, marked shoulder or machinery eye bolt (with its rating reduced for the angle) whenever something will be lifted or the pull is at an angle (Crosby; Otto Ganter; Wikipedia 'Eye bolt').
- **when_not_to_use**: Never use unrated hardware-store eyes or hooks to lift loads overhead, to hold a person, or for swings, hammocks, climbing or play equipment: the makers themselves say not to (National Hardware); use the swing maker's through-bolted hangers or rated hardware instead (Treehouse Supplies swing hanger instructions; CPSC). Don't use a plain eye bolt or screw eye for a sideways or angled pull: use a shoulder or machinery eye bolt, a swivel hoist ring, or turn the eye so the pull is in line (Crosby; Wikipedia). Don't screw a hook into plasterboard or a hollow ceiling on its own: find a joist, or use a swag hook with a toggle (Bob Vila; see Hollow-wall anchors). Don't screw into end grain or close to an edge where the wood can split (USDA Wood Handbook). Where a load must be clipped and unclipped often, a Spring-gate hook (carabiner) or Shackle and quick link on a fixed eye is safer than an open hook. For heavy equipment on a 3D-printed part, bolt through with a washer or use an insert rather than a screw eye in plastic (Hubs).
- **environment_limits**: Outdoors and in damp places use galvanised, stainless or brass eyes; preservative-treated wood corrodes some metals faster, so match the hardware to the treatment (CPSC handbook; USDA Wood Handbook 'Corrosion and Staining'). Steel eye bolts should not be left in aluminium for long periods because of corrosion (Crosby). Rated hardware has temperature limits: ASME B30.26 eyebolts +30°F to +275°F (Hanford ch. 2-26), DIN 580 eye bolts -20°C to +200°C (Otto Ganter); keep eyes away from aggressive chemicals and acid fumes (Otto Ganter). Vibration and shock can unscrew eye bolts; DIN 580 eyes left in place should be secured, e.g. with thread-locking adhesive (Otto Ganter). Wood that wets and dries can lose much of its grip on a driven fastener (nail withdrawal falls to as little as 25%) (USDA Wood Handbook, written for nails). Printed plastic hooks creep under constant load and soften in heat ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **safety_notes**: Never use unrated eyes or hooks for lifting people, climbing, swings, hammocks or overhead loads; hardware-store eyes are marked 'Do not use for overhead lifting, support of human weight, athletic or playground equipment' (National Hardware), and US rules forbid 'makeshift fasteners, formed from bolts, rods, etc.' in rigging (OSHA 29 CFR 1926.251). Plain eye bolts must not be loaded at an angle, and even shoulder eye bolts lose most of their rating at 45° (Crosby). Bent-wire eyes and hooks can open under load and let go suddenly (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'). Stand clear of anything lifted and lift with a steady pull, not a jerk (Crosby). Open hooks and protruding bolt ends can catch clothing and cause cuts or strangulation on play equipment; close S-hooks and cover bolt ends (CPSC). Check for hidden cables and pipes before drilling into walls and ceilings ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **common_failures**: Bent eye opens: a wire or turned eye straightens under a heavy or snatch load; use a forged or welded eye (Wikipedia 'Eye bolt'). Shank bends or snaps: a plain eye bolt pulled sideways, or a shoulder that isn't seated flush; use a shoulder or machinery eye bolt seated with washers and reduce the load for the angle (Crosby). Pulls out of the wood: thread too short, end grain, soft or rotten wood, pilot hole too big, or plasterboard with no joist behind it; use a longer screw into side grain, a through-bolt, or a toggle (USDA Wood Handbook; Bob Vila). Wood splits: no pilot hole, or too near an edge or end (USDA Wood Handbook). Hook drops its load: an open hook bounced or lifted; use a closed eye, a safety hook with a keeper, or a clip (gatelatchusa.com). Eye unscrews: vibration or a load that twists it; secure with thread locker or a nut (Otto Ganter). Rust and corrosion outdoors; use stainless or galvanised and match the metal to treated wood (CPSC). Eye bolt pulled out of a thin metal sheet or a 3D print: the load wasn't spread; add a large washer or backing plate (Crosby; Hubs).

## 373 Staples for wood and fabric (staple gun, hammer tacker, cable staple)

*everyday fasteners & fixings.* A U-shaped wire fastener driven by a staple gun or hammer tacker, whose crown bridges what it holds, which suits fixing fabric, mesh, film or cables flat onto wood

*Also covers:* Also covers upholstery and hog-ring uses and insulated cable staples. Safety: staple guns fire like nail guns; never fix mains cable with plain staples.

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A small U-shaped wire clip fired from a staple gun (or slapped in with a hammer tacker): its two legs go into the wood while the flat top bridges over fabric, mesh, plastic sheet or a cable and pins it down
- **term**: Staple (driven wire staple, tacking staple), fired by a staple gun (tacker) or hammer tacker
- **aliases**: 
  - staple, wire staple, construction staple, tacker staple, staple-gun staple (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)', 'Staple gun')
  - tacking: driving straight-leg staples into a solid base with no anvil, as opposed to 'stapling' paper with bent legs (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)')
  - staple gun, trigger tacker, hand tacker, forward-action staple gun; electric, cordless and pneumatic staplers (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'; Wikimedia Commons 'Tool-heavy-stapler.jpg')
  - hammer tacker, hammer stapler, slap stapler, slapper (Wikipedia 'Hammer tacker')
  - narrow-crown staples (crown about 1/4 to 3/8 in, 6.35-9.52 mm) for trim, cabinetry and upholstery; medium and wide-crown staples for sheathing and roofing (aftfasteners.com 'Narrow crown staples')
  - upholstery staples and outward-clinch (blind) staples, whose legs curl outwards inside foam (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)')
  - insulated cable staple, cable staple, arched or rounded staple, cable clip (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)', 'Staple gun')
  - fence staple (U-shaped staple hammered over fence wire) (Bekaert 'How to properly staple high tensile wire')
  - hog ring, C-ring: a sharp-ended C- or D-shaped wire ring squeezed shut with hog ring pliers, used for upholstery, car seats and wire mesh (aftfasteners.com 'Hog rings')
  - T50 (Arrow, brand) is a widely sold staple-gun staple type that many beginners search for; staples are matched to the gun, and 'staples from one manufacturer may or may not fit another manufacturer's unit' (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)')
  - standard: ASTM F1667 'Driven Fasteners: Nails, Spikes, and Staples' in the US (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)')
- **family**: everyday fasteners & fixings
- **other_families**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware, interlocking joints & joinery
- **one_liner**: A U-shaped wire fastener driven straight into wood (or a similar soft base) by a staple gun or hammer tacker, whose flat top (crown) bridges over whatever it holds, which makes it the quick way to fix fabric, leather, mesh, plastic film, insulation and low-voltage cables flat onto wood.
- **complexity**: Single piece: a staple has a flat or arched top (crown) joining two pointed legs, and is supplied glued into strips that load into the tool's magazine (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)', labelled diagram). The tool (staple gun, hammer tacker or powered stapler) is separate. Variants add a plastic saddle (insulated cable staples) or are closed with pliers (hog rings).
- **how_it_works**: The tool drives the staple's two legs into the wood in one fast blow; a hand staple gun stores energy in a spring and releases it as 'a sharp and powerful blow', because with no anvil behind the work the staple must be fully in 'before the workpiece has time to move' (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'), while a hammer tacker uses the momentum of a swing (Wikipedia 'Hammer tacker'). The legs hold like two thin nails, gripped by the wood fibres, so their withdrawal resistance grows with their size and how deep they go (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). The crown presses the material flat between the legs: it 'can bridge a piece and fasten it without puncturing, with a leg on either side', which is how cables are fixed, and it gives more bearing area than a nail head on thin materials (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'). Outward-clinch staples have a V-shaped crown and bevelled points so the legs curl outwards inside soft material, and hog rings are squeezed into a closed ring round wire or fabric (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'; aftfasteners.com).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: join and hold: pins fabric, leather, vinyl, mesh, netting, plastic film, insulation, paper and thin board, or a cable, flat onto wood; hog rings join mesh, wire and fabric to frames and springs
- **motion**: none: a driven staple holds things still.
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 once driven. Exception by design: fence wire is 'loose stapled' so it can slide under the staple as it expands and contracts (Bekaert).
- **special_behaviour**: none: it simply holds by its legs in the wood and its crown over the material
- **holding_principle**: 
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the legs are gripped by the wood fibres like nails (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8)
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the crown straddles the cable, wire or folded fabric so it can't lift off; outward-clinch staples and hog rings are bent into a hook or closed ring (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'; aftfasteners.com)
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed) for the fixing; the tool is manual (spring-loaded staple gun, hammer tacker) or powered (electric, cordless or pneumatic stapler) (Wikipedia 'Staple gun').
- **removability**: semi-permanent: 'Once a staple has been driven, it is difficult to remove without causing damage to the surrounding material' (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'); it is prised out with pliers, a thin screwdriver or a staple or tack remover (The Bulletin, Bend, 'DIY: Reupholster chairs'), leaving two small holes.
- **reuse_cycles**: once: a removed staple is bent and thrown away; the material can be re-stapled in fresh places a few times before the wood or fabric edge is shredded ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **tools_needed**: A staple gun (manual, electric, cordless or pneumatic) or hammer tacker loaded with staples made for it; for cable staples, a gun with a wire guide or a cable-staple gun, or hammer-in cable clips (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'); hog ring pliers for hog rings (aftfasteners.com); pliers, a thin screwdriver or a staple/tack remover to take staples out (The Bulletin); a hammer to tap down proud staples ⚑ [uncertain]; safety glasses (Toolstation PCT162 manual). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **seal**: none: staples leave holes; seal or tape over them if the sheet must be weatherproof (e.g. house wrap is taped later) ⚑ [uncertain]. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **load**: Light. Each leg resists pull-out like a small nail: the Wood Handbook uses its nail equation p = 54.12 G^2.5 D L (N, mm) for one staple leg, though 'no verification tests have been done'; for a leg about 1 mm thick going 10 mm into wood of specific gravity 0.5 that gives roughly 95 N (about 10 kgf) per leg, under 200 N per staple, as a short-term breaking value (calculated here; safe loads are far lower) ⚑ [uncertain]. Sideways (shear) strength varies with leg diameter to the 3/2 power, so each leg must be about two-thirds the diameter of a nail to match it (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Staples 'generally have lower holding power compared to nails or screws' (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'). Weak points: pulling straight out, thin fabric tearing out round the crown, and staples driven parallel to the grain splitting the wood ⚑ [uncertain]. Strongest when the load slides along the surface (shear), as when taut fabric is pulled across a frame. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **adjustable**: no: to move or re-tension fabric, pull the staples and re-staple (upholsterers start in the middle of each side and work outwards so tension can be evened out) (The Bulletin; FOX 2 Detroit).
- **play_and_precision**: not applicable: staples hold flexible material in place; they do not locate parts accurately.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): staple guns and hammer tackers have no anvil and drive into a solid base from the front (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'); hog rings need the pliers to reach round both sides of the wire or fabric (aftfasteners.com).
- **materials**: Staples: zinc-plated (galvanised) steel wire, with stainless steel and coated staples for damp or corrosive places (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8 on staple coatings); insulated cable staples have a plastic saddle (Wikimedia Commons 'U_Shape_Insulated_Staple_01.JPG'). Base material: softwood, plywood and similar; staple guns are sold for 'wood, plastic, or masonry' (Wikipedia 'Staple gun') but hand guns struggle in hardwood ⚑ [uncertain]. Holds: fabric and upholstery, leather and vinyl, insulation, house wrap, roofing felt, carpet and underlay, plastic film, insect screen, wire mesh and netting, card and thin board, and low-voltage cables (Wikipedia 'Staple gun', 'Hammer tacker'). Not for: 3D-printed plastic or brittle acrylic as the base (it cracks; see making tips), hard metals, or holding loads people rely on. *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: buy a hand staple gun and the matching staples; the skill is choosing leg length, keeping fabric taut and keeping fingers out of the way.

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: staples, staple guns and hammer tackers are bought
  - woodwork: fabric and mesh onto wooden frames, canvas onto stretcher bars, cabinet backs and crates
  - 3D print (FDM): printed cable clips and hangers with a thin tab that a staple is fired through (see make_it_yourself); printed parts are not a good base to staple into
  - laser cut: plywood frames and panels that fabric or mesh is stapled onto ⚑ [uncertain] *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **print_in_place**: no: staples are bought wire parts; only clips and frames that are stapled are printed.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Don't staple into printed plastic as if it were wood: there are no fibres to grip the legs and the blow can crack the part ⚑ [uncertain]. To fix fabric to a printed frame, glue or screw in a wooden strip to staple into, or clamp the fabric with a printed bar and screws instead ⚑ [uncertain]. 2) To fix a printed clip, hanger or cable holder to wood with a staple gun, give it a thin flat tab with a groove the width of the staple crown, so the staple passes through the plastic and both legs go into the wood; makers print such clips so the gun lines up every time (Printables 'Cable Clips for Makita Staple Gun', 'Staple-Mount Cable Hanger'). 3) Use a tough material for stapled parts: one maker reports that ABS works, nylon, HIPS and ASA should, but 'PLA and PETG probably will not, because they tend to shatter' when stapled (Printables 'Cable clip for mounting with a staple gun', TheParrotGuy). 4) Keep the staple well away from the edges of the printed tab so the legs don't split it out ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) A printed wire guide on the gun's nose holds a cable centred so the staple straddles it instead of piercing it (Printables 'Stanley Staple Gun Helper/Wire Holder'); never do this on mains cable (see safety_notes). 6) Printable staple removers and staple-gun holders are popular workshop aids (see make_it_yourself). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Hand staple gun (trigger tacker, forward-action or heavy-duty), long-nose staple gun for recessed corners, cable staple gun with wire guide, electric, cordless and pneumatic staplers, upholstery stapler, hammer tacker (Wikipedia 'Staple gun', 'Hammer tacker'); staples in the gun's own type and leg lengths (typically 6, 8, 10, 12 and 14 mm for staple guns: Wikipedia 'Staple gun'), narrow, medium and wide-crown staples, outward-clinch staples, stainless or galvanised staples, insulated cable staples and nail-in plastic cable clips, fence staples, hog rings with straight or angled hog ring pliers, staple and tack removers (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'; aftfasteners.com). Search supplier catalogues by staple type and the gun model rather than by size.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Staples behave like small nails: withdrawal of each leg varies almost directly with its circumference and depth, the nail equation p = 54.12 G^2.5 D L (N, mm) has been used for one leg, and each leg must be about two-thirds the diameter of a nail to give the same sideways load; neither has been verified by tests for staples (USDA Wood Handbook 2021, ch. 8, pp. 8-9 to 8-10).
  - Corrosion, sustained loads and durability matter as much as the first-day strength when judging a stapled joint (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-10); in moist, preservative-treated wood use stainless or suitably coated fasteners (USDA Wood Handbook 'Corrosion and Staining').
  - Fabric on a seat: start with a couple of staples in the middle of one side, then the opposite side pulled taut, then the other two sides, working out to the corners; staples about 1 in (25 mm) apart and about 1 in from the edge; about three staples per corner; 1/4 to 3/8 in (6-10 mm) staples (The Bulletin, Bend, 'DIY: Reupholster chairs', 2014).
  - Don't staple too close to the edge of a seat: the points can poke through the fabric and cut someone (FOX 2 Detroit upholstery feature).
  - Fence wire: 'loose staple every wire' so it can move under the staple and the strainer post, not the stapled post, takes the impact (Bekaert).
  - Cables: some staple guns have wire guides and some take rounded staples so the staple straddles a small cable without piercing it (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'); flat cables must not be stapled on edge (Baltimore City Code 334.30, an adoption of the US National Electrical Code).
  - Hammer tackers suit sheet materials: 'typically the shallower the staple, the better hold its grip on the surface' (Wikipedia 'Hammer tacker').

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - The fabric on the underside of a dining-chair seat (The Bulletin; FOX 2 Detroit)
  - Insulation, house wrap and carpet underlay fixed with a hammer tacker (Wikipedia 'Hammer tacker')
  - Phone, alarm or TV cable fixed along a skirting board with arched or insulated staples (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)', 'Staple gun')
  - Wire fencing stapled to wooden posts, and the big staples that close corrugated cardboard boxes (Bekaert; Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)')
- **when_to_use**: Use staples whenever a thin, flexible material must be fixed flat onto wood quickly: re-covering seats and cushions, stretching canvas or insect screen over a frame, fixing chicken wire or netting to a wooden frame, holding plastic sheet, insulation, roofing felt or carpet, closing crates and boxes, and running low-voltage cables (alarm, phone, speaker, LED strip leads) along woodwork. The crown spreads the hold over a wider area than a nail head, bridges two pieces butted together, and straddles a cable without piercing it (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'), and a staple gun works one-handed from the front (Wikipedia 'Staple gun').
- **when_not_to_use**: Not for carrying real loads or joining solid wood structurally: staples hold less than nails or screws (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'); use Wood screws, Nails or Metal brackets. Not where the part must come apart again and again: use screws, Hook-and-loop fastener or snaps. Not into 3D-printed plastic, acrylic or other brittle materials (use screws, inserts or glue). Not for mains cable with ordinary staples, and not with a tool its maker says is unsuitable for electrical cables (Toolstation PCT162 manual): mains cable is fixed only with fittings designed for that cable, following local wiring rules (Baltimore City Code 334.30; UK Approved Document P). For fabric that must be sewn to fabric, use stitching (see Load-bearing stitch patterns); for strap ends, use rivets or sewing.
- **environment_limits**: Plain steel staples rust outdoors and in damp places and can stain wood; use galvanised or stainless staples there (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'; USDA Wood Handbook 'Corrosion and Staining'). Wood that goes through wetting and drying cycles loses much of its grip on driven fasteners: nail withdrawal can fall to 25% of its first value (USDA Wood Handbook, for nails, which staples resemble). Sunlight degrades the fabric and plastic held by the staple more than the staple itself ⚑ [uncertain]. Fence wire expands and contracts with temperature, so it is loose-stapled (Bekaert). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **safety_notes**: Staple guns and powered staplers fire like nail guns: always assume the tool is loaded, never point it at yourself or anyone nearby, only fire with the nose pressed firmly on the work (otherwise 'the fastener may be deflected away from your target'), and always wear eye protection (Toolstation PCT162 tacker and power-tool safety warnings); keep your other hand clear of the nose and of the back of thin material ⚑ [uncertain]. Unplug or disconnect a powered stapler before clearing a jam, and take care when clearing any jam because 'the fastener may be forcefully discharged' (Toolstation PCT162). Never fix mains cable with plain staples: a staple can pierce or crush the insulation and cause 'electric shock and fire hazards'; use only fasteners designed for electrical cable installations, make sure cables are not live, and check the insulation afterwards (Toolstation PCT162). Mains wiring work must follow local law (e.g. US electrical codes as adopted locally, such as Baltimore City Code 334.30; Building Regulations Part P in England), so this entry gives no wiring steps. Staple points that stick through fabric near an edge can cut people (FOX 2 Detroit); hog ring and staple points are sharp (aftfasteners.com). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **common_failures**: Staple stands proud of the work: legs too long for a hard base, too little force, or the gun not pressed down; use shorter staples, press the nose firmly, increase the hammer force on a powered stapler, or tap it down ⚑ [uncertain]. Legs fold over or the staple jams: hardwood, a knot, or the wrong staple size for the gun; use the gun's own staple type and clear jams safely (Toolstation PCT162 troubleshooting: 'wrong size fasteners', 'bent fasteners'). Fabric pulls out round the staples: staples too close to the edge, too far apart, or single-layer thin fabric; fold the edge over, staple closer together and further from the edge (The Bulletin). Wood splits: staples near an edge or in thin brittle wood ⚑ [uncertain]. Cable pierced or crushed: staple not centred or driven too hard; use a wire guide or insulated cable staples (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'). Rust streaks outdoors (USDA Wood Handbook). Wrinkled or uneven fabric: stapling round from one corner instead of from the middle of each side outwards (The Bulletin). *(⚑ parts need confirming)*
- **related**: 
  - Nails, brads and tacks: one leg instead of two; upholstery tacks and brads do the same jobs more slowly
  - Wood screw: much stronger and removable, for structural wood joints
  - Hook-and-loop fastener: when fabric must come off and go back on
  - Leather and textile rivets (double-cap, tubular and split rivets): permanent joins in straps and leather, through both layers
  - Load-bearing stitch patterns for straps and webbing: sewing fabric to fabric where staples won't do
  - Cable gland and strain relief: holds a cable where it enters a box, rather than along a surface
  - Linear ratchet (cable-tie ratchet): cable ties and cable-tie mounts as an alternative way to hold cables
  - Push-in plastic rivet (fir-tree clip): pushes into a hole to hold trim panels and fabric-covered boards
  - Hog ring (C-ring): a squeezed wire ring for upholstery, car seats and mesh (a variant in this entry, not a separate one)
