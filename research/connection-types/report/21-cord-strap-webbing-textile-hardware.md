## 121 Cam cleat

*cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware.* Two spring-loaded toothed cams that let a rope pull through one way and grip it the other way; lift the rope up and out to release

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A rope holder with two springy toothed jaws side by side: pull the rope back between them and it slides through, let go and the teeth bite and hold it, then flick the rope up and out to free it
- **term**: Cam cleat
- **aliases**: 
  - spring-loaded cam cleat
  - ball-bearing cam cleat (the bearing type used by Harken and others)
  - swivel cam cleat (a cam cleat on a turning base)
  - Curry clamp / Curryklemme (German name, after the sailor Manfred Curry)
  - bulldog jam cleat (early US name, according to German Wikipedia)
  - jam cleat (loose usage; the name is also used for clam cleats)
  - Cam-Matic and Carbo-Cam (Harken brands)
  - C-Cleat and T-Cleat (Ronstan brands)
- **family**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware
- **other_families**: one-way & ratchet, self-locking & anti-back-drive, quick-release & tool-free, tensioning, reels & take-up
- **one_liner**: A pair of spring-loaded toothed cams on a small base that let a rope be pulled through in one direction, grip it harder the more the load pulls back, and let it go when the rope is lifted up and out of the jaws.
- **complexity**: A small assembly that acts as one unit: a base plate with two pivot posts; two toothed cams (jaws, which Harken calls pawls); one or two torsion springs per cam; and, on better cleats, ball bearings (Harken's standard 150 uses 66 small Delrin balls in three rows), washers and cam caps. Often fitted with add-ons: a fairlead (guide loop) over the jaws, a riser or a 15° wedge. Some designs gear the two cams together so they open and close as a mirrored pair (US 4,620,499).
- **how_it_works**: Two cams sit side by side on pivots, and springs turn them so their toothed, curved faces meet in the middle. The curve is off-centre (eccentric): the teeth get further from the pivot along the cam, so turning the cams one way widens the gap and turning them the other way narrows it (US 4,620,499). Pulling the rope toward you drags the teeth the opening way and the rope slides through; when the load pulls back, the rope drags the teeth the closing way and wedges itself in, so 'as the load on the line increases, the cams' pressure and grip increase as well' (SAIL Magazine). German Wikipedia notes the jaws usually follow a logarithmic spiral so the rope is gripped at the same angle whatever its diameter. To free it you tension the tail and lift it up and out of the jaws (Upffront, Wikipedia).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - stop it moving back (one-way): the rope pulls through toward you and is held against the load pulling the other way
  - lock, and release by hand: lift the rope up and out of the jaws and it runs free
  - tighten and hold tension: pull the tail to tighten a line and it holds wherever you stop
- **motion**: slide: the rope runs straight through the jaws in one direction only, with no fixed steps (stepless); the cams themselves only rock a little on their pivots
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1 for the rope: it moves only along its length, and only in the tightening direction while it sits in the jaws. Each cam also turns about its own pivot (1 each, or 1 shared when the cams are geared together). Lifting the rope out of the top is the release path, not a working motion.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - one-way (moves freely one way, locks the other): the rope pulls through toward you and locks against the load
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): the load drags the cams tighter, so the harder it pulls the harder they grip
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): you lift the rope up and out; lever-release and pull-down-release versions also exist (Nash cam cleat, US 3,765,061, described in US 4,620,499; Nash Trigger Cleat in Practical Sailor)
  - quick-release: one upward flick frees the line, which is also why a line knocked upward by accident can come loose
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): pull the tail to tension a line and it holds at any point
- **holding_principle**: 
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the toothed jaws pinch the rope, and the load wedges them tighter
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): torsion springs keep the cams closed so they catch the rope as soon as you let go
- **input_output**: not applicable: it holds a rope rather than passing motion on (back-drivable: no while the rope stays down in the jaws; lifting the rope out releases it)
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: lift the rope up and out of the jaws; the cleat itself is screwed or bolted down and comes off with a screwdriver
- **reuse_cycles**: effectively unlimited: it grips and releases thousands of times (Practical Sailor ran 1,000-cycle cleat-and-release tests). What wears is the rope, the teeth, the springs and the bearings; Harken sells springs, balls, washers and base plates as spares.
- **tools_needed**: none to use; a drill and screwdriver (plus a spanner for through-bolts) to mount it. Harken's standard 150 takes #10 (5 mm) flat-head fasteners; its micro cleats take #8 (4 mm) round-head screws (Harken Quick Service sheet).
- **seal**: none: nothing makes it seal
- **load**: Light to medium, and only as a pull along the rope. Makers' maximum working loads: Harken Micro Cam-Matic 91 kg (200 lb); Harken Standard Cam-Matic 150 136 kg (300 lb), breaking load 340 kg (750 lb); Ronstan Small C-Cleat 165 lb (breaking 330 lb); Ronstan Large C-Cleat 510 lb (breaking 1,010 lb). Weak against any upward pull on the rope, which releases it, and against leads from the side or from above, which make the rope pop out (Harken). Lewmar's catalogue warns that cam cleats are 'designed to hold only the load that can be applied manually' (quoted by Practical Sailor).
- **adjustable**: yes: stepless; pull more rope through to tighten, or tension the tail, lift it and let some run to ease it; it holds at any point along the rope
- **play_and_precision**: There are no fixed positions (stepless). Each cleat takes a range of rope sizes (for example 3 to 12 mm for Harken's standard 150), but it does not tolerate a crooked lead: the line must come in straight, or it pops out of the cams (Harken Quick Service). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): the rope is loaded and released from the top. Mounting needs the back only if you through-bolt it; screws into wood or a thick deck go in from the front.
- **materials**: Cams: hard-anodised aluminium, glass-fibre-reinforced or carbon-fibre composite, stainless steel or bronze. Bases: glass-fibre-reinforced polymer, aluminium or stainless steel. Springs: stainless steel. Bearings: Delrin (acetal) balls, or PTFE-impregnated acetal slotted bearings (Harken, Ronstan, Allen Brothers, Practical Sailor). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate: printed cams and a printed base work for light lines, but you need metal pivot screws and springs and a sensible tooth shape; for anything carrying real load, buy one

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: by far the usual route (Harken, Ronstan, Holt/Allen, Barton, Spinlock and others)
  - 3D print (FDM): cams, base and fairlead, assembled with metal torsion springs and screw pivots
  - CNC: aluminium cams (Helms's patent cut the cam jaws from extruded aluminium bar, US 3,730,129)
  - woodwork: mounting pads, risers and wedges to set the angle *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **print_in_place**: no: working versions are printed as separate cams and base and assembled with metal springs and screw pivots (Erik Stensmo's Printables cam cleat uses four metal springs). A print-in-place version with printed springs exists, but its maker says it grips only 'sometimes'.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Print each cam lying flat, with its pivot hole vertical, so the teeth are drawn by the perimeters in one layer plane and the rope's pull runs along the layers rather than trying to split them (FFF prints are stronger in one direction than another, Prusa). Use a metal screw, shoulder bolt or pin as the pivot rather than a printed peg, and metal torsion springs: Erik Stensmo's CC BY Printables cam cleat needs only the springs as metal parts and recommends two springs per cam (four per cleat), with the bearing ideally printed in PTFE. Leave the cams free to turn: Harken warns that over-tightening the mounting screws makes the cams (pawls) bind, so let the screw clamp a spacer or post, not the cam itself. Keep teeth slightly rounded rather than knife-sharp: Helms's patent found tips rounded to about 0.25 mm best (0.13 to 0.76 mm acceptable), which a 0.4 mm nozzle gives naturally, and Practical Sailor reports that too-sharp teeth chew rope. Add a fairlead or guide over the jaws so the rope enters straight, and a riser or wedge if the line comes from above the cleat's plane (Harken). Material: PETG, ASA or PC outdoors; PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in sunlight (Prusa PLA page), PETG suits outdoor parts below about 80 °C, and ASA resists UV and heat up to 93 °C (Prusa). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Cam cleats in micro, standard and large sizes (search 'cam cleat', 'Cam-Matic', 'Carbo-Cam', 'C-Cleat', 'T-Cleat'); cam cleat fairleads (wire, bullseye or 'X-Treme angle' fairleads), 15° wedge kits, flat and 15° angled risers, cam caps and adapter plates (Harken Quick Service list); replacement torsion springs and Delrin ball kits (e.g. Harken 150KIT); swivel cleat bases (Ronstan); 316 stainless flat-head machine screws with nyloc nuts or washers. For DIY builds: small stainless torsion springs and stainless shoulder bolts or pins for the pivots.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Match the rope to the cleat's diameter range and the load to its maximum working load: Harken Micro Cam-Matic 2-6 mm, 91 kg (200 lb); Harken Standard 150 3-12 mm, 136 kg (300 lb), breaking load 340 kg (Harken product pages); Ronstan Small C-Cleat 3/32-5/16 in, 165 lb; Large C-Cleat 1/4-5/8 in, 510 lb (Ronstan product pages).
  - Makers set the maximum working load at roughly 40-50% of the breaking load: Harken 150 136 of 340 kg; Ronstan C-Cleats 165 of 330 lb and 510 of 1,010 lb (Harken and Ronstan product pages).
  - Use it only for loads a person can hold by hand: Lewmar's catalogue warns cam cleats are 'designed to hold only the load that can be applied manually' (quoted by Practical Sailor, 'The Civilization of Cam Cleats'). For higher, longer-held loads such as halyards, use a clutch or jammer (Upffront).
  - Lead the line straight into the cams: 'Line pops out of cams: Lead is not fair. Use risers or wedges so line enters straight into the cam' (Harken Quick Service sheet); Harken's wedges and angled risers are 15°.
  - When a cleat is mounted upside down, 'add a second spring to each cam alongside the original', and 'do not over-tighten mounting screws; pawls will bind' (Harken Quick Service sheet).
  - Tooth shape from Helms's extruded cam cleat patent (US 3,730,129): tooth tips rounded to about 10 mils (0.25 mm), with 5-30 mils (0.13-0.76 mm) satisfactory; the rear face of each tooth at about 90° (85-95°) to the cam arc; and the cam arc's centre set back about one-third of its radius and out about one-sixth, giving a fairly flat arc so 'at least four teeth of each jaw' grip the line.
  - Strength is rarely the limit; release is. In Practical Sailor's 1992 test of 16 cleats the best 'easily withstood a 1,000-lb linear pull, without slipping', but freeing a heavily loaded line is hard and risky (Practical Sailor).
  - Blunter teeth are kinder to rope: early cam cleats' 'too-sharp cam teeth chewed voraciously on any line', and makers moved to 'blunter, more line-lenient teeth' (Practical Sailor).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Sailing dinghies: the mainsheet and small control lines (cunningham, outhaul, vang) run through cam cleats (SAIL Magazine, Practical Sailor)
  - Hunting climbing sticks: a Printables adapter holds a Harken 150 cam cleat on a climbing stick used for saddle hunting *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **when_to_use**: When a light to moderately loaded rope needs frequent, quick adjustment and fast release, with one hand and no knot: sheets and control lines on small boats, tarp and awning lines, hauling or hoisting lines where the load is held by hand, and lines on kayaks and camping gear. It is faster and easier than a knot on a horn cleat, especially for beginners (Practical Sailor), and holds at any point along the rope.
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid it for loads beyond what a person can hold by hand, or loads that must be released under high tension: use a rope clutch or jammer, a winch, or a horn cleat with a cleat hitch (Upffront; Practical Sailor). Avoid it anywhere a foot, elbow or flapping line could knock the rope upward and release it, such as critical lines or anything over people (SAIL Magazine; Practical Sailor): use a horn cleat and a proper hitch. Avoid it for flat webbing (use a cam buckle) and for ropes outside its diameter range (they slip or won't seat). Where you want no springs or moving parts, a clam cleat is simpler but harder to release under load (Wikipedia).
- **environment_limits**: Salt, sand and dirt make the cams stiff: flush with fresh water and clean with mild detergent at regular intervals (Harken Quick Service). Aluminium cleats are highly resistant to UV (Upffront); carbon-composite cams resist heat and abrasion, and slotted bearings resist sand and salt better than ball bearings, which 'may deform under high load' (Ronstan). Springs corrode, tire or break over time and the cams then stop snapping shut (Harken). For printed cleats: PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV; PETG is fine outdoors below about 80 °C; ASA is UV-resistant up to 93 °C (Prusa material pages). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Fingers can be caught 'when snapping a taut line out of a cam cleat, especially one that has an integral fairlead' (Practical Sailor). A heavily loaded line may be impossible to lift out, so keep a knife close at hand and a mechanical way (winch or purchase) to take the load off (Practical Sailor). A line accidentally knocked upward 'will scuttle off along the deck' (SAIL Magazine), so never rely on a cam cleat alone for anything that could fall on people or for a person's weight. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: Line pops out of the cams: the lead is not fair; add a riser, wedge or fairlead so the line enters straight (Harken). Cams do not snap shut: springs damaged or missing; replace them (Harken). Line drops out of an upside-down cleat: fit a second spring per cam (Harken). Stiff or uneven cam motion: dirt or salt, or missing bearing balls; flush with fresh water and refit the right number of balls (Harken). Cams bind after fitting: mounting screws over-tightened (Harken). Rope slips: rope too thin, worn, slippery or outside the rated range, or teeth worn smooth; use the right cleat size and replace worn parts. Rope fuzzes or wears: sharp teeth; choose line-friendly cleats (Practical Sailor's abrasion tests rated Harken Carbo-Cam, Ronstan C-Cleat, Spinlock PX and RWO Carbocleat best).
- **related**: 
  - Clam cleat (grooved V-cleat): one solid piece with no springs, more compact and cheaper, but the rope is less easily released under load (Wikipedia); in Practical Sailor's 1997 abrasion test Clamcleats and Ronstan V-Cleats wore rope less than cam cleats
  - Horn cleat with a cleat hitch: slower to tie and untie, but holds higher loads and can't be knocked loose by accident
  - Rope clutch or jammer: a lever-operated cam that holds higher loads for longer, such as halyards, and releases under load (Upffront)
  - Rope grab and ascender (cam rope clamp): a single toothed cam presses the rope against a fixed wall, for climbing and hauling
  - Cord lock (spring toggle): a spring plunger that pinches a thin cord, for very light loads
  - Cam buckle (webbing cam buckle): the same one-way toothed-cam idea for flat straps, released by pressing a lever
  - Binding-plate lock (tilting friction plate): a tipped plate wedges on a rod instead of cams wedging a rope
  - Sprag clutch: the same wedge-tighter-under-load principle, used between rotating shafts

## 122 Clam cleat (grooved V-cleat)

*cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware.* A block with ridged V-shaped grooves that jams a rope pulled into it and releases when the rope is lifted out; no moving parts

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A small block with a ridged V-shaped slot: press a pulled-tight rope down into the slot and the load drags it deeper until it jams, then lift the rope up and back to let it go; nothing on it moves
- **term**: Clam cleat (grooved V-cleat; jamming cleat)
- **aliases**: 
  - Clamcleat (brand; Clamcleats Ltd, England)
  - jam cleat / jamming cleat (Wikipedia and patent term; 'jam cleat' is also used for cam cleats and tapered horn-type cleats)
  - V-cleat (Ronstan's V-Cleat range)
  - grooved cleat
  - rope cleat
  - Kammklemme (German)
  - Loop cleat and Line-Lok (Clamcleat brands: versions for tensioning guy lines; Line-Lok seen only in search results)
  - fender cleat (in-line version for fender lines)
  - Jiffy Tie (Clamcleat brand: a small tie for light loads)
- **family**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware
- **other_families**: one-way & ratchet, self-locking & anti-back-drive, quick-release & tool-free, tensioning, reels & take-up
- **one_liner**: A one-piece block with a narrow V-shaped groove lined with slanted ridges: a tensioned rope pressed into the groove is pulled deeper by its own load and jams, and a short upward-and-backward pull lifts it free.
- **complexity**: single piece: one moulded or cast block (two ridged walls joined by a base), fixed with two screws or bolts. Some versions add a built-in fairlead (guide loop) that steers the rope into the teeth, a becket (a loop to tie a rope end to), a cage or keeper that holds the rope near the cleat, or a tapered pad to set the angle.
- **how_it_works**: The groove narrows toward the bottom, and both walls carry ridges that slope toward the end the load pulls from (45° in Reginald Emery's original design, US 3,574,900). When a tensioned rope is laid in and the load pulls it along the groove, the sloping ridges drag it down into the narrowing V, so 'the stronger the pull, the more the rope is forced into the V-shaped slot and hence the firmer is the grip' (US 3,574,900). Nothing moves except the rope, so there are no springs to break or seize (Ronstan). To release, pull the tail 'upward and backward' so the rope rides up the ridges and out of the mouth of the groove (Ronstan; US 3,574,900).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - stop it moving back (one-way): the rope can be pulled through toward you but is held against the load
  - lock, and release by hand: a short upward and backward pull frees the rope
  - tighten and hold tension: tension the rope, drop it into the groove and it holds at that length
- **motion**: slide: the rope slides along and down into the groove, stepless; the cleat itself has no moving parts
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 inside the cleat, because it is one solid piece. The rope has 1 (along its length), and while it is jammed it can move only in the tightening direction.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - one-way (moves freely one way, locks the other): the rope can be pulled forward through the groove but jams when the load pulls back
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): the sloping ridges pull the rope deeper the harder the load pulls, so grip grows with load
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): a short upward and backward pull lifts the rope out; it is 'less easily released under load' than a cam cleat (Wikipedia)
  - quick-release: no knot to untie, so the line comes free in one move
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): used on guy lines, awnings and sunshades to keep a line tight
- **holding_principle**: friction or clamping (force-fit): the rope is wedged between the ridged walls of a narrowing V, and the load pulls it deeper
- **input_output**: not applicable: it holds a rope rather than passing motion on (back-drivable: no while the rope stays in the groove; lifting it up and back releases it)
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: lift the rope up and back out of the groove; the cleat itself is screwed or bolted on and comes off with a screwdriver
- **reuse_cycles**: effectively unlimited: there are no moving parts; only the ridges and the rope wear. Nylon cleats used for light loads on control lines are often over 10 years old, but 'tensioning a rope by dragging it back through the teeth will quickly burn out a nylon cleat' (Clamcleat FAQ text reproduced by Mastrant).
- **tools_needed**: none to use; a drill and screwdriver (or spanner for bolts) to fit it ('Fix with bolts or screws', Premium Ropes listing for the Clamcleat CL231)
- **seal**: none: nothing makes it seal
- **load**: Light to medium, as a pull along the rope only. Example: the Clamcleat CL231 nylon cleat with fairlead, for 4-8 mm rope, is listed with a breaking strength of 670 kg (Premium Ropes). Grip depends on seating the rope fully: Emery found that a rope held only by the front ridges can take as much as 50% less load than one gripped by all the ridges (US 5,675,872). Weak against any upward pull, which is how it is released. Ronstan recommends cam cleats instead 'for applications where constant line draw-through or high loads are expected'.
- **adjustable**: yes: stepless; lift the rope out, adjust its length and press it back in. Tension the rope first rather than dragging it back through the teeth, which wears the ridges (Mastrant, reproducing Clamcleat's FAQ).
- **play_and_precision**: Stepless. Each cleat suits a narrow band of rope sizes (for example 3-6 mm or 4-8 mm); ropes toward the middle of the stated range work best, and the listed extremes are just that (Mastrant), and in Emery's design the rope's diameter should run from 'a little larger than, down to a little smaller than' the width of the slot mouth (US 3,574,900). It dislikes a crooked lead unless it has a fairlead: the CL231's integral fairlead guides the rope into the teeth 'from up to 90 degrees' (Premium Ropes). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): the rope is loaded and released from the top. Mounting needs the back only if you through-bolt it.
- **materials**: Marine-grade nylon, aluminium and hard-anodised aluminium (the Clamcleat range, as described by Ronstan); PTFE-impregnated glass- and carbon-fibre-reinforced composite (Ronstan V-Cleats). Nylon: corrosion-proof and lasts for years outdoors, for most light-load uses; aluminium: for high loads or surges, unaffected by the heat of a rope surging back through the cleat ('rope burn') and suitable for sub-zero temperatures; hard-anodised aluminium: best protection against corrosion and against wear from 'aggressive modern fibres' (Ronstan). Emery's patent names injection-moulded nylon (US 3,574,900). It works on 'deformable' rope that can be wedged into the groove (US 3,574,900), so soft braided rope suits it better than stiff wire.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: a single printed block and two screws make a working cleat, but printed ridges wear faster and are weaker than injection-moulded nylon, so keep printed ones to light loads

### Making it

- **processes**: off-the-shelf part: the usual route (Clamcleat, Ronstan V-Cleats and others), 3D print (FDM): one solid piece with no assembly, CNC: machined aluminium versions *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **print_in_place**: yes: it is one solid piece with no moving parts, so it comes off the printer complete and working; only two screws are added to mount it
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Orientation matters because the rope's pull tries to split the ridges from the walls. The makers of printed clam cleats on Printables disagree on orientation: Patrice Peccard stood his polycarbonate clam cleat upright with a large brim (16 loops, 2 layers) at 40% infill and also printed it in PETG; JMAW prints his 'Marine Lines Clamp Cleat' at 45° 'for ultimate strength', with 2 mm walls, 25% honeycomb infill and light-coloured PETG, fixed with M5 countersunk screws for 5 mm line (scale it to 80% with M4 screws for 4 mm paracord). Whichever way you print, use thick walls round the groove, since FFF prints are stronger in one direction than another (Prusa). Copy the proven geometry from Emery's patent (US 3,574,900): walls 8-20° apart (14° best), ridges sloping 20-50° toward the loaded end (45° best), ridge tips about 90° with a chamfered lead-in at the mouth, and a groove mouth about as wide as the rope. Make the rearmost ridges reach deep enough that the rope is gripped by all of them (US 5,675,872). Material: PETG, ASA or PC outdoors; PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in sunlight (Prusa PLA page), PETG suits outdoor parts below about 80 °C, and ASA resists UV and heat up to 93 °C (Prusa). McSail's printed fender cleat has held boat fenders for two seasons in PETG, but he plans ASA once UV damage shows and warns a printed part 'will not be equivalent in strength' to an injection-moulded nylon one. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Clamcleat (brand) cleats by rope size, e.g. CL274 Micro 1-4 mm, CL204 Mini 3-6 mm, CL209 Midi 4-8 mm, CL205 Major 6-12 mm, CL201 Vertical 10-16 mm (Toplicht); aluminium and hard-anodised aluminium versions for higher loads (Ronstan); versions with an integral fairlead (e.g. Ronstan V-Cleats for 3-6, 5-8 and 8-12 mm line; stainless countersunk screws or bolts. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Groove angle: the two walls should be 'inclined relative to each other at an angle of between 8 and 20°'; 'the angle of 14° is the optimum' (Emery, US 3,574,900).
  - Ridge slope: ridges sloping toward the loaded end at 20-50° to the base, '45° having been found to be generally desirable' (US 3,574,900); Emery's later racquet cleat also uses 45° ridges (US 5,675,872).
  - Ridge shape: ridge apex angle 40-100°, '90° having been found to be generally desirable', with the wall tops chamfered to give a lead-in to the slot (US 3,574,900).
  - Rope size: the rope's diameter may run from 'a little larger than, down to a little smaller than, the width of the slot at its mouth' (US 3,574,900); choose rope toward the middle of a cleat's stated range, because the listed extremes are just that (Mastrant).
  - Seat the rope on all the ridges: if the rearmost ridges don't grip, 'the loading the string can withstand can be as much as fifty per cent less than when all of the ridges grip' (US 5,675,872). The same patent angles the ridge crests so they close in by about 2.5° (range 0.5-8°) from front to rear to improve grip.
  - Nylon cleats suit light loads; where 'high loads or surges are encountered aluminium cleats are recommended' (Ronstan).
  - Don't tension by dragging the rope back through the teeth: it 'will quickly burn out a nylon cleat'; alloy cleats cope, as long as the rope is clean (Clamcleat FAQ text reproduced by Mastrant).
  - Where the rope can't be led straight in, use a cleat with an integral fairlead: the Clamcleat CL231 guides rope into the teeth 'from up to 90 degrees' (Premium Ropes).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Sailing dinghies and yachts: small control lines and halyards held in black nylon or aluminium clam cleats (Ronstan; Wikimedia Commons photo)
  - Sunshades, awnings, tents and antenna masts: guy lines tensioned with clam-type cleats ('one use is for tensioning sunshades', Premium Ropes; Mastrant, which makes guying rope for antenna masts, sells Clamcleat tensioners)
  - Boat fender lines held in an in-line fender cleat (McSail's Printables fender cleat)
  - Light-pull cords: the first Clamcleat grew out of a 1963 idea for attaching a knob to a light pull-cord (Rope44)
- **when_to_use**: When a light to moderately loaded rope needs holding and quick adjustment and you want the simplest, cheapest, most compact option with no springs or moving parts to break, seize or corrode: small-boat control lines, guy lines for tents, awnings and antennas, blind and light cords, tarps, fender lines and kit on kayaks. Clamcleat's makers describe grip 'proportional to the load, without the need for complex moving parts' (Rope44). It is easy to print at home in one piece.
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid it for heavy loads, lines that must be released while heavily loaded, or lines constantly pulled back and forth through the cleat: use a cam cleat, whose opening action releases more easily (Ronstan; Wikipedia), or a rope clutch or winch. Avoid it where the rope might be knocked upward and released, or for anything carrying a person or hanging over people: tie off on a horn cleat with a cleat hitch or use a rated device. Avoid it for flat webbing (use a cam buckle or ladder-lock buckle) and for ropes much thinner or thicker than the cleat's range (they slip or won't seat). Avoid dirty, sandy ropes on nylon cleats, which wear the ridges quickly (Mastrant).
- **environment_limits**: Nylon cleats are 'completely resistant to salt water corrosion' and to many acids and chemicals; aluminium has 'excellent' corrosion resistance, and hard-anodised aluminium slightly better corrosion and wear resistance than plain (Clamcleat FAQ text reproduced by Mastrant). Aluminium cleats are unaffected by the heat of a rope surging back through them and work below freezing (Ronstan). A sandy rope 'will wear out any fitting' (Mastrant). Clamcleat makes some items from UV-resistant material for outdoor use (Jiffy Tie, Mastrant). For printed cleats: PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV; PETG is fine outdoors below about 80 °C; ASA is UV-resistant up to 93 °C (Prusa material pages).
- **safety_notes**: A rope knocked upward can pop out, so don't use it for life-safety or overhead loads. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: Slipping: rope too thin for the groove, worn or glazed, or not pressed down far enough to reach all the ridges (up to 50% less holding, US 5,675,872); fix by using the right cleat size and seating the rope fully. Ridges burnt or worn smooth: rope dragged back through the teeth under load, or sandy rope (Mastrant); use aluminium or hard-anodised cleats for this kind of use. Rope pops out or won't enter: poor lead angle; add a fairlead, cage or tapered pad, or re-site the cleat. Can't release: too much load for the cleat; take the load off first. Rope fuzz and wear: sharp or damaged ridges.
- **related**: 
  - Cam cleat: two spring-loaded toothed cams instead of fixed ridges; easier to release under load and better for constant pulling-through, but has springs and moving parts (Wikipedia; Ronstan)
  - Horn cleat with a cleat hitch: slower, but holds bigger loads and can't be knocked loose
  - Tapered jam cleat (horn type): the rope is wedged under a tapering horn rather than in a ridged groove (Wikipedia gallery)
  - Guy-line slider (friction line tensioner): a plate with holes that holds a tent line by friction, even simpler and lighter
  - Cord lock (spring toggle): a spring plunger for thin cords
  - Rope clutch or jammer: a lever-operated cam for high, long-held loads
  - Ladder-lock buckle: the flat-webbing equivalent that holds by friction when loaded and slides when tilted

## 123 Cam buckle (webbing cam buckle)

*cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware.* A buckle with a toothed, spring-loaded cam that grips a strap after you pull it tight; press the lever to release

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A buckle on a strap with a springy toothed flap inside: pull the loose end and the strap slides through tight, let go and the teeth pinch it so it can't slip back, then press the flap to loosen it
- **term**: Cam buckle (webbing cam buckle; cam strap)
- **aliases**: 
  - cam lock buckle
  - cam buckle strap / cam strap
  - cam buckle tie-down
  - spring-loaded cam buckle
  - lashing strap with cam buckle
  - roller cam buckle (version with a roller under the webbing)
  - Klemmschloss / Spanngurt mit Klemmverschluss (German)
  - NRS strap (brand)
  - Rollercam (brand)
  - cam band (scuba tank strap; usually an over-centre lever buckle, a different mechanism)
- **family**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware
- **other_families**: one-way & ratchet, self-locking & anti-back-drive, quick-release & tool-free, tensioning, reels & take-up
- **one_liner**: A strap buckle in which a spring presses a toothed cam against the webbing, so the strap pulls through one way to tighten, is gripped harder the more the load pulls back, and slides free when you press the cam's lever.
- **complexity**: A few parts: a U-shaped frame (die-cast zinc, pressed steel, stainless steel or plastic) with a bar or plate the webbing presses against; a toothed (serrated or knurled) cam lever on a pivot pin; a coil or torsion spring; sometimes a separate serrated pressure plate (US 3,678,542) or a roller (Rollercam; US 6,877,189); plus the webbing, usually sewn to the buckle at one end.
- **how_it_works**: The webbing runs through the frame between a fixed bar or plate and a toothed cam on a pivot pin. A spring pushes the cam toward the bar so its teeth press on the webbing (US 3,678,542; German Wikipedia 'Spanngurt'). Pulling the loose tail drags the teeth the opening way, so the webbing slides through and tightens; when you let go, the load pulls the webbing the other way, which pulls the cam face closer to the bar, so 'a belt cannot be pulled out of the buckle unless [the] lever ... is first depressed' (US 3,678,542). Pressing the lever against its spring lifts the teeth and the strap slides freely either way. Because you tighten it by hand, it can't be over-tensioned the way a ratchet can (DC Cargo), and friction in a plain cam buckle means the strap tension can be 'two to three times less' than your pull (US 6,877,189).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: pull the tail to cinch a strap round a load and it stays tight
  - stop it moving back (one-way): the webbing slides through only in the tightening direction
  - lock, and release by hand: press the lever and the strap slides free
  - join and hold: ties a load, a bundle or a boat to a rack, frame or trailer
- **motion**: slide: the webbing pulls through in one direction, with no fixed steps (stepless); the cam itself only rocks a little on its pin
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1 for the webbing: it moves only along its length, and only in the tightening direction unless the lever is pressed. The cam turns about one pin (1).
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - one-way (moves freely one way, locks the other): the strap slides through to tighten and locks against the load
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): the load pulls the cam tighter onto the webbing
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): press the thumb lever against its spring to free the strap
  - quick-release: one press and the strap can be pulled out completely
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): pulling the tail takes up slack and holds the strap at any length; the tension is limited to what you can pull by hand
- **holding_principle**: 
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the toothed cam pinches the webbing against a bar or plate, and the load pulls it tighter
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): a small spring keeps the cam pressed on the webbing so it catches as soon as you let go
- **input_output**: not applicable: it holds a strap rather than passing motion on (back-drivable: no while the cam is engaged; pressing the lever releases it)
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: press the lever and pull the strap out
- **tools_needed**: none to use. To make your own strap: a sewing machine or heavy needle to sew the webbing to the buckle's bar, or a bolt-on buckle (a printable repair buckle uses an M3 screw, Printables 817333). NRS sells its buckles separately for custom straps but 'cannot guarantee the strength of custom straps'.
- **seal**: none: nothing makes it seal
- **load**: Light to medium, as a straight pull along the strap. NRS 1-inch heavy-duty strap: minimum breaking strength 1,500 lb, working load limit 500 lb; 1.5-inch strap: 2,000 lb breaking load, 650 lb working load limit (NRS; Rafting Magazine). The NRS 1-inch buckle alone has 'over 2,000 lb. load capacity', so the webbing is usually the weak link. The tension you can put in is limited to a hand pull (DC Cargo) and reduced by buckle friction (US 6,877,189), so for heavy cargo ratchet straps are used instead (Rollercam). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **adjustable**: yes: stepless; pull the tail to tighten, or press the lever and let some strap slide out to loosen
- **play_and_precision**: Stepless, so it holds at any length. The webbing must match the slot width and suit the gap: Milspec Monkey notes its 1.5-inch slot 'works better with thicker textured webbing'. Polypropylene webbing has 'quite a bit of elasticity and creep under loads' (Rafting Magazine), so straps can loosen slightly over time. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **access_needed**: not applicable: the strap wraps around the load or through a rack, and you only need to reach the buckle to tighten or release it
- **materials**: Frames and cams: die-cast zinc (NRS), pressed steel, stainless steel, aluminium, or moulded plastic for light straps; springs and pivot pins: stainless steel (NRS). Webbing: polypropylene (NRS: floats, UV-treated, doesn't stretch when wet), polyester (most load-securing straps) or polyamide (nylon). DIN EN 12195-2, as summarised by German Wikipedia, gives temperature ranges of -40 to 120 °C for polyester, -40 to 100 °C for polyamide and -40 to 80 °C for polypropylene webbing.
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate: fitting a bought buckle is easy (sew or bolt it to webbing), but making one means printing a frame and toothed cam and adding a metal pin and spring, and a printed buckle only suits light straps and should be tested before you trust it

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: buckles sold on their own to sew onto your own webbing, or complete cam straps
  - sheet metal: pressed-steel frames and cams on many cheap buckles (German Wikipedia: cast metal or formed steel sheet)
  - 3D print (FDM): frame and cam for light straps, with a metal pin and spring *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Printed cam buckles exist for light loads: Radulf3d's Printables 'Cam buckle' for straps up to 25 mm wide is printed in PETG and meant for 'light loads, bags, gear'; Scott Lomas's 'cam lock buckle repair' bolts onto a strap with an M3 screw, is made for 20 mm (3/4 in) straps and should be printed 'in the orientations provided for maximum strength'; Robin Lunde's PETG 'Camera buckle' (for about 27 mm straps) holds a dog harness, but its small rear cross-bar broke off. Material: PETG or ASA rather than PLA, because PLA softens above about 60 °C (a car roof in summer sun can get hot) and degrades in UV (Prusa PLA page); PETG suits outdoor parts below about 80 °C and ASA resists UV and heat up to 93 °C (Prusa). Test a printed buckle at the load you need before relying on it, and never use one for anything that carries a person or could fall on someone. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Cam buckles sold loose by webbing width (e.g. 1 in and 1.5 in; search 'cam buckle', 'cam lock buckle', 'spring loaded cam buckle'); die-cast zinc or stainless heavy-duty buckles (e.g. the NRS 1-inch HD cam buckle); roller cam buckles (Rollercam); complete cam straps and loop straps (NRS, Rollercam, Ancra, Kinedyne); buckle bumpers (covers that stop the buckle scratching paint); polypropylene, polyester and nylon webbing in matching widths.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Working load limit is about one third of breaking strength: NRS 1-inch strap 1,500 lb breaking, 500 lb working load limit; 1.5-inch 2,000 lb breaking, 650 lb working load limit (NRS product page; Rafting Magazine).
  - Tension is limited to your hand pull: 'You get exactly as much tension as your arm can produce and not one pound more' (DC Cargo). Friction in a plain (static) cam buckle can make the strap tension 'two to three times less' than the pull on the tail (US 6,877,189); roller cam buckles reduce that friction (Rollercam; US 6,877,189).
  - Add straps rather than trusting one: Rafting Magazine's example of five 125 lb boats on two 1-inch straps is outside the straps' working load limit; use more straps, wider 1.5-inch straps, and at least four contact points even for small boats.
  - Snug, not crushing: on kayaks and canoes, pull 'until the boat stops moving when you push it side to side, then stop'; if the hull flexes inward you have gone too far (DC Cargo).
  - Put two or three twists in a long open span of strap to stop it humming in the wind (DC Cargo); NRS warns this vibration can 'melt or fray your strap'.
  - Keep the buckle off the load's surface: a buckle pressed flat on a hull 'vibrates a pressure mark into the plastic' (DC Cargo); NRS sells buckle bumpers to protect vehicles and composite kayaks (Rafting Magazine).
  - The cam's surface roughness and its angle to the bar decide whether the buckle can still be released under load (German Wikipedia, 'Spanngurt').

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Roof-rack straps for kayaks, canoes and surfboards (DC Cargo)
  - Rafting straps holding gear and frames on whitewater rafts; NRS first sold its 1-inch strap in 1978 for this (NRS)
  - Bag, backpack and bike-rack straps (German Wikipedia: tie-down straps are used to close bags and backpacks)
  - Military and tactical webbing: quick-release cam buckles go back to ALICE gear and are used on modern slings (Milspec Monkey)
- **when_to_use**: When a strap needs to be pulled snug by hand, hold at any length and come off in one press: tying kayaks, canoes and boards to roof racks, lashing gear to rafts and trailers, bundling timber, pipes or tent poles, closing bags and bike loads. It is the better choice when over-tightening could damage the load, because a hand pull can't crush a hull the way a ratchet can (DC Cargo), and it is quick and simple to use (Rollercam).
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid it for heavy cargo on trucks and trailers that needs high tension or a rated working load: use ratchet straps (Rollercam; Rafting Magazine). Avoid it for lifting, climbing, rescue or anchor lines: Rafting Magazine says cam straps make poor flip lines, because 1-inch tubular webbing is far stronger (about 4,000 lb against 1,500 lb). For a strap you set once and rarely adjust, a ladder-lock buckle or tri-glide is simpler and has no spring. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **environment_limits**: Webbing sets most of the limits: DIN EN 12195-2 (via German Wikipedia) gives about -40 to 80 °C for polypropylene, -40 to 100 °C for polyamide and -40 to 120 °C for polyester webbing. Polypropylene creeps under load, but it keeps its strength when wet, resists rot and UV well and floats (Rafting Magazine). Wind vibration can melt or fray a strap unless it is twisted (NRS). Printed buckles: PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV; PETG is fine outdoors below about 80 °C; ASA is UV-resistant up to 93 °C (Prusa material pages). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Never use a cam strap to lift or hold a person, or as a rescue or anchor line (Rafting Magazine). A loose strap tail 'whipping at highway speed will crack a windshield, scratch paint, or work the buckle loose', so tie off or tuck the tail (DC Cargo). A heavy metal buckle thrown over a car can crack a window (Rafting Magazine). If one of two straps fails, the load can shift and break the other, so use enough straps (Rafting Magazine). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: Strap loosens on a long drive: polypropylene creep and settling of the load (Rafting Magazine); stop and re-tension. Strap frays or melts: wind flutter (twist it, NRS) or rubbing on sharp edges. Load damage: buckle pressed against the hull or paint (DC Cargo). Load lost: too few straps for the weight (Rafting Magazine). Printed buckles: thin cross-bars break (Robin Lunde's Printables note). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **related**: 
  - Ratchet tie-down strap (ratchet buckle): winds webbing onto a spool with a lever, giving much higher tension for heavy cargo, but can over-tighten and crush (DC Cargo; Rollercam)
  - Ladder-lock buckle (tension lock) and tri-glide: slotted buckles with no spring or cam that hold by friction when loaded
  - Side-release buckle: joins two strap ends with springy arms but doesn't tension the strap
  - Over-centre cam band buckle (scuba tank strap): a lever that snaps past centre to tension a band (Wikipedia 'Scuba set')
  - Cam cleat: the same spring-loaded toothed-cam idea for round rope, released by lifting the rope out
  - Cord lock (spring toggle): a spring plunger that pinches a thin cord
  - Binding-plate lock (tilting friction plate): a tilting plate that bites a rod one way

## 124 Ladder lock buckle (tension lock, tri-glide)

*cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware.* A slotted buckle that locks a strap by friction when it is loaded and slides when you tilt it; tri-glides are the simpler non-locking version

*Also covers:* Also covers double D-rings and G-hooks

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A flat plastic or metal buckle with slots and bars that a strap weaves through; it holds by itself when the strap is pulled tight, and lets the strap run when you lift its tab
- **term**: Ladder lock buckle (tension lock); the non-locking version is a tri-glide slide; variants covered here: double D-ring fastener and G-hook
- **aliases**: 
  - ladder lock / ladderlock
  - tension lock buckle (Buckleguy: 'Ladder locks, AKA tension lock buckles')
  - ladder buckle
  - ladder lock slider buckle / slider buckle
  - strap adjuster / webbing adjuster
  - Ladderloc (brand, ITW Nexus), Superloc (brand, ITW Nexus)
  - tri-glide slide / tri-glide / triglide (non-locking version)
  - webbing slide / slide (non-locking version)
  - Triglide (brand spelling, ITW Nexus)
  - Conway buckle (curved slide with a post, used on horse tack; a close relative of the tri-glide)
  - double D-ring fastener / double-D ring / D-ring buckle (variant)
  - G-hook / tension G hook (variant) *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **family**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware
- **other_families**: self-locking & anti-back-drive, clamping, magnetic & friction connections, tensioning, reels & take-up, quick-release & tool-free
- **one_liner**: A one-piece buckle with two or three bars that a strap weaves through, so that pulling on the load makes the strap pinch itself against a bar and hold by friction, while tilting the buckle's tab lets the strap slide to lengthen it; a tri-glide is the same idea without the lock, and double D-rings and G-hooks are close relatives.
- **complexity**: Single piece. Ladder lock: one moulded or cast frame with a fixed bar for the sewn-on strap, one or two gripping bars (often with small teeth or ridges) and a finger tab. Tri-glide: a rectangle split by one centre bar (two slots). Double D-ring: two D-shaped metal rings sewn side by side on one strap end. G-hook: a flat G-shaped hook with a slot for the strap. The webbing strap itself is the other half of the joint.
- **how_it_works**: One strap is sewn permanently round the back bar; the adjustable strap comes up through the middle gap, goes round the gripping bar next to the tab and back out, so that when the load pulls, the strap is pinched between itself and the bar and friction holds it (a friction lock that tightens itself as the load rises). Pulling the free tail shortens the strap even under load, but the load cannot pull it back out. Lifting the tab tilts the gripping bar so the strap is no longer pinched and it can run out to lengthen. A tri-glide has no tab and no self-tightening pinch: the strap simply zig-zags over and under its centre bar and is held by friction and tension, so it is used for adjusting and tidying rather than as a lock. A double D-ring works like a slip knot: the strap goes through both rings and back through one, and pulling on it squeezes the rings together on the strap.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: pull the free tail to shorten a strap and it stays tight
  - lock, and release by hand: holds under load until you lift the tab
  - stop it moving back (one-way): the strap can be pulled shorter but the load cannot pull it longer
  - join and hold: joins a strap end to a bag, frame or another strap
- **motion**: slide: the strap slides through the buckle when you adjust it (unlimited travel along the strap); the ladder lock itself also tilts a few tens of degrees when you lift its tab to release
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 when loaded and locked. When released (tab lifted, or a tri-glide with no load), 1: the strap slides through the buckle along its length.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): load on the strap makes it pinch itself tighter against the bar, so it holds without a spring or catch; it can still creep under repeated jerky loads
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): you release it by lifting the tab, which tilts the bar and frees the strap
  - one-way (moves freely one way, locks the other): pulling the free end shortens the strap at any time, but the load cannot lengthen it
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): pull the tail to cinch a load down and it stays tight
  - quick-release: lifting the tab lets the strap run out quickly; a G-hook unhooks from its webbing loop in one movement
  - none (tri-glide): a tri-glide only holds by friction and has no locking action of its own
- **holding_principle**: friction or clamping (force-fit): the strap is pinched between itself and a bar (ladder lock, double D-ring) or wound over and under a bar (tri-glide); ridges or teeth on the bar add grip. A G-hook holds by shape (the hook sits through a webbing loop) while tension keeps it seated.
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **reuse_cycles**: effectively unlimited: adjusting is its everyday job and there is no spring or snap arm to wear out; the webbing wears first
- **tools_needed**: none to use or adjust. Fitting: sewing machine or needle and heavy thread to sew the fixed end; a lighter or hot knife to seal cut ends of synthetic webbing; none for no-thread tri-glides
- **seal**: none
- **load**: light to medium. A retailer states its low-profile ladder locks hold 60 lb (27 kg) without bending or snapping and are at least twice as strong as ordinary ones (Zpacks). A maker's PETG-printed ladder lock lifted a 10 kg watering can without deforming (Printables model 2433). Strong in a straight pull along the strap; weak against repeated jerky or on-off loads (the strap creeps through), against a sideways pull that tilts the buckle, and on thin or slippery webbing. Tri-glides hold less and should not be the only fastener on anything safety-related. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **adjustable**: yes: the strap length can be changed at any time by pulling the tail (shorter) or lifting the tab (longer), steplessly along the strap
- **play_and_precision**: not applicable (it adjusts a flexible strap and does not locate parts); the strap can creep a little under repeated loads, so set length is not exactly repeatable
- **access_needed**: not applicable: it hangs on a strap and can be reached from all round; you only need to reach the tab and the strap tail
- **materials**: Usually injection-moulded acetal (POM), nylon or polypropylene-type plastics; also die-cast or stamped metal (zinc alloy, steel, aluminium) for tri-glides and slides, and titanium or stainless steel for G-hooks and double D-rings. Works with flat woven webbing (nylon, polyester, polypropylene) matched to the slot width; thin grosgrain ribbon and twill tape tend to slip. 3D-printed versions: PETG (tested), ASA for sun and heat.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: cheap to buy, and printable in one piece without supports; the hard part is sewing the fixed end and choosing webbing thick enough to grip

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part (most common): plastic ladder locks, tri-glides, D-rings and G-hooks sold by webbing width
  - 3D print (FDM): single-piece ladder locks and tri-glides print flat without supports
  - sheet metal / die casting (how metal slides and tri-glides are made; Hawk Hill describes die-cast and stamped slides)
  - injection moulding (how commercial plastic ones are made; not a home process)
- **print_in_place**: yes: ladder locks and tri-glides are single pieces that work straight off the print bed (e.g. Printables 263258 'No supports, No bridging, Single Piece'); the strap is threaded afterwards
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Print it flat, with the bars lying on the bed, so the bars are made of long strands of plastic rather than stacked layers that can split apart (one single-piece design says to print 'with the flat side down and the tab facing up'; Protolabs Network/Hubs warns that parts built up in the Z direction are weaker because of layer bonding). Use a tough material: PETG is the usual choice. One maker's PETG ladder lock, printed at 0.15 mm layers and 80% infill with no supports, lifted a 10 kg watering can without deforming. Another designer prints in PETG (or ASA) at 0.20 mm layers and 100% infill, with a 0.4 mm nozzle. A 24 mm printed ladder lock takes webbing up to 24.6 mm wide and 4.3 mm thick. Chamfer or round every edge the webbing touches so it does not saw through the strap (one tri-glide design chamfers all edges; a customisable one has a 'broken edges' option). For grip, add small ridges or teeth on the top of the gripping bar next to the tab, as commercial ladder locks do. To scale a design, divide your webbing width by the designed width and scale by that factor. A no-thread tri-glide can have a slot cut through its centre bar so it clips onto an existing strap. Don't use printed strap hardware where a failure could hurt someone. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search for: 'ladder lock buckle' or 'ladderlock' by webbing width (e.g. 20 mm/3/4 in, 25 mm/1 in, 38 mm/1.5 in, 50 mm/2 in); 'tension lock buckle'; 'curved ladder lock'; 'tri-glide slide' / 'webbing slide' (plastic, or metal die-cast/stamped); 'sternum strap slide'; 'double D-ring' / 'D-rings' (metal or plastic); 'G-hook' / 'tension G hook' (plastic, aluminium, titanium or stainless steel); flat nylon or polypropylene webbing to match. Side-release buckles often have a ladder lock built into one or both halves ('dual adjust'). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Match the webbing to the slot width; if the webbing is too narrow it may slip under load (Trivantage, https://www.trivantage.com/blog/products/fastener-physics-101-cam-buckles-ladderloc-webbing).
  - Thread it the right way: the webbing comes up from below through the same gap between the two inner bars on both sides; the fixed loop is sewn, the adjustable strap wraps the bar nearest the tab, and that bar has teeth on top to stop slipping. Lifting the tab lets the webbing run out (Sailrite threading instructions, https://directus.sailrite.com/assets/559fd74e-792e-4a5d-8941-b2329371258b).
  - Find the right side of the buckle: it is the side where the bar nearest the tab stands higher than the other bar, and it may be ribbed (Sacôtin, https://sacotin.com/en/ladder-lock-buckle-tutorial/).
  - Grosgrain ribbon and twill tape slip through most ladder locks from 3/4 in (20 mm) upwards; use medium-weight nylon webbing, or sew two layers of grosgrain together (Backpacking Light forum, https://backpackinglight.com/forums/topic/42388/).
  - Ladder locks are best for a 'set it once' adjustment; they can creep under bouncing, on-off loads (e.g. a dog pack). Fixes that worked: thicker webbing, adding a tri-glide, or tacking the tail with a few hand stitches where it rarely needs adjusting (Hammock Forums, https://www.hammockforums.net/forum/archive/index.php/t-87145.html; Trivantage, see above).
  - Never use a tri-glide as the only fastener on safety equipment (e.g. climbing harness, dog collar): pair it with a buckle and stitch the strap end so it cannot slip out (Hawk Hill, https://www.hawk-hill.com/glide-slide-hardware/).
  - A printed PETG ladder lock (0.15 mm layers, 80% infill) held 10 kg without deforming (Printables model 2433, read via https://api.printables.com/graphql/).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - backpack shoulder straps: the buckle at the bottom of each strap that you pull to tighten and lift to loosen
  - bra straps and bag straps adjusted with a small metal or plastic tri-glide slide
  - motorcycle helmet chin straps closed with two metal D-rings (double D-ring)
  - roll-top bags and vests closed with a flat G-hook hooked into a webbing loop
- **when_to_use**: When a strap needs to be adjusted in length and then hold without knots: backpack shoulder, hip and compression straps, bag straps, pet harnesses, camera straps, tie-down straps for light loads, and sewn gear (making your own gear, 'MYOG'). Ladder locks suit one-handed tightening and quick loosening. Tri-glides suit length adjustment and tidying a strap next to another buckle. Double D-rings give a very simple, strong, infinitely adjustable metal closure (used on motorcycle helmets). G-hooks give a flat, quick hook-on attachment to a webbing loop.
- **when_not_to_use**: Not for life-safety or heavy loads (climbing, fall arrest, lifting, cargo tie-downs): use rated buckles, cam buckles or ratchet straps. Not where the load bounces on and off all day and must not creep (use a cam buckle, a stitched or fixed strap, or add a tri-glide or stitches). Not with thin or slippery ribbon (use thicker webbing or a cam buckle). If you need to join and separate two straps quickly, add a side-release buckle; if you need a set length that never changes, sew the strap instead.
- **environment_limits**: Acetal (the usual buckle plastic) has a service temperature range of -40 °F to 140 °F (-40 °C to 60 °C) for ITW Nexus parts, and ITW says its Ladderloc is 'unaffected by extremes in weather and temperature'. Nylon absorbs water, so its strength and stiffness change with humidity. Polyolefin (polypropylene-type) hardware loses properties at low temperatures, and ITW recommends it only for basic, low-performance uses. PLA prints soften in heat and degrade in sunlight; *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Friction adjusters can slip under jerky loads, so don't let a ladder lock or tri-glide be the only thing holding safety gear or a load overhead. Double D-ring helmet straps must be threaded and tightened correctly on every ride. Loose strap tails can snag, so tuck or secure them. Small plastic parts are a choking hazard for small children (noted on a printable tri-glide).
- **common_failures**: (1) The strap slips or creeps, from wrong threading, an upside-down buckle, webbing too thin or narrow for the slot, slippery webbing, or bouncing on-off loads. Fix it by re-threading, using thicker or wider webbing, choosing a buckle with toothed bars, adding a tri-glide, or tacking the tail with stitches. (2) Accidental release when something catches and lifts the tab; route straps so the tab is not snagged. (3) The plastic bar cracks under shock load or in deep cold, or a printed bar splits along its layers; print it flat and use tough, good-quality material. (4) Sharp edges wear through the webbing; round or chamfer them. (5) A tri-glide used as a buckle lets go when the strap is slack or slippery; pair it with a buckle and stitch the end.
- **related**: 
  - Cam buckle (webbing cam buckle): a spring-loaded toothed cam grips the strap; better for frequent adjustment and on-off loads, and releases with a lever instead of a tilt
  - Side-release buckle: joins and separates two straps quickly; often has a ladder lock built into one or both halves ('dual adjust')
  - Guy-line slider (friction line tensioner): the same pinch-by-friction idea for round cord instead of flat webbing
  - Cord lock (spring toggle): grips a round cord with a spring-loaded plunger instead of a pinch
  - Ratchet strap: winds the strap onto a spool with a ratchet for much higher tie-down loads
  - Traditional belt buckle with prong: holds by a pin through punched holes, with fixed steps instead of stepless adjustment; the textile baseline
  - Conway buckle: a curved tri-glide-like slide with a post through a punched hole, used on horse tack
  - Binding-plate lock (tilting friction plate): the same 'tilt to release, jam under load' principle used on rods and tubes

## 125 Side-release buckle

*cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware.* A plastic buckle whose two springy arms snap into a housing and release when you squeeze both sides, as on backpacks

### What it is

- **plain_name**: The plastic backpack clip: a pronged half that clicks into a hollow half, and comes apart when you pinch the two sides
- **term**: Side-release buckle (plastic snap-fit buckle)
- **aliases**: 
  - side release buckle / SRB
  - quick-release buckle
  - parachute buckle
  - Fastex buckle (Fastex is an ITW brand name, used as a generic name)
  - clip buckle / clip
  - snap-fit buckle / snap buckle
  - male and female buckle; latch and body (ACW); plug and socket (patent wording)
  - curved or contoured side-release buckle; dual-adjust side-release buckle (variants)
  - Klickschnalle / Steckschnalle (German)
  - boucle rapide (French)
- **family**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware
- **other_families**: snap & clip fits, quick-release & tool-free, locks, latches & catches, compliant mechanisms & flexures
- **one_liner**: A two-part plastic buckle in which two springy arms on the male half flex inward as it is pushed into the female half, then click outward into side windows so it cannot pull out, until you pinch both arms through the windows to let it go.
- **complexity**: A few parts (two moulded pieces). Male half (latch or plug): a strap bar, a stiff centre tongue (guide post) and two springy arms with hooked heads. Female half (body or socket): a hollow housing with an open mouth, a guide channel for the tongue, two side windows the heads snap into, and a strap bar. One or both halves may carry a ladder-lock adjuster. Metal versions add pivoting arms and a separate spring.
- **how_it_works**: As you push the male half in, sloped faces on the arm heads hit the mouth of the housing and bend the two arms inward (cantilever snap fits). When the heads reach the side windows, the arms spring back out and the flat back faces of the heads catch on the window edges with a click, so a pull on the straps cannot separate them. The stiff centre tongue slides in a channel to line the halves up and resist twisting, and it also stops the arms being bent too far. To open, you press both heads inward through the windows at once and slide the male half out; pressing only one side does not release it.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - join and hold: connects two strap ends
  - lock, and release by hand: stays shut under strap tension until you pinch both sides
  - tighten and hold tension (adjustable versions only): a built-in ladder lock lets you pull the strap tight
- **motion**: slide: the male half slides straight in and out of the housing (about the length of the arms); the arms flex a millimetre or two sideways as it snaps
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 when latched (the two halves are locked together). While being joined or released, 1: sliding in and out along the strap.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - quick-release: joins with one push and opens with one pinch, even one-handed
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): strap tension cannot open it; a person must squeeze both arms together
  - self-aligning or self-centring: the centre tongue runs in a guide channel that centres the male half as it goes in and helps resist twisting loads
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): only on versions with a built-in ladder-lock adjuster
- **holding_principle**: shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): hooked heads catch on the edges of side windows, held there by spring force (the arms' own springiness, a compliant cantilever)
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: the two halves separate by hand; the straps are usually sewn round each half's bar (semi-permanent), or threaded through an adjuster
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: made to be opened and closed daily, but the arms bend every time and can eventually crack from fatigue (patent US6378466 notes the arms 'may break' after many flexures)
- **tools_needed**: none to use. Fitting: needle and thread or sewing machine to sew strap loops; none for adjustable ends. Printed versions of some designs use an M3 screw as a strap pin (Allen key)
- **seal**: none
- **load**: light to medium, and it varies widely by maker. American Cord & Webbing (ACW) states its 1-inch MSR side-release buckle has a tensile strength of 300 lb (about 1.3 kN) and a 1-inch high-strength design (T-1 Bullet) about 500 lb (about 2.2 kN), and warns that there is 'no single strength rating' and that buckles of the same width from different makers can differ greatly. Strong in a straight pull along the straps. Weaker under combined twisting and straight loads (the arms can unlatch or break, patent US5737810), under sudden shock loads such as a lunging pet (patent US6378466), and under side pressure on the arms, which releases it. Thin, flat ones for 20 mm binding tape 'will break under high tension' (Zpacks).
- **adjustable**: yes on adjustable versions: one or both halves have a ladder-lock bar so the strap length can be changed; plain versions have fixed sewn loops
- **play_and_precision**: small: a little looseness in and out and side to side once latched; the centre tongue and channel keep the halves lined up. Not a precision locating joint.
- **access_needed**: not applicable: it hangs on a strap; to open it you need finger and thumb on both side arms at once
- **materials**: Usually acetal (POM), which is stiff, keeps its spring and gives a clear click, or tough nylon (polyamide), which resists impact but absorbs water so its stiffness changes; polyolefin (polypropylene-type) for cheap, low-duty buckles; heat-stabilised plastics for fire-resistant gear; die-cast zinc, aluminium or steel for metal versions. The original patent names 'a tough resilient plastic material such as nylon or Delrin' (acetal). Webbing: nylon, polyester or polypropylene matched to the buckle width. 3D-printed versions: PETG (most common), ASA for sun and heat, nylon for the flexible male part; PLA works for light duty but is less springy.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to buy and fit (cheap, sold by webbing width); moderate to 3D print: the springy arms need a tough material, printing flat, and careful clearances, and the hollow female half needs supports cleaned out

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part (most common)
  - 3D print (FDM): two-part printable designs and parametric OpenSCAD generators exist
  - 3D print (SLS/MJF nylon), recommended by Protolabs Network for snap fits that are opened and closed many times
  - injection moulding (how commercial buckles are made; not a home process)
  - die casting / machining (metal buckles; not a typical home process)
- **print_in_place**: no: the male and female halves are printed separately and snapped together; the female half usually needs support material inside
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Print the male half flat on the bed so the springy arms are made of long strands in the X-Y plane (Appropedia: 'print with prongs flat on bed'; Protolabs Network/Hubs: avoid snap-fit arms built vertically in Z, because layer bonding makes them weaker). The female half can be printed flat with support 'everywhere' (Appropedia), or standing up with supports (one Printables remix). Clear every trace of support from inside the housing, or the buckle will be hard to use. Materials: PETG is the usual choice. One designer uses ASA or PETG for outdoor use in heat and strong sunlight. The outer (female) part should be the stiffer one, and the inner (male) part needs to be 'somewhat flexible' (ASA, nylon or PETG). PLA works for normal use but is less springy and less UV-resistant. Settings used on tested designs: 0.2 mm layers, 0.4 mm nozzle, 4 perimeters and 100% infill for strength (a Printables 24 mm buckle); or 30% infill and PLA at 210 °C for a light-duty parametric buckle (Appropedia). Mouse-ear brims help the thin arm tips stick. General snap-fit rules from Protolabs Network: leave about 0.5 mm clearance between mating parts for FDM; add a fillet at the root of each arm of at least half the arm's thickness; taper the arm towards its tip; make it at least 5 mm wide; and make sure the arm is only bent while clicking in, not all the time. Scaling a 25 mm design to 80% width made it fit 20 mm straps. Designers warn that printed buckles are untested for 'life or death' uses.
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Supplier product-line names you may see: Fastex, Classic SR, GTSR (ITW Nexus, brands); Mojave (Duraflex, brand); MSR, T-1 Bullet (ACW, brand). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - There is no single strength rating: check the maker's figure and never assume one 1-inch buckle is as strong as another; for safety-critical uses, test the whole strap-and-buckle assembly under the real (dynamic) load (ACW, https://acw1.com/side-release-buckles/).
  - Example ratings: ACW's 1-inch MSR side-release buckle is rated at 300 lb tensile and its 1-inch T-1 Bullet at 500 lb (ACW, https://acw1.com/side-release-buckles/).
  - Choose acetal for a crisp click that keeps its spring after many releases, and nylon for impact toughness and flex; nylon hooks can 'creep open' after long use (My Fave Straps, https://myfavestraps.com/blogs/technical/plastic-hardware-defined; ACW, see above; ITW Nexus materials, https://na.itwnexus.com/content/general-material-descriptions-0).
  - For FDM snap arms: about 0.5 mm clearance, a fillet at the arm root of at least 0.5 times the root thickness, a taper towards the tip, at least 5 mm width, and no arms printed upright in Z (Protolabs Network/Hubs, https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/how-design-snap-fit-joints-3d-printing/).
  - Print the male half with its prongs flat on the bed; print the female half with support and clear it out fully (Appropedia, https://www.appropedia.org/Parametric_quick_release_buckles).
  - Combined straight and twisting loads can make the arms unlatch or break; a centre guide post in a channel and larger latching shoulders were patented to resist this (US5737810, https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5737810).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: backpack hip belts and chest (sternum) straps, some with a whistle built into the buckle, pet collars and harnesses, life jackets, luggage straps and camera bags, sandal and boot straps
- **when_to_use**: When two strap ends must be joined and separated quickly, often and one-handed, with no tools and a clear 'click' that shows it is shut: backpacks, bags, pet collars, life jackets, straps on gear, and do-it-yourself sewn projects. Light, cheap, rust-free and available in standard webbing widths. Pick an adjustable (ladder-lock) version when the strap length must change.
- **when_not_to_use**: Not for climbing, fall arrest, lifting or other life-safety loads: use rated metal hardware (carabiners, rated buckles) instead. Not where a sudden shock load could snap the plastic, such as a strong dog lunging (use a metal side-release buckle or a metal buckle with a prong). Not where things pressing on the sides could open it by accident (use a buckle with a release guard or a different latch). Not in deep cold with cheap or unknown plastics. For a flat, low-profile hook-on use a G-hook; to cinch a strap tight often, use a cam buckle.
- **environment_limits**: ITW Nexus quotes a service temperature of -40 °F to 140 °F (-40 °C to 60 °C) for its acetal buckles. Nylon absorbs water, so it is stronger but less impact-resistant when dry, and weaker but tougher when wet. Polyolefin (polypropylene-type) buckles lose properties in the cold. For printed buckles, ASA or PETG is advised in sun and heat. Arms that stay bent or under constant load creep and lose their spring (Protolabs Network, on snap fits in general). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: A plastic buckle can break suddenly under a shock load; one patent gives the example of a lunging pet. Printable designs are labelled not for 'life or death' use. Loaded straps can whip back if a buckle fails. The small parts are a choking hazard. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: (1) The arms crack or snap from fatigue after many openings, or from cold, shock loads or a cheap resin (a Wikimedia Commons photo shows a backpack buckle with fatigue fractures in three places); buy good-quality acetal or nylon and replace cracked buckles. (2) It releases under load when twisting and pulling forces combine and push the arms inward; choose designs with a strong centre guide and deep latching shoulders. (3) Only one arm clicks in, or it is not fully latched; listen and feel for both clicks and tug to check. (4) It is hard to open or jams because of dirt, sand or support material left inside a printed one; clean it out. (5) Printed arms split along layer lines; print them flat and use PETG, ASA or nylon. (6) The strap slips in an adjustable version; see the ladder lock entry (thread it correctly and use webbing of the right thickness).
- **related**: 
  - Cantilever snap fit: the general principle; the side-release buckle is two cantilever snap fits working as a pair
  - Ladder lock buckle / tri-glide: adjusts strap length; often built into one or both halves of a side-release buckle
  - Magnetic-assisted buckle: magnets pull and line the halves together, and a catch takes the load
  - Cam buckle: cinches a strap tight and releases with a lever, but does not separate into two halves
  - Belt buckle with prong (conventional buckle): metal, strong, stepped adjustment, slower to open
  - Seat-belt style centre-release buckle: a push-button releases a metal tongue; for higher, safety-rated loads
  - Spring button lock (snap button): the same 'spring pops into a hole, press to release' idea used on tubes *(unverified parts omitted)*

## 126 Cord lock (spring toggle)

*cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware.* A spring-loaded plunger in a small barrel that grips a cord passing through it; squeeze to slide and adjust

### What it is

- **plain_name**: The little spring-loaded plastic stopper on a hoodie or stuff-sack drawstring: squeeze it to slide it along the cord, let go and it grips the cord where it is
- **term**: Cord lock (spring cord lock, spring toggle)
- **aliases**: 
  - cord toggle / toggle
  - cord stopper / cord stop
  - drawstring toggle / drawstring stopper / drawstring lock
  - spring toggle stopper / spring stop
  - cord fastener, plastic stopper, spring clasp (Wikipedia)
  - barrel cord lock / barrel lock; ball cord lock; single-hole and double-hole cord locks (shape variants)
  - Cordloc (brand spelling, ITW Nexus)
  - Kordelstopper / Tanka (German)
- **family**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware
- **other_families**: clamping, magnetic & friction connections, self-locking & anti-back-drive, quick-release & tool-free
- **one_liner**: A small barrel with a spring-loaded plunger inside: when you let go, the spring pushes the plunger's hole out of line with the barrel's holes and pinches the cord passing through; when you squeeze, the holes line up and the lock slides freely along the cord.
- **complexity**: A few parts: a barrel or body (housing) with a hole across it, a plunger (toggle, piston) with a matching cross hole and a thumb button, and a spring (usually a small metal coil spring). Some designs replace the metal spring with a springy plastic part moulded onto the plunger (e.g. a W-shaped spring in patent US4622723), and some have no spring at all, using a wedge-shaped plunger instead.
- **how_it_works**: The cord runs through a hole in the barrel and a hole in the plunger. The spring pushes the plunger outward, so its hole sits off-centre from the barrel's holes and the cord is pinched between the edge of the plunger hole and the barrel wall. Friction holds the cord in either direction (a spring-loaded clamp). Pressing the plunger in against the spring lines the holes up, the pinch is released and the lock slides along the cord; let go and it grips again. How hard it grips depends on the spring force and on how well the cord fills the hole.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: holds a drawstring pulled tight so a bag or hood stays closed
  - lock, and release by hand: grips until you squeeze it
  - join and hold: holds two cord ends together or stops a cord pulling back through a hole
- **motion**: slide: the plunger slides a few millimetres in the barrel when squeezed, and the whole lock slides along the cord when released
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 along the cord when gripping (it can still turn on the cord). When squeezed, 1: sliding along the cord. The plunger itself has 1 (in and out of the barrel).
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): pulling the cord does not open it; you have to squeeze the plunger
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): the spring keeps the cord pinched with no effort; a hard pull on the cord can still drag it through, because the grip is only as strong as the spring
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): slide it up the cord to close a bag or hood and it stays put
  - quick-release: one squeeze frees the cord at once
  - one-way (some springless designs only): a rectangular springless type with small teeth was found to be hard to slide one way and easy the other
- **holding_principle**: friction or clamping (force-fit), with spring force (a metal or plastic spring presses the plunger to pinch the cord)
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: it slides off the cord end when squeezed (unless the cord ends are knotted or capped); the lock can be taken off by untying or unthreading the cord
- **reuse_cycles**: effectively unlimited for everyday squeezing; a good stainless spring keeps its springiness, but cheap steel springs rust and all-plastic ones can jam or lose grip
- **tools_needed**: none. To thread cord: squeeze and push the cord through. Some ITW cord locks are supplied with the spring not engaged and are armed by pushing the button hard once
- **seal**: none
- **load**: light. It is made to hold a drawstring, a hood or a stuff sack closed, not to carry weight. The grip depends on the spring and on matching the cord to the hole: in a side-by-side test, cheap round 'ball' cord locks barely held 3 mm paracord, while stiff-spring barrel types held firmly. It holds best with the pull along the cord; a hard jerk can drag the cord through. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **adjustable**: yes: squeeze and slide it anywhere along the cord, steplessly
- **play_and_precision**: not applicable (it clamps a cord at any point and does not locate parts)
- **access_needed**: not applicable: it hangs on the cord; you pinch the button and the barrel between finger and thumb
- **materials**: Spring: stainless or plain steel coil spring, or a moulded plastic spring. Cords: round drawcord, paracord, shock (bungee) cord and flat cords that fit the hole. 3D-printed versions: PETG (also needed for printed springs), ABS for items that go through a washer and dryer. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to buy and fit; moderate to 3D print, because the spring needs a tough springy material or a salvaged metal spring (e.g. from a pen), and the small parts need 100% infill and several perimeters

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part (by far the most common)
  - 3D print (FDM): body and plunger print easily; the spring can be a salvaged metal spring, a printed PETG spring, or designed into a one-piece springless body
  - injection moulding (how commercial cord locks are made; not a home process)
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Print the small parts solid: one designer found parts with less than 100% infill broke at their thinnest point and used 100%, while another uses 4 perimeters so the tiny parts are strong. Use a tough material. PETG is the common choice, and one designer found PLA parts warped in a washer and dryer while ABS held up. The spring is the hard part. A metal coil spring about 11 mm long with a 5-9 mm outside diameter fits one spherical design. Printed springs must be PETG: one designer says 'PLA will not work well'. Print 6-8 small printed springs at once, so each layer has time to cool before the next. Capture the plunger with small snap nubs that click past a lip, so the spring cannot push it out. Size the holes to the cord you will use (see the hole sizes of commercial locks under rules_of_thumb); a wedge-type design with a 15° angle locked better than a wider one but took thinner cord. Printable one-piece springless designs (the body acts as the spring) work with 3-6 mm cord in PETG at 0.2 mm layers with no supports. Round the edges of the cord holes so they do not fray the cord.
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search for 'cord lock', 'spring cord lock', 'cord stopper', 'toggle stopper', 'drawstring toggle', 'single hole cord lock' or 'double hole cord lock'; shapes: barrel, ball (spherical), flat or oval; size by hole diameter (roughly 3-5 mm for drawcord and shock cord); 'stainless steel spring cord lock' for wet or salty use; 'micro' or 'mini cord lock' for thin (1-3 mm) cord; 'cord end' or 'cord end lock' to cap cord tips; 'shock cord' / 'bungee cord' and 'drawcord' to match. ITW sells a 'Cordloc' range (brand).
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Match the cord to the hole: a 3 mm hole takes two passes of 1.3 mm cord or 1/16 in shock cord, and a 5 mm hole takes two passes of cord up to 2.5 mm or 3/32 in shock cord (Zpacks, https://zpacks.com/products/mini-cord-lock-5-pack). Litesmith's Micro takes one line up to 2.9 mm or two up to 1.6 mm, and its Mini one line up to 3.7 mm or two up to 2.2 mm (https://www.litesmith.com/cord-locks/).
  - For thin line, a smaller lock with a stiffer spring grips better: Litesmith says its Micro has 'better holding power' on thin lines than the larger Mini with its weaker spring (https://www.litesmith.com/cord-locks/).
  - Typical size: 1-3 in (25-76 mm) long; generally three parts, a barrel, a plunger and a spring (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cord_lock).
  - For wet or salty use choose stainless springs: in an informal 6-week salt-water test, cheap springs corroded and lost grip, and an all-plastic springless lock jammed with salt and debris, while two brands with stainless springs kept their grip (Canoetripping.net forum, https://www.canoetripping.net/threads/cord-lock-experiments.123727/).
  - Print cord-lock slides at 100% infill (thinner infill broke at the smallest diameter), and use ABS or PETG rather than PLA for clothing that is machine washed and tumble dried (Printables model 201172, read via https://api.printables.com/graphql/).
  - Printed springs: use PETG, not PLA, and print several at once so each layer cools (Printables model 617291, read via https://api.printables.com/graphql/).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - the drawstring stoppers on a hoodie, jacket hood or jacket hem
  - stuff sacks, laundry bags and tent bags closed with a drawcord
  - backpack shock-cord lacing on the outside of a pack
  - window blind cords (Wikipedia)
- **when_to_use**: When you want to cinch a cord or drawstring and have it stay put without a knot, and loosen it with one squeeze: hoods, hems, stuff sacks, shock-cord lacing, lanyards, headlamp bands and simple cord tensioning on light gear. Good with gloves on or cold fingers, which is why mountaineers use them (Wikipedia).
- **when_not_to_use**: Not for holding real loads, such as guy lines in wind, hanging things or tie-downs: use a guy-line slider, a cam cleat, a knot or a buckle. Not on drawstrings in the hood or neck area of young children's clothing, where cords are a strangulation and entrapment hazard (EN 14682 in Europe prohibits them there for young children). Not in salt water with cheap steel springs (choose stainless). For flat webbing use a ladder lock or cam buckle instead.
- **environment_limits**: Cheap steel springs rust in salt water and lose grip. All-plastic enclosed locks can jam with salt, pollen and dirt. Stainless springs survived a 6-week salt-water test (informal forum test). PLA printed parts warped in a washer and dryer, while ABS was fine. A drawcord that is too thin or slippery for the hole will not hold at all. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Drawstrings and cords on children's clothing are a strangulation and entrapment risk. The European standard EN 14682 prohibits drawstrings and functional cords in the hood and neck area of clothes for young children (its strictest rules apply under 7 years) and also sets rules for toggles; more than 3,100 recalls of children's clothing with dangerous cords appear on the EU Safety Gate system. The small parts and springs are a choking hazard. The springs are small and can fly out if a lock is forced apart.
- **common_failures**: (1) It won't hold: the cord is too thin for the hole, the spring is too weak (common in cheap 'ball' types), or the cord is slippery. Fix it by using a smaller or stiffer-spring lock, double the cord, or use thicker cord. (2) The spring rusts or loses its springiness, especially in salt water; choose stainless springs and rinse the lock. (3) It jams with dirt, salt or ice; rinse and work it. (4) The plunger falls out or the body cracks (cheap plastic, or a printed part with low infill); choose good acetal parts, or print at 100% infill with a snap-captured plunger. (5) The cord frays at sharp hole edges; round the edges or choose a smoother lock. (6) The cord end pulls back out through the lock; knot the end or add a cord end cap.
- **related**: 
  - Guy-line slider (friction line tensioner): holds a cord by friction around a plate with holes, with no spring; for tent guy lines
  - Cam cleat / clam cleat: grips a rope pulled into toothed cams or V-grooves; much stronger, for boats and hauling
  - Ladder lock buckle / tri-glide: the friction-lock idea for flat webbing instead of round cord
  - Springless cord locks and toggles: a wedge or V-shaped plunger or a springy body does the job of the spring; one type grips harder in one direction
  - Knot or cord end: permanent or fixed stop with no adjustment
  - Spring plunger (ball detent): the same 'spring pushes a plunger' idea used to hold parts in position, not to grip a cord
  - Ratcheting reel closure (dial lacing): tightens a lace with a clicking reel for higher, repeatable tension

## 127 Magnetic-assisted buckle (magnet guides, catch locks)

*cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware.* A buckle where magnets pull the two halves together and line them up, then a mechanical catch locks them so the magnet doesn't carry the load

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A strap buckle that snaps itself shut when you bring the two halves close, because hidden magnets pull them together, but that only opens when you slide, tilt or pull it in one special direction
- **term**: Magnetic-mechanical buckle (magnet-assisted latching buckle)
- **aliases**: 
  - magnetic buckle
  - magnetic quick-release buckle
  - magnetic snap buckle / magnetic slide-release buckle / magnetic push buckle (variants named by how they open)
  - magnetic-mechanical fastener or connecting structure (patent wording)
  - Magnetsteckschnalle mit mechanischer Verriegelung (German patent title: magnetic plug-in buckle with mechanical lock)
  - self-closing buckle / self-securing buckle
  - magnetic helmet buckle / magnetic chin-strap buckle
  - Fidlock buckle (FIDLOCK is a brand, often used as a generic name; its buckle families are SNAP, SLIDER, HOOK and V-BUCKLE)
  - Woojin magnetic push buckle (brand)
- **family**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware
- **other_families**: locks, latches & catches, quick-release & tool-free, clamping, magnetic & friction connections, snap & clip fits, self-locking & anti-back-drive
- **one_liner**: A two-part strap buckle in which magnets pull the halves together and line them up so they click shut almost by themselves, while a mechanical catch (not the magnet) takes the strap's pull until you open it with a deliberate slide, tilt or tug.
- **complexity**: A few parts, or a small assembly that acts as one unit. Male (top or plug) half: a moulded body with a steel plate (armature) or a magnet, and a hook, tongue or snap tab. Female (bottom or socket) half: a moulded body with a sealed magnet and the matching catch (often a spring latch). A release: the sliding direction itself (SNAP, SLIDER types), a top part you tilt up (HOOK type), or a stainless-steel flap with a pull tab (V-BUCKLE type). Each half has a strap bar, split bar or ladder-lock adjuster for the webbing.
- **how_it_works**: When the halves come within a few millimetres, the magnet pulls them together and into line (self-aligning), and that pull also pushes the catch past its spring so it snaps home; the Fidlock patent WO2008006355A2 says the magnet's pull 'counteracts the locking force and thus facilitates the snapping'. Once latched, a pull on the straps is carried by the interlocking catch (a form-fit), not by the magnet; the related patent US8430434B2 says that when the closure is pulled, the locking elements are 'subjected only to shear forces'. Opening uses a different move from closing: you slide the halves sideways, tilt the top part up, or pull a flap, which frees the catch and moves the magnets apart until their pull is weak enough to separate by hand. On self-securing designs (V-BUCKLE) the catch grips harder as strap load rises, so it is 'harder to open the more load it is sustaining'.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - join and hold: connects two strap or webbing ends
  - lock, and release by hand: strap tension alone cannot open it; you open it with a deliberate slide, tilt or pull
  - line parts up exactly: the magnets pull the halves into the right position, so it closes one-handed or without looking
- **motion**: multiple: the halves come together in a short straight push that the magnets do for you (a few millimetres), and come apart with a different move depending on type: slide sideways (SNAP, SLIDER), tilt or lift the top part (HOOK), or pull a flap (V-BUCKLE)
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 when latched (the halves are locked together). While closing, 1 (the halves approach along one line, guided by the magnets); while opening, 1 (the release slide, tilt or pull).
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - self-aligning or self-centring: the magnets pull the halves into line and shut, so it can be closed with one hand, with gloves on, or under the chin where you cannot see it
  - quick-release: opens with one hand and one short movement, no tools
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): the catch, not the magnet, holds the strap load, and the release needs a specific direction of force; self-securing types (V-BUCKLE) get harder to open the more load they carry
- **holding_principle**: shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): a hook, tongue or spring latch catches behind a ledge and carries the strap load; plus magnetic (the magnet pulls the halves together and helps the catch snap in, but is not meant to carry the load) and spring force (the latch spring or springy snap tab)
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed): permanent neodymium magnets and a mechanical catch. Some versions add a reed switch that senses whether the buckle is open, but the holding is still passive.
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: the halves separate by hand; each half is sewn to its strap (semi-permanent) or threaded on with a split bar or ladder lock (removable)
- **tools_needed**: none to use. Fitting: needle and thread or a sewing machine for sewn loops; none for split-bar versions that slip onto existing webbing. Printed versions: a drop of superglue or epoxy to fix the magnets (PLA), or none if they press-fit (PETG).
- **seal**: none
- **load**: light to medium. Fidlock's own static breaking loads: SLIDER 25 about 245 N (25 kg), HOOK 15 helmet buckle 491 N (50 kg), SNAP helmet buckle 20 638 N (65 kg), V-BUCKLE 25 and V-BUCKLE 40 686 N (70 kg); a US reseller lists the 1-inch V-Buckle at 'Load Capacity: 70 kg (154 lbs.)'. Strong in a straight pull along the strap, which goes through the mechanical catch. Weak in the release direction by design: a sideways push opens slide-release types and a lift opens tilt types, although a reseller says 'specific directional forces are required to unlatch' so accidental opening is unlikely. A magnet on its own grips poorly sideways (sliding, or shear, force is about half its straight pull), which is why the catch is needed.
- **adjustable**: yes on versions with a ladder-lock adjuster on one or both halves (strap length can be changed); versions with a plain bar or sewn loop are not adjustable
- **play_and_precision**: Not a precision locating joint. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **access_needed**: not applicable: it hangs on a strap; you only need one hand at the buckle to close or open it
- **materials**: Commercial: glass-fibre reinforced nylon (PA66-GF), with PA6 carbon/glass-filled grades on helmet buckles; stainless steel for the pull flap, lever or D-ring; neodymium magnets sealed or moulded into the plastic. A reseller notes Fidlock uses nylon for the SNAP buckle and high-performance polyamides (Durethan or Grivory, brands) in SNAP helmet buckles. 3D-printed versions: PETG (magnets can press-fit) or PLA (magnets must be glued), with small neodymium blocks or discs such as 5 x 5 x 2 mm, 4 x 5 x 1.5 mm or 8 x 2.5 mm. Webbing: nylon or polyester matched to the buckle width (about 15-40 mm).
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to buy and fit (sold by webbing width; split-bar versions fit existing straps without sewing); moderate to 3D print: the magnet pockets must be sized right, the magnet polarity must be correct, and the catch must be strong enough, and printed ones are for light duty only

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part (most common)
  - 3D print (FDM): two printed halves with bought neodymium magnets pressed or glued in, or sealed in during a pause mid-print
  - injection moulding with moulded-in magnets (how commercial buckles are made; not a home process)
- **print_in_place**: no: the two halves are printed separately and the magnets are added afterwards (pressed or glued into pockets) or dropped in during a mid-print pause
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Pockets: design each magnet pocket 0.2-0.3 mm larger in diameter than the magnet and exactly as deep as the magnet is thick for a finger-pressure press fit (Rapid Elevation). A PETG print is springy enough for 5 x 5 x 2 mm magnets to press in without glue; in PLA, which is stiffer, glue them in (Magic strap buckle, Printables 84848). Avoid hot glue: it bonds poorly to PLA and softens when warm, so magnets move (Rapid Elevation). Polarity: test the pair with loose magnets and mark which face goes up before fitting them; one designer reports a buckle that 'will refuse to close' after he fitted a magnet the wrong way round, and a magnet sealed in mid-print cannot be turned over (Magic strap buckle; Rapid Elevation). Orientation: the Magic strap buckle prints in its supplied orientation with no supports and 100% infill for strength; a small Fidlock-style buckle was printed in PLA at 0.1 mm layers with 4 x 5 x 1.5 mm magnets glued in (Tiny Magnetic Buckle, Printables 463663). Catch: treat the hook or snap tab like any printed snap fit, with about 0.5 mm clearance for FDM, a fillet at the root at least half the arm's thickness, and arms that do not stand upright in Z, because layer lines make them weak (Hubs/Protolabs Network). Let the printed hook carry the load; the magnet only helps close it. Strap bars: one user's strap tensioner part broke on a backpack, so the designer added an 'extra strong' version, so make bars thick. Designers warn not to use printed buckles where 'loss of life or injuries could occur'.
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search for 'magnetic buckle' by webbing width (15, 20, 25, 38 or 40 mm; 5/8 in to 1-1/2 in). Fidlock (brand) families and how they open: SNAP buckle (slide sideways), SLIDER (slide sideways), HOOK (tilt or lift the top part), V-BUCKLE (pull the metal flap; self-securing). Variants: split bar (fits onto existing webbing), ladder lock (LL, adjustable), single bar (SB), sewable, swivel mount, helmet buckle, 'SNAP buckle with reed switch' (senses open or closed). Other makes: Woojin magnetic push buckle (brand, pinch left and right to release). For building your own: neodymium block or disc magnets (N-grade, e.g. 5 x 5 x 2 mm blocks, 4 x 5 x 1.5 mm blocks, 8 x 2.5 mm or 6 x 3 mm discs), plastic- or rubber-coated magnets for wet use. Do not confuse with a 'magnetic snap' or 'magnetic clasp' (bag and jewellery closures held by the magnet alone).
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Let a mechanical catch carry the strap load and use the magnet only to pull the parts together and help the catch snap in: the magnet's pull 'counteracts the locking force and thus facilitates the snapping' (Fidlock patent WO2008006355A2, https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2008006355A2/en), and when the closure is pulled the locking elements are 'subjected only to shear forces' (US8430434B2, https://patents.google.com/patent/US8430434B2/en).
  - A magnet's grip against sliding (shear) is only about half its straight pull; K&J Magnetics measured 47-70% and simulations gave 40-75% of the axial pull force (https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/magnet-shear-force). So a magnet-only closure slides apart much more easily than it pulls apart.
  - Typical ratings for commercial magnetic-mechanical buckles are 245-686 N static breaking load (SLIDER 25: 245 N; HOOK 15 helmet buckle: 491 N; SNAP helmet buckle 20: 638 N; V-BUCKLE 25 and 40: 686 N) (Fidlock product pages, e.g. https://www.fidlock.com/components/en/products/v-buckle?v=166 and https://www.fidlock.com/components/en/products/slider?v=53).
  - Check the maker's limits: the SLIDER is 'Not suitable for safety-related applications'; the SNAP helmet buckle is not for motorcycle or equestrian helmets; the V-BUCKLE is 'Not for use on dog leashes' (Fidlock product pages, e.g. https://www.fidlock.com/components/en/products/snap-helmet-buckle?v=147); a reseller adds 'NOT for use as a seat belt buckle ... or ... to support human loads' (https://www.ahh.biz/hardware/buckles/magnetic/fidlock-25mm-v-buckle.php).
  - Standard N-grade neodymium magnets lose some strength for good above 80 °C (176 °F) and all of it above 310 °C (https://www.kjmagnetics.com/faq.asp).
  - Magnet pockets in prints: 0.2-0.3 mm larger in diameter than the magnet and exactly the magnet's thickness deep (Rapid Elevation, https://rapidelevation.com/article-07-magnets-in-prints.html).
  - Printed snap catches: about 0.5 mm clearance for FDM, root fillet at least 0.5 x the arm's root thickness, and no arms built upright in Z (Hubs/Protolabs Network, https://www.hubs.com/knowledge-base/how-design-snap-fit-joints-3d-printing/).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - bicycle and ski helmet chin straps that close with a click under your chin
  - backpack chest straps, bag flaps and baby-carrier straps with a metal pull flap (V-BUCKLE style)
  - dog collars, and some sneaker and boot straps
- **when_to_use**: Good when you want the buckle to find its own position and close with a clear click, and when opening should need a deliberate, specific movement. Pick a self-securing type (V-BUCKLE style) where a heavier pull should make it harder, not easier, to open. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **when_not_to_use**: Not for life-safety or human-supporting loads (climbing, fall arrest, seat belts, lifting): use rated metal hardware. Not on dog leashes (maker's warning); use a rated metal clip or side-release buckle. Not where the release direction is likely to be knocked or rubbed, for slide- or tilt-opening types; use a side-release buckle, whose two arms must be pinched together. If the halves are held only by magnets with no catch (a magnetic snap or clasp), expect them to slide apart easily; use a real magnetic-mechanical buckle or a mechanical buckle instead. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **environment_limits**: Heat: standard N-grade neodymium magnets permanently lose part of their strength above 80 °C (176 °F), for example on a dashboard in a hot car; higher-temperature grades exist (K&J Magnetics). Water and salt: bare neodymium corrodes and loses pull force, so commercial buckles seal or mould the magnets into the plastic, and home-made ones should use coated magnets and keep the coating intact (K&J Magnetics). Printed buckles: PLA creeps and softens in heat, and hot-glued magnets move when warm (Rapid Elevation). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Small strong magnets are dangerous if swallowed: children who swallowed several magnets have had parts of the gut pinched between them, causing injury or death (Wikipedia, 'Neodymium magnet'), so keep loose magnets and broken DIY buckles away from children and pets. People with pacemakers or other implants should ask their doctor before handling strong magnets (K&J Magnetics). Makers state these buckles are not for human-supporting loads, and printed ones are not for uses where injury could occur. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: (1) A DIY buckle refuses to close because one magnet is in the wrong way round; test and mark polarity before fitting. (2) A glued or press-fitted magnet works loose and falls out; size pockets 0.2-0.3 mm over, use suitable glue (not hot glue), or seal magnets in with a mid-print pause. (3) Printed strap bars or catches crack; print them thicker, with 100% infill, and in a tougher material (PETG or nylon). (5) The magnets weaken after overheating or corrosion, so it no longer closes by itself (K&J Magnetics). (6) It is used beyond its rating (such as on a leash) and the catch breaks; respect the maker's load and use limits. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **related**: 
  - Side-release buckle: the ordinary plastic backpack clip; two springy arms must be pinched together to release; no magnets, so you must line it up and push it home yourself
  - Magnetic snap or magnetic clasp: held by the magnets alone, with no catch; very easy to use but slides apart easily under sideways load
  - Cantilever snap fit: the springy-hook principle often used for the catch inside a magnetic buckle
  - Ladder lock buckle / tri-glide: the strap-length adjuster often built into one half
  - Hidden latches (magnetic-key latch): also use magnets, but to move a hidden catch rather than to pull two halves together
  - Mid-print embedding: the technique for sealing magnets inside a printed part
  - Holding electromagnet (maglock): a powered magnet that holds by magnetic force alone and lets go when power is cut
  - Seat-belt style centre-release buckle: metal tongue and push button, for safety-rated loads
