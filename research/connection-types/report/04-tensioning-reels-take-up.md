## 1 Ratcheting reel closure (dial lacing system)

*tensioning, reels & take-up.* A knob that winds a lace or cable onto a spool to pull two sides together, clicks as it tightens, stays locked, and releases only when you pull or turn the knob by hand (as on snowboard boots and cycling shoes)

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A turn-to-tighten knob that winds a thin lace onto a small hidden reel, clicks as it tightens, holds tight by itself, and lets go only when you pull the knob out or turn it back by hand
- **term**: Reel-based closure (ratcheting reel lacing system, dial lacing system)
- **aliases**: 
  - dial lacing system
  - reel lacing / reel-based lacing system
  - reel-based closure system
  - rotary closure
  - ratchet dial closure
  - lace reel
  - cable reel closure
  - micro-adjust dial
  - ratchet dial fit adjuster (on helmets)
  - ratcheting shoelace tensioner (maker term)
  - BOA (brand), BOA Fit System (brand), 'Boa laces' / 'Boa dial' (brand used as a generic name)
- **family**: tensioning, reels & take-up
- **other_families**: one-way & ratchet, cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware, quick-release & tool-free, self-locking & anti-back-drive
- **one_liner**: A knob on a small reel that you turn to wind in a thin lace and pull two sides of a shoe, boot, brace or bag together; springy catches (pawls) click over ratchet teeth so it cannot unwind by itself, and it releases when you pull the knob up or turn it backwards.
- **complexity**: A small assembly that acts as one unit: base/housing with a ring of ratchet teeth and lace holes, a spool (reel) that the lace winds onto, 2-3 springy catches (pawls, often flexible plastic arms moulded onto the spool or knob), a knob (dial) with drive lugs, a centre screw or clip, the lace itself (coated steel cable, polymer cable or cord) and low-friction lace guides on the far side of the opening.
- **how_it_works**: Turning the knob turns the spool, which winds the lace in through holes in the housing and pulls the two sides of the opening together (rotary motion turned into a straight pull). Springy catches (pawls) ride over sloped ratchet teeth inside the housing, which makes the clicks; when you stop, lace tension tries to turn the spool back but the catches drop into the teeth and block it, so it stays tight with no effort (a one-way ratchet). To loosen, some knobs are lifted or pulled out, which uncouples the spool so the lace pays out all at once; others turn backwards, and ramps on the knob push the catches out of the teeth one click at a time (incremental release). In the patented design examined, three flexible pawl arms on the spool sit in about 33 triangular notches in the housing wall.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: winds in a lace and keeps it tight
  - stop it moving back (one-way): the ratchet stops the lace unwinding by itself
  - lock, and release by hand: stays locked under load until you pull the knob up or turn it back
  - join and hold: pulls two edges of a shoe, boot, brace, helmet cradle or bag together
- **motion**: multiple: the knob and spool rotate (unlimited turns, one way only while locked) and the lace slides through its guides as it winds in; pull-to-release knobs also move a few millimetres along their axis to unlock
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1 in normal use: the spool turns about one axis and the lace travel is tied to that turn. Pull-to-release knobs add a second, on/off axial movement that uncouples the spool.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): each turn winds more lace onto the spool
  - one-way (moves freely one way, locks the other): turns freely to tighten, blocked from unwinding by the pawls
  - hand-release only: lace tension cannot open it; only a person pulling the knob or turning it back releases it
  - self-locking (won't back-drive): the load on the lace cannot turn the knob backwards
  - indexing (clicks into set positions): tension changes in small repeatable click steps
  - quick-release: pull-to-release knobs dump all the tension at once
  - motion conversion (rotary to linear): turning the knob becomes a straight pull on the lace
  - torque-limiting (some designs only): patented versions add a slipping clutch so the lace cannot be over-tightened, e.g. for medical braces
- **holding_principle**: shape (the pawls interlock with the ratchet teeth; form-fit), with spring force (the pawls' own springiness) keeping them engaged
- **input_output**: Input: rotation of the knob -> output: straight-line pull on the lace (rotary to linear). Lace taken in per knob turn = spool circumference; per click = spool circumference / number of ratchet teeth (about 33 teeth in one patented design). Knob and spool are in line (coaxial). Back-drivable: no, lace tension cannot unwind the spool while the pawls are engaged. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **power**: passive (no power needed). Motorised 'powered lacing' shoes exist but are a different type (see related).
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: the lace is released by hand without tools (pull the knob or turn it back); the reel housing itself is sewn, riveted or screwed onto the item and is semi-permanent
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: tightening and releasing is its everyday job; the lace is the wearing part and is replaced when it frays (repair kits exist), while the dial is covered by a lifetime guarantee on the product it is fitted to according to one retailer
- **tools_needed**: none to use. Fitting: needle and thread, rivets or small screws (M3 screw and nut or heat-set insert on printed versions). Lace replacement: small pliers and a replacement lace kit
- **seal**: none (it closes an opening in a shoe, brace or bag but does not seal anything)
- **load**: light to medium. Enough for shoes, boots, braces, helmets and bag compression straps. A maker's fully 3D-printed PLA reel held about 45 lb (about 200 N) in a weight test. Strong along the lace pull (taken by the pawls in the teeth); weak where the lace rubs over a sharp edge (it frays and snaps) and, on pull-to-release knobs, against an accidental upward knock of the knob. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **adjustable**: yes: tension can be changed at any time in small click steps. Two-way (bi-directional) dials loosen one click at a time by turning back; pull-to-release dials release all at once and must be re-tightened.
- **play_and_precision**: small: tension can only be set to the nearest tooth (one click) and there is a little springback as the pawl settles into its notch. Repeatable enough that the same number of clicks gives much the same fit. Tolerates plenty of misalignment because the lace is flexible and runs through guides; the knob itself must turn on its own axis.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): operated from the outside; the base is fixed to the outer surface, and the lace is routed through guides on the item
- **materials**: Lace: stranded stainless steel cable (e.g. 7x7) coated with a slippery plastic such as nylon or PTFE, single-strand nitinol wire, multi-strand polymer cable, or textile laces. Guides: low-friction polymer or metal tubes. Maker versions: PLA or PETG/ABS/ASA for the body, PA12 (nylon) for the springy click part, 0.8-1 mm UHMWPE (Dyneema) or UV-resistant cord for the lace. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate: fully printable designs exist and work, but they need fine layers, a tough springy material for the pawls (nylon/PA12 or PETG), a strong lace anchored inside the spool and protection where the cord rubs; buying a replacement dial and lace kit is easier for footwear

### Making it

- **processes**: 3D print (FDM): the whole reel can be printed; pawls/click ring in PA12 or PETG, off-the-shelf part: replacement reel and lace kits, injection moulding (how commercial reels are made; not a home process)
- **print_in_place**: no: printed as separate parts (base, spool, click/pawl ring, spacer, spring, knob) and assembled with a centre M3 screw; some designs glue the pawls in separately for strength
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Proven printed designs: (1) Noel Swede's 'BOA style ratchet' (Printables 818537, CC BY) is fully printed in PLA, held about 45 lb in a weight test, and winds over 40 inches of 0.8 mm Dyneema (UHMWPE) cord. It needs a 0.4 mm nozzle and 0.2 mm or 0.1 mm layers (the author warns not to use 0.25 mm). The pawls are printed separately and glued in to make them stronger. The cap is held by an M3x10 screw in a heat-set insert, and pieces of 1/16 in welding wire act as detents that keep the cap down and as wear strips where the cord rubs over an edge. (2) MakerT's 'One-Hand Ratchet Lace Closure' (Printables 1743611, CC BY) turns to tighten and pulls to release. It was tested with a PA12 (nylon) click mechanism, PLA for the other parts and 1 mm UV-resistant cord. Recommended settings: 0.16 mm layers, 3-4 perimeters, 20-40% infill, Arachne on, supports only where needed. PETG, ABS or ASA are suggested for better durability. The click part is glued into its grooves one lip at a time with CA glue, so test that the glue works on your filament first. The lace is knotted and mechanically anchored inside the spool, so it does not rely on friction. Check that the M3 screw slides freely through the spacer and base and only threads into its captured nut. General advice: fine teeth and a small spool give finer adjustment. A prosthetic-hand builder found that only 5-6 clicks over the full range was too coarse, and that the cable bound up when released under tension. The fix was a larger ratchet wheel with more teeth and a smaller pull radius. Put the springy pawls in a tough material (PA12/PETG) rather than PLA, which Prusa describes as breaking into shards on impact. PLA also softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV, so use PETG (fine below about 80 °C) or ASA for footwear and outdoor gear. Round off or reinforce every edge the lace passes over.
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Replacement dial/reel kits (search 'lace reel replacement kit', 'dial lacing replacement kit', 'BOA replacement kit' (brand)); coated stainless-steel lace cable; lace guides; UHMWPE (Dyneema) or braided polyester cord 0.8-1 mm; *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Lace pulled in per click = spool circumference / number of ratchet teeth; one patented reel uses about 33 triangular notches (about 100 degree notch angle) and three flexible pawl arms (Boa Technology patent US8468657B2, https://patents.google.com/patent/US8468657B2/en). Worked example, illustrative only: a 10 mm spool with 33 teeth gives about 1 mm of lace per click.
  - Too few clicks over the working range makes fine adjustment impossible: a printed tendon ratchet with 5-6 clicks from open to closed was too coarse, and the fix was more teeth and a smaller pull radius (https://hackaday.io/project/162231-hintha-modular-prosthetic-hand/log/156107-prototype-version-01-review-part-2-ratchet-system).
  - Use a slippery lace in slippery guides: the patent examples use stranded stainless-steel cable (e.g. 7x7) coated with nylon or PTFE, running through tube-like guides of lubricious polymer or metal (https://patents.google.com/patent/US20120246974A1/en).
  - Make the knob need a push to engage before it turns, so that it is not knocked loose by accident during wear (https://www.snowandrock.com/expert-advice-and-inspiration/buying-guides/what-is-the-boa-fit-system.html).
  - Where people can over-tighten (for example braces for diabetic patients), add a tension-limiting slip clutch to the reel (Boa Technology patent US9629417B2, https://patents.google.com/patent/US9629417B2/en).
  - A printed PLA reel with 0.8 mm Dyneema held about 45 lb (about 200 N); reinforce where the cord rubs, using metal wire as in that design (https://www.printables.com/model/818537-boa-style-ratchet, read via the Printables API).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - snowboard boots with one or two dials (one for the shin, one for the forefoot)
  - cycling and golf shoes with a dial instead of laces
  - medical braces and orthoses with a dial
  - bike helmet fit dials at the back of the head, which use the same click-and-hold ratchet idea
- **when_to_use**: When you want quick, even, repeatable tightening with one hand and no knots, and release in one movement: shoes, boots, braces, splints, helmet cradles, prosthetic sockets, bag and backpack compression, or tying down a bundle. Also good for fine tension adjustment of a cord over a long travel, such as winding up a tendon in a printed prosthetic hand. Useful for people who find tying laces hard (a maker designed a printed version for one-handed use).
- **when_not_to_use**: Not for heavy or safety-critical loads such as lifting, tie-downs, climbing or load securing: use a ratchet strap, winch or rated hardware instead. Not where the lace would rub on sharp or abrasive edges (snowboard edges fray steel laces), unless you add guides. Not where an accidental knock on a pull-to-release knob would be dangerous; choose a turn-back (incremental) release or a latch. For a simple fixed-tension closure, a buckle, cam cleat or cord lock is simpler. If the tension must never exceed a limit (medical use), use a reel with a built-in tension limiter.
- **environment_limits**: Printed reels: PLA softens and deforms above about 60 °C (a hot car or a boot by a heater) and degrades in UV light, while PETG handles up to about 80 °C and outdoor use (Prusa material guides). Cord: one maker chose UV-resistant 1 mm cord for shoes. Steel laces fray and snap when a sharp steel edge rubs them, for example a snowboard edge resting on the boot on a chairlift. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Over-tightening can cut off blood flow. A reel patent points out that for diabetic patients in braces this can lead to serious complications, which is why tension-limiting reels exist. Pull-to-release knobs can release suddenly if caught on something, so don't use them where a sudden release is dangerous. Keep fingers out of the lace path while winding. Small parts are a choking risk for young children. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: (1) The lace frays and snaps, mostly where it rubs a sharp edge; carry a spare lace kit, as reels are designed to be serviced. (2) The pawls wear, crack or lose their spring, so the knob slips back or will not click; on printed versions make the pawls from nylon/PETG and glue them in as separate parts. (3) The knob pops off or releases by accident; use push-to-engage knobs, detents or a screw-held cap. (4) Coarse adjustment (too few clicks) or binding when released under load; use more, finer teeth and a smaller spool radius. (5) The lace slips at its anchor; anchor it mechanically inside the spool (knot in a pocket) rather than by friction. (6) Guides with high friction give uneven tension; use low-friction guides and coated lace.
- **related**: 
  - Ratchet and pawl: the basic one-way mechanism inside the reel, without the spool and lace
  - Ratchet strap / linear ratchet (cable tie): also tightens and holds, but the ratchet acts on a strap or toothed strip, not on a wound lace
  - Winch or capstan with pawl: the same wind-up-and-hold idea at much higher loads
  - Spring-return reel (constant-force spring reel): winds cord back in automatically instead of holding it locked
  - Cam cleat / cord lock: holds a cord at any position by pinching it, with no winding or clicking
  - Friction slip clutch / ratchet-tooth torque limiter: some reels include one so they cannot be over-tightened
  - Quick-lace pull cord with locking slider (as on some snowboard boots): pull by hand and lock, with no dial
  - Motorised (powered) self-lacing shoes: a motor does the winding

## 128 Belt tensioner (spring-loaded idler)

*tensioning, reels & take-up.* A pulley or roller pushed against a belt or chain by a spring or screw to keep it tight as it stretches

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A little wheel on a springy arm (or on a sliding bracket) that presses on the side of a belt or chain to keep it tight as it stretches
- **term**: Belt tensioner (spring-loaded idler; automatic tensioner)
- **aliases**: 
  - belt tensioner / chain tensioner
  - idler pulley / idler sprocket / idler wheel (a pulley that carries no load)
  - tensioner pulley / tensioner arm
  - jockey pulley / jockey wheel
  - belt tightener / tightening pulley (older name)
  - automatic tensioner / auto-tensioner / spring-actuated idler
  - rotary tensioner (spring acts through a pivoting arm) and linear tensioner (idler on a spring-loaded plunger)
  - inside idler / backside (outside) idler (named by which face of the belt it presses)
  - singlespeed chain tensioner (bicycles)
  - Kettenspanner (German, chain tensioner) *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **family**: tensioning, reels & take-up
- **other_families**: springs & energy storage, gear trains, gearboxes & transmissions, clutches & couplings
- **one_liner**: A free-spinning pulley, sprocket or plastic shoe pushed against the slack side of a belt or chain by a spring (or set by a screw) so the drive stays snug as the belt or chain stretches and wears.
- **complexity**: A small assembly that acts as one unit: an idler pulley, sprocket or plastic wear shoe on a ball bearing; an arm that pivots on a bolt or bushing (rotary type) or a plunger or slide (linear type); a spring (coil, torsion or compression) or an adjusting screw; and a base or bracket bolted to the frame. Car engine tensioners also have a damper; manual versions have slots and clamp bolts instead of a spring.
- **how_it_works**: A belt or chain only grips and runs quietly if it is kept tight, but it stretches in use. The tensioner's wheel runs on the belt's slack (unloaded) side and a spring keeps pushing it in, so as the belt gets longer the arm simply moves further and the tension stays roughly the same, with no need to move the motor or shafts. Because the idler only rolls with the belt, it passes on no power. A screw-set (fixed) idler works the same way, but you push it in by hand, lock it, and re-adjust it later as the belt stretches.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: keeps a belt or chain snug as it stretches, wears and warms up
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): the idler spins with the belt, and the arm swings or slides to follow the slack
- **motion**: multiple: the idler rotates continuously with the belt; the arm pivots (rotary tensioner) or slides (linear tensioner) through a short range as the belt stretches
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 2: the idler spins on its bearing (1) and the spring-loaded arm swings or slides to take up slack (1). A screw-set idler has only the spin (1) once its bracket is clamped.
- **special_behaviour**: tensioning (takes up slack and holds): the spring keeps pushing the idler into the belt, so tension is kept up automatically as the belt stretches; screw-set versions hold a tension you set by hand
- **holding_principle**: spring force (a spring presses the idler against the belt); screw-set versions hold by friction or clamping (bolts clamp the bracket in its slot). Otherwise it is not a holding joint: it passes on motion.
- **input_output**: Not a transmission: the idler turns freely with the belt and passes on no power. It keeps the belt gripping the working pulleys; slack V-belts slip, which can cut drive efficiency by as much as 10% (Machine Design). Shaft arrangement: the idler axle is parallel to the drive shafts. Back-drivable: yes; a spring-loaded arm gives way if the belt on its side goes tight (for example when a drive reverses, brakes or is back-pedalled), which is why spring tensioners are not used on reversing drives.
- **power**: passive (no power needed): a spring or weight does the work. Some engine timing drives use hydraulic tensioners instead (Wikipedia 'Tensioner').
- **removability**: removable: bolted to the frame or engine; to change a belt you swing the arm back against its spring or slacken its bolts
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: the tensioner can be released and reset at every belt change, but its spring, pivot bushing and bearing wear out, so car tensioners are often replaced along with the belt
- **tools_needed**: spanners, sockets or Allen keys to mount it and to swing or lock the arm; for screw-set idlers, a way to measure belt tension (Gates suggests the force-deflection method or a sonic tension meter). Bicycle tensioners fit the derailleur hanger with an Allen key.
- **seal**: none (bought idler pulleys usually have sealed ball bearings, but the tensioner does not seal anything)
- **load**: light to heavy depending on size: light for 3D-printer GT2 belts, medium for bicycle chains and small machines, heavy for car serpentine belts and industrial drives. Strong in the direction it pushes (into the slack span); weak against a load that pushes it back out, because only the spring resists: if its side of the belt becomes the tight side the spring force may be 'inadequate to maintain proper tension' (Machine Design).
- **adjustable**: yes: spring-loaded versions adjust themselves as the belt stretches, and some let you set the spring's preload with a screw; screw-set idlers are moved along a slot or pivoted and then clamped (Gates' adjustable idler brackets can do both)
- **play_and_precision**: Noticeable springiness: the spring lets the idler move, so the belt span is less stiff and its tension varies a little with load. Fine for passing on power, but it can let a toothed-belt positioning drive shift or skip; Gates advises that on synchronous drives 'All idlers should be rigidly mounted in place to minimize movement or deflection'. Flat idlers let a belt wander slightly sideways (Bestorq), and a chain tensioner must line up with the chain line.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): usually bolted to one face of a frame, bracket or engine; you need room to reach the arm to release it when fitting a belt
- **materials**: Arms and brackets: steel, cast iron or aluminium; idler pulleys: steel, aluminium or plastic (nylon or acetal) running on ball bearings; chain tensioners may use a sprocket or a low-friction polymer wear shoe (Machine Design); springs: steel coil or torsion springs. 3D-printed arms and brackets: ASA, ABS, PETG or nylon, not PLA near motors or heated chambers, used with a bought bearing or GT2 idler.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to moderate: easy with a bought idler pulley or bearing and a spring on a printed or sheet-metal arm; moderate to get the spring force, alignment and placement (slack side) right; car and industrial automatic tensioners should be bought

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part (idler pulleys, idler sprockets, automatic tensioners, bicycle chain tensioners, adjustable idler brackets)
  - 3D print (FDM): arms, brackets and sliders, with a bought bearing or idler pulley, a pivot bolt and a spring
  - sheet metal or CNC: brackets and arms *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **print_in_place**: no: printed arms or brackets are assembled with a bought ball bearing or idler pulley, a pivot bolt and a spring (or an adjusting screw)
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Buy the rolling part: use a toothed idler (e.g. a GT2 20-tooth idler) or bearing-based pulley inside the belt and a smooth, flat, uncrowned idler on the belt's back (Gates GT3 manual). Size it: a flat idler on the back of the belt should be at least 1.5 times the small pulley's diameter and at least the belt's minimum (e.g. 0.3 in for MXL, 0.6 in for 3M, 0.8 in for XL belts); an inside idler should be about the size of the smallest pulley (Bestorq). Place it on the slack side, preferably inside the belt, giving nearly equal wrap on both pulleys (Machine Design, Bestorq). Material: 3D-printer tensioner designers say 'DO NOT PRINT OUT OF PLA' and use ASA, ABS, nylon or PETG, especially near a hot motor or heated chamber, and one recommends at least 2 mm walls or 100% infill to reduce creep (Printables 1066464 and 1702885). Pivot: make the pivot hole a close fit on a shoulder bolt; one designer tightened his after the first version was sloppy (Printables 437104). Springs: salvage a rear-derailleur spring for a bike chain tensioner (Printables 437104) or reuse a printer's stock springs in a screw-adjustable holder (Printables 1066464, 1702885). A spring-loaded belt on a fast printer is softer than a locked one; one engineer who locked his spring tensioners with a screw raised the axes' resonance frequencies by a few hertz. Take care when removing stock spring tensioners: 'things can go flying fast and far'.
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search terms: 'idler pulley' (flat, flanged or V-groove), 'GT2 idler pulley 20T 3 mm or 5 mm bore' (toothed or smooth), 'idler sprocket', 'belt tensioner', 'automatic belt tensioner' or 'serpentine belt tensioner' (car), 'spring-loaded tensioner arm', 'rotary tensioner' and 'linear chain tensioner' (with polymer shoe), 'singlespeed chain tensioner' (bicycle; fits the derailleur hanger), 'adjustable idler bracket' (Gates, brand), 'torsion spring', 'belt tension meter' (force-deflection or sonic). Idler pulleys usually have sealed deep-groove ball bearings (e.g. 6304-6310 on Gates industrial idlers; *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Put the tensioner on the slack (unloaded) side, on the inside of a belt, placed so both pulleys get nearly equal wrap; on a chain, put the idler outside the loop on the slack side, midway between the sprockets (Machine Design, 'Belt and chain tensioners', https://www.machinedesign.com/archive/article/21828669/belt-and-chain-tensioners).
  - Order of preference for idler position: inside slack side, outside slack side, inside tight side, outside tight side; the matching service-factor add-ons are 0.0, 0.1, 0.1 and 0.2 (Bestorq 'Idlers', https://www.bestorq.com/wp-content/uploads/Idlers.pdf).
  - Never use a spring-loaded tensioner on a reversing drive, because the slack side becomes the tight side (Machine Design; Bestorq). On bicycles, chain tensioners 'must not be used with fixed gears or coaster brakes' (Sheldon Brown, https://www.sheldonbrown.com/no-derailers.html).
  - Automatic tensioners are 'not recommended for use on synchronous belts unless the drive is intended strictly for motion transfer' (Machine Design); Gates says idlers on synchronous drives should be on the slack side and 'rigidly mounted' (Gates PowerGrip GT3 Drive Design Manual, section 12 'Use of Idlers').
  - A flat idler on the back of the belt: at least 1.5 x the small pulley's diameter, placed close to the small pulley to increase its wrap; minimum flat outside idler diameters include MXL 0.3 in, XL 0.8 in, 3M 0.6 in, 5M 1.1 in, 8M 2.9 in and A-section V-belt 4.3 in. An inside idler should be the diameter of the smallest pulley and should not be flanged (Bestorq).
  - Gates minimum idler sizes for 5M PowerGrip GT3: inside grooved 14 grooves, inside flat 2.50 in OD, backside 1.25 in OD; for XL timing belts: 12 grooves, 2.50 in and 1.00 in OD (Gates PowerGrip GT3 Drive Design Manual, idler size table, https://www.gatesaustralia.com.au/~/media/files/gates-au/industrial/catalogues/powergrip-gt3-design-manual-us-january-2014.pdf).
  - Flat idler width: a flanged one about 15% wider than the pulley face, an unflanged one at least 25% wider; flange height at least 25% of belt thickness (Bestorq).
  - Chains: slack should not exceed 4% of the span (under 2% for heavy loads or frequent starts and stops); a span of more than 50 chain pitches typically needs a tensioner; an idler sprocket should have at least three teeth engaged (Machine Design).
  - Adjustable shaft centres are usually preferred over idlers, because every idler adds a bending cycle that shortens belt life (Bestorq; Gates: use idlers only when 'functionally necessary').

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - the spring-loaded arm and pulley pressing on a car engine's serpentine belt
  - a single-speed or internal-gear bicycle's chain tensioner (and a rear derailleur, which is also a spring-loaded chain tensioner)
  - a belt sander's spring-loaded idler drum that keeps the sanding belt tight
  - spring or screw belt tensioners on 3D printers
- **when_to_use**: When the shaft positions cannot be moved to tighten a belt or chain (fixed motor mounts, vertical bicycle dropouts, several accessories on one engine belt), when the belt or chain will stretch or wear and you want tension kept up automatically, to damp whip on long spans, to route a belt around an obstruction or increase wrap on a small pulley, or to make fitting a new belt easy. Spring tensioners suit one-direction power drives (engines, fans, pumps, conveyors, bike freewheels); screw-set idlers suit drives that need a fixed, stiff tension.
- **when_not_to_use**: Not on drives that reverse, brake through the belt or are back-pedalled (fixed-gear bikes, coaster brakes, reversing machines): the spring gives way when the belt on its side goes tight, so use adjustable centres (sliding motor mount, horizontal dropouts, eccentric bottom bracket) or a locked (screw-set) idler instead. Not for precise toothed-belt positioning drives (CNC, 3D printers) where stiffness matters: use a rigidly mounted idler or adjustable centres. Avoid an idler altogether if the shaft centres can be adjusted, because idlers add bending and shorten belt life; avoid backside idlers on V-belts where possible, because reverse bending is especially bad for them (Bestorq).
- **environment_limits**: Heat: springs lose tension over time when exposed to heat (Brake & Front End). Rust, corrosion, dirt or mud can jam the pivot so the arm cannot follow the belt; road salt is a common cause (Brake & Front End). Debris packed into toothed-belt pulleys causes bad tooth engagement, so shield synchronous drives in dirty places (Gates GT3 manual). Temperature changes: install timing-belt tensioners with the engine and tensioner at the same room temperature, as tension set hot or cold will be wrong (Litens installation procedure). 3D-printed arms creep under constant spring load, especially PLA near heat; use ASA, ABS, nylon or PETG and thick walls (Printables 1066464, 1702885).
- **safety_notes**: Belts meeting pulleys and chains meeting sprockets form in-running nip points that can pull in fingers, hair or clothing; guard them (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.212 lists 'ingoing nip points' among hazards that need guarding). The spring stores energy: when removing or releasing a tensioner, hold the arm with a tool, because parts can 'go flying fast and far' (Printables 1066464). Never turn a drive by hand with fingers near the belt and idler. If the worn bushing in a car tensioner pulley seizes it may snap the belt, and a car whose single serpentine belt breaks instantly loses several critical functions at once (Brake & Front End; Wikipedia 'Serpentine belt').
- **common_failures**: (1) A weak or broken spring lets the belt slip, glaze and squeal; replace the spring or tensioner. (2) A worn pivot bushing lets the arm rock, causing belt flutter, noise and misalignment. (3) Worn idler bearings make the pulley wobble or growl, and a worn pulley bushing causes vibration and noise; if the bushing seizes it may snap the belt (all Brake & Front End). (4) The tensioner is on the tight side, or the drive reverses, so the spring is overpowered and the belt jumps teeth or the chain falls off; move it to the slack side or use a locked idler. (5) An idler that is too small, or a backside idler on a V-belt, shortens belt life through extra or reverse bending; follow minimum diameters. (6) A misaligned or crowned idler lets the belt walk off; align it and use flat, uncrowned backside idlers. (7) A printed arm cracks or creeps; print thicker, denser and in a heat-resistant plastic.
- **related**: 
  - Belt drives (timing, round and V-belt): the drives a tensioner serves
  - Eccentric adjuster: tensions a belt or chain by turning an off-centre bushing and clamping it; set once, not automatic
  - Adjustable shaft centres (slotted motor mount, jacking screw, horizontal dropouts): move a working pulley instead of adding an idler; preferred where possible
  - Hydraulic tensioner: an oil-damped plunger used on engine timing chains and belts
  - Rear derailleur: a spring-loaded chain tensioner that also shifts the chain between sprockets
  - Belt-tightener clutch: an idler pressed in by a lever to engage a slack belt drive and released to stop it (507 Mechanical Movements no. 5)
  - Constant-force spring (spring-return reel): another way to keep a steady pull on a cord or tape
  - Turnbuckle: tensions a rod, wire or cable by screwing, with no rolling idler

## 129 Eccentric adjuster (eccentric nut, spacer or bushing)

*tensioning, reels & take-up.* An off-centre spacer or bushing that moves a wheel, pulley or shaft sideways as you turn it, for setting tension or taking up play

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A round spacer, nut or sleeve with its hole drilled off-centre: turn it with a spanner and the wheel, pulley or shaft riding on it shifts a little sideways, then clamp it so it stays put
- **term**: Eccentric adjuster (eccentric spacer, eccentric bushing or eccentric cam bolt)
- **aliases**: 
  - eccentric nut (common 3D-printer name, e.g. on V-wheel carriages)
  - eccentric spacer (V-slot and OpenBuilds systems)
  - eccentric bushing / eccentric bush / offset bushing / eccentric sleeve
  - eccentric bottom bracket, EBB (bicycles) / 'the eccentric' (tandems)
  - eccentric hub (bicycle rear hubs; motorcycles with single-sided swingarms)
  - cam bolt / camber bolt / eccentric bolt with eccentric or offset washer (car wheel alignment)
  - eccentric stud cam follower / eccentric type cam follower (bearing catalogues)
  - cam height adjustment (cabinet hinge mounting plates)
  - eccentric-mounted tensioner pulley (engine timing belts)
  - Exzenter (German)
- **family**: tensioning, reels & take-up
- **other_families**: locating & alignment, clamping, magnetic & friction connections, sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **one_liner**: A spacer, bushing or bolt whose hole is off-centre, so turning it moves the part mounted on it sideways by up to twice the offset; you turn it until a belt, chain, wheel or gear is just right and then clamp it to lock the setting.
- **complexity**: A few parts: the eccentric itself (a round or hex-headed spacer, sleeve, plug or bolt with an off-centre bore or shank, often marked on its thin side); the axle, bolt or bearing that runs through the off-centre hole; the part being adjusted (a wheel, pulley, sprocket, bottom bracket or gear); and a clamp to lock it (a nut, pinch bolts, set screws or a wedge). Example: 'eccentric spacer, M5 bolt, V-wheel, lock nut'.
- **how_it_works**: The eccentric sits in a round hole or on a bolt and can turn about its own centre, but the part it carries sits in the off-centre hole, so as you turn it the carried part travels around a small circle whose radius is the offset (eccentricity). Half a turn takes it from one side to the other, giving a total sideways shift of twice the offset; turning further brings it back again, so unlike a normal nut it never gets 'tighter and tighter'. Once the belt, chain or wheel is at the right tightness you clamp the eccentric (nut, pinch bolt, set screws or wedge) so friction stops it turning. Because the carried part moves on an arc, it also moves slightly at right angles to the direction you want, for example an eccentric bottom bracket also changes the pedal height.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: takes up slack in a chain or belt, or presses rollers onto a rail
  - line parts up exactly: sets a wheel, bearing, gear or hinge to the right position and takes up play
  - join and hold: once clamped it is also the mounting for the axle or bolt it carries
- **motion**: rotate: you turn the eccentric through up to half a turn (180°) while adjusting, which shifts the carried part sideways along a short arc; none once it is clamped
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 once clamped. While adjusting, 1: turning the eccentric, which moves the carried part along an arc (mostly sideways, a little up or down).
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): set once and clamped; it does not re-adjust itself as a belt or chain stretches (unlike a spring tensioner), so it must be reset as parts wear
  - motion conversion (e.g. rotary to linear): a turn of the eccentric becomes a small, finely controllable sideways movement
- **holding_principle**: friction or clamping (force-fit): a nut, pinch bolt, set screws or a wedge clamps the eccentric so it cannot turn; plus shape (form-fit): the off-centre bore holds the axle or bolt
- **input_output**: An adjuster, not a drive: a rotation of the eccentric (up to 180°) gives a sideways shift of up to twice the offset, e.g. about 0.79 mm in total for an OpenBuilds V-slot eccentric spacer, and 0.25-1.5 mm eccentricity on catalogue eccentric cam followers. The ratio is not constant: by geometry, the shift per degree is largest in the middle of the range and smallest near the two ends. Back-drivable: yes when unclamped, because a sideways load on the carried part tries to turn the eccentric; clamped, it is held only by friction, so under-tightened eccentrics can slip.
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: removable: unclamp, then slide off or unscrew; set screws can dent the eccentric so it is harder to re-set finely
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: metal eccentrics can be re-adjusted and re-used indefinitely if not corroded; printed eccentrics wear and can break at thin sections
- **tools_needed**: a spanner to turn it (10 mm for full-size OpenBuilds spacers and Ender 3 eccentric nuts, 8 mm for 'mini' spacers), plus a spanner or Allen key for the lock nut, ideally holding the eccentric with one tool while tightening the nut with another; a pin spanner (e.g. Park Tool SPA-1) for bicycle eccentric bottom brackets; a special hook spanner for motorcycle eccentric hubs; a screwdriver for cabinet hinge cams
- **seal**: none
- **load**: light to heavy depending on size: V-slot wheel spacers and hinge cams are light-duty; bicycle eccentric bottom brackets, motorcycle eccentric hubs, car camber bolts and bearing cam followers carry heavy loads (catalogue eccentric cam followers have static load ratings of kilonewtons). Strong in straight shear across the bolt or axle; the weak direction is rotation: a load pushing the carried part sideways makes a turning force on the eccentric that only the clamp's friction resists, which is why under-clamped eccentrics creep, creak or slip.
- **adjustable**: yes: that is its purpose; loosen the clamp, turn the eccentric to shift the part (total range twice the offset), and re-clamp
- **play_and_precision**: none once clamped: it is used to remove play (e.g. between V-wheels and a rail, or in a chain). Very fine adjustment, down to a fraction of a millimetre, especially near the ends of its range. Tolerates only small misalignment corrections (at most twice the offset, typically well under a few millimetres, except large bicycle and motorcycle eccentrics). The carried part moves on an arc, so there is a small unwanted shift at right angles.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind) in most cases: V-slot spacers, cam bolts and hinge cams are turned from the head side; bicycle eccentric bottom brackets and motorcycle eccentric hubs are reached from the side of the frame or swingarm
- **materials**: Stainless steel (OpenBuilds eccentric spacers), steel (car cam bolts, cam-follower studs, cabinet hinge plates), aluminium or steel (bicycle eccentric bottom brackets and hubs), bronze or steel (industrial eccentric bushes). 3D-printed eccentrics in PETG or similar for light, low-stress uses, always with a metal bolt through them. The part around it can be metal, printed plastic, acrylic or wood; a metal eccentric spacer's collar needs a round hole of about 7.1 mm.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to use bought ones (V-slot eccentric spacers are cheap and fit a round hole in a printed or cut plate); moderate to make your own: the off-centre hole must be accurate, which needs a lathe or careful printing, and printed ones are weak

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part (most common: eccentric spacers, eccentric bushings, eccentric cam followers, eccentric bottom brackets, cam bolts)
  - 3D print (FDM): design round pockets in printed plates to take bought eccentric spacers, or print light-duty eccentric spacers or bushings
  - CNC or lathe: turning an off-centre bore (metal eccentric bushings)
  - laser cut: round holes in acrylic or plywood gantry plates to take eccentric spacers
- **print_in_place**: no: the eccentric is a separate part fitted onto a bolt or into a hole; a printed eccentric still needs a metal bolt and nut
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Easiest: print or cut the gantry plate or carriage with round holes that fit bought metal eccentric spacers (OpenBuilds spacers have a collar that 'fits into a 7.12mm hole'; one maker drilled acrylic plates to 7.1 mm), and use them on only one side of each set of wheels, with plain spacers on the other side. Print a hole-size test gauge first to find the right printed hole for M5 bolts and 7 mm eccentric collars on your printer (Printables 466199). If you print the eccentric spacer itself, keep it for light duty: one designer notes that the neck of his printed long eccentric spacer 'is a bit weak so the spacer shouldn't be turned under the stress' (Printables 79881). Mark the thin side of a home-made eccentric as OpenBuilds does with a divot, so you always know where the offset points. Leave room for a spanner (or print a long eccentric-nut wrench, Printables 890104). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search terms: 'eccentric spacer' (V-slot, 5 mm bore, 6 mm or 1/4 in tall, 8 mm or 10 mm hex), 'eccentric nut' (3D printer V-wheels), 'eccentric bushing' or 'eccentric bush', 'eccentric cam follower' or 'eccentric stud cam follower' (e.g. IKO CFE and CFES, THK CFH-A and CFH-AB series; brands), 'eccentric bottom bracket' (bicycles), 'eccentric hub' (singlespeed bicycles with vertical dropouts), 'camber bolt' or 'cam bolt kit' (cars), 'cam adjustable mounting plate' (cabinet hinges, e.g. Blum CLIP top, brand). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Total adjustment is twice the eccentricity: half a turn moves the carried part from one side to the other (geometry). Examples: an OpenBuilds eccentric spacer gives 'a total range of adjustment of approximately 0.79mm' (MakerTechStore, https://www.makertechstore.com/products/eccentric-spacer); THK eccentric cam followers have 0.25-1.0 mm eccentricity (https://www.thk.com/jp/en/products/other_rotary_guides/cam_follower/eccentric/); IKO CFE 0.4-1.5 mm (https://www.ikont.co.jp/eg/product/needle/ndl0603.html); IKO CFES 0.25-0.6 mm (https://www.mrosupply.com/bearings/862859_cfes16_iko-bearing/).
  - V-wheel carriages: turn eccentric nuts in very small steps until the wheels roll smoothly and can easily be pushed both ways but the carriage never wobbles; too tight and the wheels grind and damage themselves and the rail (3D Print Beast, https://www.3dprintbeast.com/eccentric-nuts-ender-3-v2-pro/). Over-tight V-wheels develop flat spots (FlixMySwitch video title, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I6NI7lGAv44).
  - Tandem front chain set with an eccentric: about 1/2 inch (12 mm) of up-and-down play halfway between the axles, checked all the way round the crank rotation (Park Tool, https://www.parktool.com/en-int/blog/repair-help/chain-tension-for-tandem-front-end); for a synchronising chain, set it taut at the tightest point of the crank rotation (Sheldon Brown, https://www.sheldonbrown.com/synchain.html).
  - Choose the clamp to suit the frame: pinch-bolt eccentrics are easiest to adjust but are 'normally only used on steel frames because of the risk of breaking off the ears'; set screws are simple but dent the eccentric; wedge types must be kept greased or they corrode in place (Sheldon Brown, https://www.sheldonbrown.com/synchain.html).
  - Cabinet hinge mounting plates with a cam give about +/-2 mm of height adjustment (Blum catalogue 2022/2023, https://publications.blum.com/2022/catalogue/en/144/).
  - Car camber bolts with eccentric or offset washers can change camber by up to about two degrees (Wikipedia 'Camber angle', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camber_angle).
  - Setting gear backlash by turning an eccentric hub to change the centre distance shifts the tooth contact zone, can lower load capacity and efficiency, and causes start-up wear (Wikipedia 'Duplex worm', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duplex_worm).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - the 'eccentric nuts' you turn with a 10 mm spanner to snug up the wheels on a 3D printer such as the Ender 3
  - the eccentric bottom bracket that tensions the chain or belt on a tandem, single-speed or belt-drive bicycle
  - cam bolts used in car wheel alignment to set camber
  - kitchen cabinet hinge mounting plates with a small cam screw for moving the door up or down
- **when_to_use**: When you need a small, precise, lockable adjustment of where a wheel, pulley, sprocket, bearing or shaft sits: taking up play between rollers and a rail (V-slot carriages), setting chain or belt tension where the axles cannot slide (vertical bicycle dropouts, tandem timing chains, belt-drive bikes, motorcycles with single-sided swingarms), setting gear mesh or roller contact, or trimming alignment (car camber, cabinet doors, cam followers). It is compact, needs no slots, and holds its setting firmly once clamped.
- **when_not_to_use**: When tension must be kept up automatically as a belt or chain stretches: use a spring-loaded tensioner. When you need more travel than about twice the offset: use a slotted mount, sliding motor plate or jacking screw. When the part must not move sideways at all while you adjust it in one direction (the eccentric moves it on an arc): use a straight slide or screw adjuster. For precise, repeatable gear backlash: an eccentric centre-distance change disturbs tooth contact, so consider anti-backlash or duplex-worm gearing. In weak, soft or printed parts under heavy load: printed eccentrics can break and plastic creeps under the clamp.
- **environment_limits**: Corrosion can seize an eccentric in its housing: wedge-type bicycle eccentrics left unserviced 'can become corroded in place' (Sheldon Brown), and owners report having to tap stuck eccentric bottom brackets to turn them (Montague Bikes blog comments), so grease the outside of the eccentric. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Eccentrics set things that matter for safety, such as a car's wheel alignment, a motorcycle's chain tension or a bicycle's chain: re-tighten the clamp or pinch bolts to the maker's torque after adjusting, and check that the setting has not moved. When tightening the lock nut, hold the eccentric with a second tool so it does not turn and change the setting (VW Vortex forum, on an eccentric-mounted timing belt tensioner). Pinch-bolt eccentric shells on non-steel frames risk breaking the bolt ears (Sheldon Brown). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: (1) It slips and loses tension because the clamp was not tight enough or the load keeps trying to turn it; tighten to the proper torque and hold the eccentric while tightening. (2) It is seized by corrosion and will not turn; grease it at assembly (Sheldon Brown), and free a stuck one with gentle taps rather than by forcing the spanner, as bicycle owners report (Montague Bikes blog comments). (3) Set screws dent the eccentric, making fine re-adjustment difficult (Park Tool; Sheldon Brown). (4) Over-tightened V-wheels grind or develop flat spots; back it off until the carriage just runs without wobble. (5) Confusion because a full turn returns to the start: work in small steps and watch the effect, and use the thin-side mark. (7) A printed eccentric breaks at its thin neck when turned under load (Printables 79881). (8) Adjusting the eccentric also changes another dimension (pedal height, wheel toe) because it moves on an arc; check both after adjusting. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **related**: 
  - Belt tensioner (spring-loaded idler): keeps tension automatically as the belt stretches, instead of a set-and-lock adjustment
  - Eccentric cam lever (cam clamp, quick-release skewer): also uses an off-centre shape, but to clamp quickly with a lever rather than to set a position
  - Cam and follower: an off-centre or shaped disc that moves a follower continuously as it turns (the same geometry used for motion)
  - Slotted mount or jacking screw (adjustable centres): a straight, longer adjustment range; less compact
  - Lead screw and nut (incl. anti-backlash nut): fine straight-line adjustment with a screw
  - Dovetail slide with gib strip: takes up play in a slide with an adjustable strip instead of an eccentric
  - Kinematic coupling: locates parts exactly without any adjustment
  - Turnbuckle: sets tension in a rod, wire or cable by screwing

## 150 Turnbuckle

*tensioning, reels & take-up.* A threaded sleeve with left- and right-hand threads at its ends, so turning it pulls two rods or cables together to tighten them

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A metal sleeve with a screw going into each end, one threaded the normal way and one backwards, so twisting the sleeve pulls both screws in at once and tightens the wire, rope or rod they are attached to
- **term**: Turnbuckle
- **aliases**: 
  - stretching screw (Wikipedia)
  - bottle screw, bottlescrew (the closed, tube-bodied version)
  - rigging screw (sailing)
  - wire tensioner, cable tensioner
  - Spannschloss, Spannschraube, Wantenspanner (German)
  - screw coupling (railway couplings use one spindle with a left- and a right-hand thread, German Wikipedia)
  - AN/MS turnbuckle, clip-locking turnbuckle (aircraft: MS21251 body, MS21256 locking clip)
  - turnbuckle link (RC cars: a rod threaded left-hand at one end and right-hand at the other)
  - anti-sag gate kit (turnbuckle sold with cable and corner brackets)
- **family**: tensioning, reels & take-up
- **other_families**: cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware, sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **one_liner**: A threaded sleeve with a right-hand thread at one end and a left-hand thread at the other, so turning the sleeve draws both end fittings in (or lets them out) at the same time, tightening or loosening a wire, rope, chain or rod without twisting it.
- **complexity**: A few parts: a body (an open frame, or a closed tube called a bottle screw) with a right-hand internal thread at one end and a left-hand one at the other; two threaded end fittings (eye, hook, jaw or clevis with a pin, a plain stub for welding, or a swage stud or fork on wire rope); and locking parts: lock (jam) nuts, safety wire, locking clips, or split pins through a slotted body (Crosby; AC 43.13-1B; Petersen). Sailing rigging screws add a toggle so the stay can swivel (Seldén). A variant uses one central rod with both threads screwing into two threaded sleeves (German Wikipedia).
- **how_it_works**: The two end fittings screw into opposite ends of the body, one with an ordinary right-hand thread and one with a left-hand thread. Because the threads spiral opposite ways, turning the body one way screws both ends in together, pulling them towards each other; turning it back lets them out; and this happens 'without twisting the eye bolts or attached cables' (Wikipedia). Each full turn moves each end by one thread pitch, so the whole assembly shortens by twice the pitch per turn, and the screw's leverage turns a modest hand effort into a large pull. Thread friction stops it unwinding under a steady pull, but vibration can turn it, so it is locked with jam nuts, safety wire or clips (Wikipedia; AC 43.13-1B).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: pull a wire, rope, chain or rod tight and keep it there
  - line parts up exactly: fine-adjust the length of a stay, brace or link, e.g. making a hung scenery piece sit level in theatre rigging (Wikipedia)
  - join and hold: its end fittings link a cable to an anchor or to another cable
- **motion**: rotate+slide: turning the body (rotate) moves the two ends in or out along the line (slide); total travel is the 'take-up', e.g. 4-1/2 in (114 mm) on a Crosby 5/16 in x 4-1/2 in turnbuckle
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1 while adjusting: turning the body and changing the length are locked together by the threads (a screw motion); 0 once it is set and locked. Eye, jaw and toggle ends can also pivot on their pins or shackles, and a toggle adds articulation (Seldén).
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): its main purpose
  - motion conversion (e.g. rotary to linear): turning the body shortens or lengthens the line by twice the thread pitch per turn
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): thread friction holds it under a steady pull, but vibration can turn it, so it is secured with lock wire, clips or jam nuts (Wikipedia; AC 43.13-1B)
- **holding_principle**: shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the screw threads carry the pull, friction or clamping (force-fit): thread friction, plus jam nuts tightened against the body, stop it unwinding
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: removable: unscrew the body to release the tension, then unhook or unpin the end fittings; body and fittings can be reused
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: it is meant to be re-adjusted and reused, but stainless-on-stainless threads can gall and seize (Sailrite), and aircraft practice replaces turnbuckles with bent or distorted threads and never reuses safety wire (AC 43.13-1B)
- **tools_needed**: none for small ones (turned by hand); a bar or screwdriver through an open body, or a spanner on the flats of a closed body (German Wikipedia); spanners for jam nuts; pliers for safety wire, clips or split pins; anti-seize compound for stainless threads (Sailrite)
- **seal**: none: nothing makes it seal
- **load**: Light to heavy, in straight pull only: Crosby's turnbuckles are 'recommended for straight or in-line pull only'. Hardware-store zinc-plated turnbuckles are light duty (National Hardware 1/4 in x 7-1/2 in hook/eye: 90 lb, about 41 kg, safe working load); forged galvanized rigging turnbuckles are far stronger (Crosby working load limits at a 5:1 design factor: 1/4 in 500 lb; 5/16 in 800 lb; 3/8 in 1,200 lb; 1/2 in 2,200 lb). Sizes run from about 10 g for garden-fence wire to tonnes for bridges and buildings (Wikipedia). Weak against bending and side loads.
- **adjustable**: yes: that is its job; turn the body to set the tension or length anywhere within the take-up range, then lock it
- **play_and_precision**: Adjustment is fine and repeatable (2 × pitch per turn, and part turns give finer steps). It tolerates little misalignment: it must pull in a straight line, so rigging uses toggles to let it articulate (Seldén), and aircraft practice replaces a bent turnbuckle because it puts 'undesirable stress on the cable' (AC 43.13-1B). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **access_needed**: not applicable: it sits in the middle of the line; you only need room to reach and turn the body and to attach each end
- **materials**: Forged, quenched-and-tempered or normalized, hot-dip galvanized steel for rigging (Crosby); zinc-plated steel for light hardware-store types (National Hardware); cast or seamless-tube 316 stainless steel for marine and architectural use; chrome-plated aluminium-bronze bodies with stainless end fittings, or bronze thread inserts, to stop threads galling (Seldén; Petersen); aluminium bodies on some cheap types (Wikimedia Commons photo description). Printed: PLA, PETG, ABS or ASA (Printables). It joins steel wire rope, synthetic rope, chain, rods and cables.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to fit (buy one and hook it in); moderate to make: printed turnbuckles work for light loads with coarse threads and a parametric generator exists, but left-hand threads, thread clearance and strength along the thread axis need care

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: the usual route (hardware stores, rigging and marine suppliers)
  - 3D print (FDM): body and end fittings with modelled left- and right-hand threads (Printables 'Parametric turnbuckle'; BOSL2 threading.scad) *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **print_in_place**: no: printed as a body and two separate end fittings that are screwed in; the designs mark which end is left-handed with notches (one notch for left, two for right on the Parametric turnbuckle) so they go in the right ends (Printables 823324 and 451102)
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Model real threads, one left-handed: in OpenSCAD, BOSL2's threaded_rod() and threaded_nut() take left_handed=true, and $slop adds 4 × $slop of clearance to internal threads; Dalibor Frívaldský's Parametric turnbuckle (CadQuery) generates body, eye and hook ends with any thread diameter and take-up. 2) Go coarse and big: avoid FDM threads smaller than M5, and keep a wall around the internal thread at least as thick as the thread diameter (Hubs); the parametric design defaults to M10 with 100 mm take-up. 3) Mark the handedness with notches on the body and ends so you can't mix them up (Frívaldský; A_Str8). 4) Mind the layer direction: the end fittings are pulled along their length, so printed standing up the pull tries to split the layers ('it might break across the layer lines', ThirtyThreeDown). For strong ends, print them lying down with a flat trimmed off each side (about 1/6 of the thread diameter) and use a trapezoidal thread with about 0.12 mm clearance (ThirtyThreeDown); print the body standing up so its internal threads come out round. 5) Settings that worked: PLA, 0.15 mm layers, 3 perimeters, 15% gyroid infill (Frívaldský); 4 perimeters and 40% infill with no supports, where the heavier version held the designer's 80 kg and the lighter one at least 40 kg (super...Laserkatz, shade-sail turnbuckle). 6) Start moving-fit clearances at about 0.3 mm (Prusa) and test a short pair before printing the full length. 7) Material: ABS or ASA for weather resistance (super...Laserkatz); 9) Typical failures: ends snapping across the layers, threads stripping when too little is engaged, and threads binding when clearance is too tight. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Turnbuckles by end fitting: eye-eye, hook-eye, hook-hook, jaw-jaw (clevis), jaw-eye and stub ends (for welding), with open (frame) or closed (pipe or bottle-screw) bodies. Forged galvanized rigging turnbuckles meeting ASME B30.26 (e.g. Crosby HG-223 hook-hook, HG-226 eye-eye, HG-227 jaw-eye, HG-228 jaw-jaw) and matching lock nuts; zinc-plated hardware-store turnbuckles; stainless marine rigging screws with toggles, swage studs, forks or eyes (e.g. Seldén, Petersen, Sta-Lok, brands); aircraft turnbuckles to AN/MS drawings with MS21256 locking clips or safety wire (AC 43.13-1B); anti-sag gate kits (turnbuckle, cable, corner brackets, cable clamps; National Hardware); DIN 1478 turnbuckles in Europe (German Wikipedia); RC-car turnbuckle links and turnbuckle wrenches.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Screw both ends in by the same amount, and when tensioned leave no more than three threads showing outside the body at either end (FAA AC 43.13-1B, paragraphs 7-166 and 7-179); some bodies have a 'witness hole' to check that the threads reach far enough in (7-167).
  - Lock it against vibration: aircraft turnbuckles get safety wire (double-wrap preferred, each end wrapped at least four turns round the shank, never reused) or approved locking clips (AC 43.13-1B, 7-179 and 7-180); rigging turnbuckles use lock nuts (Crosby); closed marine bodies can be slotted for split pins (Petersen).
  - Straight, in-line pull only; forged Crosby turnbuckles have a 5:1 design factor, a proof load of 2.5 × the working load limit, and are fatigue-rated to 20,000 cycles at 1.5 × the working load limit (Crosby catalogue page 225).
  - Compare ratings before you trust one: Crosby forged galvanized 1/4 in 500 lb, 5/16 in 800 lb, 3/8 in 1,200 lb, 1/2 in 2,200 lb working load limit (Crosby catalogue) versus 90 lb safe working load for a zinc-plated 1/4 in x 7-1/2 in hook/eye hardware-store turnbuckle (National Hardware).
  - Take-up = open length minus closed length, and it is the second number in the size: a Crosby 5/16 x 4-1/2 eye-eye is 13.92 in fully open and 9.42 in closed (Crosby catalogue).
  - Each full turn of the body changes the length by twice the thread pitch, because both ends move one pitch at once (follows from the opposite-hand threads described by Wikipedia and AC 43.13-1B).
  - Stainless-on-stainless threads gall: coat them with anti-seize and turn slowly (Sailrite); marine rigging screws use chrome-plated aluminium-bronze bodies, or aluminium-bronze thread inserts from 5/8 in up, to stop seizing (Seldén; Petersen).
  - On boats, fit toggles so the stay can move freely: 'the main purpose of a toggle is to achieve correct articulation in the connection between the rigging and the mast or chainplate' (Seldén).
  - For printed turnbuckles: no FDM threads below M5, a wall at least one thread diameter thick around internal threads (Hubs), about 0.3 mm clearance for moving fits (Prusa), and BOSL2 adds 4 × $slop to internal threads (BOSL2 threading.scad).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - An anti-sag gate kit: a diagonal cable and turnbuckle hold a wooden gate square (National Hardware)
  - Shade sails, washing lines and cable fences or railings tensioned with turnbuckles (Sailrite; Wikipedia)
  - Sailing-boat shrouds and stays (rigging screws) and ships' lashings (Wikipedia; Seldén)
  - The corners of a wrestling ring, where padded turnbuckles tension the ropes (Wikipedia)
- **when_to_use**: When a wire, rope, chain or rod needs to be pulled tight and held, or its length fine-tuned, and then left: guy wires, fence and railing cables, shade sails, gate braces, hanging scenery or shelves from cables, bracing frames and towers, boat rigging, and adjustable links. It gives large force from a small turning effort, adjusts smoothly in small steps, doesn't twist the cable, can be re-tightened as things stretch, and is cheap and widely available.
- **when_not_to_use**: When tension must be released quickly (use a lever or over-centre tensioner, a cam buckle or a quick-release); when a lot of slack must be taken up (take-up is limited: use a ratchet strap, winch or pulley system); when the pull isn't in a straight line (side loads bend it: add toggles or shackles, or use another fitting); where it will be vibrated and can't be locked; for lifting or life-safety loads with unrated hardware-store turnbuckles (use rated forged ones); or where a constant tension is needed as things stretch or warm up (use a spring tensioner, e.g. a belt tensioner or constant-force spring).
- **environment_limits**: Vibration loosens it unless locked (Wikipedia; AC 43.13-1B). Stainless threads gall, especially under tension; lubricate and use bronze-bodied rigging screws (Sailrite; Seldén). One heavy closed-body range in the Crosby catalogue is rated from -4 °F to 392 °F (-20 °C to 200 °C). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Never use more than the working load limit, and don't use light hardware-store turnbuckles (e.g. 90 lb safe working load) for lifting, swings, hammocks or anything that holds people; use rated, forged hardware. Keep enough thread engaged (no more than three threads showing, AC 43.13-1B), because an end screwed out too far can strip. Aircraft control-cable turnbuckles are flight-critical parts to be adjusted and safetied by qualified mechanics (AC 43.13-1B). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: Loosens and loses tension: not locked against vibration; fit jam nuts, safety wire or clips (Wikipedia; AC 43.13-1B). Runs out of adjustment: the cable was cut too long or has stretched; shorten the cable rather than leaving only a few threads engaged (AC 43.13-1B). Seizes or galls: stainless on stainless, dirty or dry threads; use anti-seize and turn slowly (Sailrite), or bronze-bodied rigging screws (Seldén). Bends or cracks: side load or no toggle to let it align (Crosby 'straight or in-line pull only'; Seldén); replace bent ones (AC 43.13-1B). Breaks: an unrated turnbuckle overloaded. Ends in the wrong holes: left- and right-hand ends mixed up; check the markings or notches. Printed: ends snap across the layer lines or threads strip (ThirtyThreeDown; Hubs).
- **related**: 
  - Differential screw (fine adjustment screw): two threads of the same hand but different pitch give a tiny movement per turn, where a turnbuckle's opposite-hand threads give double movement
  - Lead screw and nut: one thread driving a nut along it, used for motion rather than holding tension
  - Ratchet tie-down strap (ratchet buckle): winds in webbing with a lever and ratchet; much more take-up and quick release, less fine adjustment
  - Capstan and windlass: a crank-turned drum that winds in rope with a big force over long distances
  - Guy-line slider (friction line tensioner): tensions a tent line by friction, quick but weak
  - Belt tensioner (spring-loaded idler): keeps tension constant with a spring instead of a fixed setting
  - Eccentric adjuster: a turned off-centre bush or nut that shifts a part a small amount
  - Draw latch (over-centre latch) and toggle clamp: pull things tight with a lever that snaps past centre, fast to open
  - Rod end and ball link (rose joint, RC ball link): the swivelling ends often screwed onto turnbuckle links in RC cars and linkages

## 151 Capstan and windlass (incl. differential windlass)

*tensioning, reels & take-up.* A drum turned by a crank that winds in rope with a big force; rope wrapped round a drum also holds a large load with a small pull

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A drum you turn with a handle to wind in a rope, so you can pull or lift something heavy with little effort; wrapping a rope a few turns round a drum or post also lets a small pull hold a big load
- **term**: Windlass and capstan (hand winch; differential or Chinese windlass)
- **aliases**: 
  - hand winch / crank winch / hand-crank winch
  - trailer winch or boat-trailer winch (a geared hand winch with a clicking ratchet)
  - brake winch / automatic-brake hand winch (holds the load by itself when you let go of the handle)
  - capstan (vertical-axle version, pushed round with bars; the rope takes a few turns and is pulled off the other side instead of being stored)
  - sailing winch / self-tailing winch (yachting name for a geared capstan; Wikipedia 'Winch')
  - snubbing winch (a ratcheting drum with no handle, used to hold a line; Wikipedia 'Winch')
  - anchor windlass
  - wheel and axle (the simple-machine name)
  - differential windlass = Chinese windlass (two drums of different sizes on one axle; Wikipedia 'Windlass')
  - Spanish windlass (a loop of rope tightened by twisting a stick in it; a simpler, different device)
  - windlass (UK canal term for the removable crank handle used to work lock paddles; not a rope drum)
  - capstan effect / belt friction / Euler-Eytelwein formula (the friction grip of rope wrapped round a drum; Wikipedia 'Capstan equation')
- **family**: tensioning, reels & take-up
- **other_families**: gear trains, gearboxes & transmissions, motion conversion, one-way & ratchet, self-locking & anti-back-drive, cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware, brakes, dampers & speed control
- **one_liner**: A rope drum turned by a crank or bars (a windlass if the axle is horizontal, a capstan if it is vertical) that winds rope in with a force multiplied by the ratio of handle radius to drum radius, usually with a ratchet to hold the load; the friction of a few turns of rope round a drum also lets a small pull hold a large load (capstan effect), and a differential windlass winds rope onto one drum while unwinding it from a slightly smaller one for a huge force gain.
- **complexity**: A small assembly that acts as one unit: a drum (barrel) on an axle, a frame with bearings, a crank handle (or bars pushed round, for a capstan), the rope, cable or strap and its anchor on the drum, and usually a ratchet wheel and pawl to hold the load; many add reduction gears, a hand brake or an automatic load-holding brake. A differential (Chinese) windlass has two drums of different diameters fixed on one axle plus a loose pulley hanging in the loop of rope between them (Wikipedia 'Windlass'; Make: 'The Chinese Windlass').
- **how_it_works**: Turning the crank turns the drum and winds the rope on, so turning becomes a straight pull (a wheel and axle). The force gain is the crank radius divided by the drum radius, times any gear ratio, less friction (Wikipedia 'Wheel and axle'). A ratchet wheel and spring- or gravity-loaded pawl let the drum turn only in the winding direction, so the load stays put when you let go; to let rope out you hold the handle and lift the pawl, or use a brake (Curt and Fulton winch instructions; 507 Mechanical Movements #491). On a capstan or sailing winch the rope is not stored: it takes three or four turns round the drum and someone pulls (tails) the free end, and the friction of the turns multiplies that small pull enormously (capstan equation), so the drum can haul or hold a heavy load (Harken; Wikipedia 'Capstan equation'). In a differential windlass the rope unwinds from the small drum while winding onto the big one, so each turn raises the hanging pulley by only half the difference of the two drum circumferences, giving a very large force gain but very slow lifting (Wikipedia 'Windlass'; 507 Mechanical Movements #129).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: haul a rope, cable or strap tight and keep it there
  - change speed or turning force: a long crank on a small drum trades many turns of the handle for a big pull
  - change the kind of motion: turning the crank becomes a straight pull on the rope
  - stop it moving back (one-way): a ratchet and pawl stop the drum unwinding when you let go
  - slow it, stop it or keep its speed steady: a hand brake, an automatic brake winch or easing the turns on a capstan lets a load down under control
- **motion**: rotate: the crank and drum turn continuously for as many turns as the drum holds rope; the rope itself moves in a straight line (rotary to linear)
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1: the drum turns about its axle and the rope's travel is tied to that turning; with the pawl engaged it can only turn one way
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - motion conversion (e.g. rotary to linear): turning the crank winds rope in as a straight pull
  - one-way (moves freely one way, locks the other): a ratchet wheel and pawl let the drum wind in with a loud clicking but not unwind (Curt; Fulton; 507 Mechanical Movements #491 capstan with a pawl in a circular ratchet at its base)
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): to let rope out you must hold the handle and lift the pawl, or turn a brake winch's handle backwards (Curt; Larson Electronics brake-winch manual)
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): a brake winch's built-in brake holds the load wherever you release the handle (Larson Electronics manual); Nguyen Duc Thang's 'Safety crank for windlass' animation shows a cone clutch and pawl doing the same job
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): hauls a line tight and keeps it tight; on a capstan or snubbing winch a small pull on the tail holds a large load through the friction of the turns (Wikipedia 'Capstan equation'; 'Winch')
- **holding_principle**: 
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the pawl drops between the ratchet teeth so the drum cannot unwind
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): turns of rope grip the drum (capstan effect), and brake winches hold the load with a friction brake
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): its main job is to wind rope in and let it out
- **input_output**: Crank rotation -> straight pull on the rope (rotary to linear). Ideal force gain = crank radius / drum radius (Wikipedia 'Wheel and axle'), multiplied by any gearing: e.g. the Curt 900 lb trailer winch has a 6.5 in handle and 3.25:1 gears. Harken describes a winch as a rotating lever whose ungeared gain is handle length divided by drum radius, with two- and three-speed gearing 'in effect' lengthening the lever. The gain falls as rope builds up in layers, because the drum's effective radius grows; winch capacities are rated on the first layer (Fulton; Larson). Differential windlass: ideal gain = 2 x crank radius / (big-drum radius - small-drum radius); Make's example (6 in crank, 3 in and 2.75 in diameter barrels) gives 96:1, i.e. 600 lb lifted with 12.5 lb of force in theory. Shafts: the crank is in line with the drum, or on a parallel pinion shaft in geared winches. Back-drivable: yes; a plain drum unwinds under load unless the pawl or brake is engaged, and a capstan's grip slips if the tail is let go. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **power**: passive (no power needed): hand-cranked. Powered capstans and winches (electric, hydraulic, pneumatic or engine-driven) do the same job (Wikipedia 'Capstan (nautical)'; *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **removability**: removable: the winch bolts to a post, stand, wall or vehicle, and the rope unhooks and unwinds; a capstan just needs the turns taken off
- **reuse_cycles**: effectively unlimited for the drum, crank and ratchet with occasional cleaning and inspection (Larson asks for at least a yearly check); the rope, strap or cable wears and is replaced at the first sign of kinks, broken wires or fraying (Larson; Curt)
- **tools_needed**: None to operate. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **seal**: none: the drum, gears and rope are open to the weather; hand winches need occasional cleaning and light lubrication, but never on the brake parts (Larson)
- **load**: Light to heavy. Hand trailer winches are rated for a few hundred to a few thousand pounds of straight pull (Curt 900 lb; Fulton models listed from 600 to 3,700 lb), on the first layer of line; a differential windlass can lift very heavy loads slowly by hand (Make's 600 lb example). Strong only in a straight pull on the rope; an off-angle pull piles rope at one flange and loads the frame sideways. The mounting must be far stronger than the winch (Fulton asks for a stand good for five times the rated capacity), and the rope's anchor on the drum is not rated for the full load, so turns must always stay on the drum (Curt).
- **adjustable**: Yes: rope length and tension are set by cranking in or letting out; a ratchet holds at each tooth and a brake winch holds at any point. Multi-speed sailing winches let you change the gear ratio as the load grows (Harken).
- **play_and_precision**: Not a precision locator. A ratchet holds only at tooth boundaries, so the load can creep back up to one tooth spacing before the pawl catches (backlash; Wikipedia 'Ratchet (device)'), and rope or cable stretches under load. The rope should meet the drum close to square: Harken wants the hauling part at 95-100 degrees to the winch drum to avoid a riding turn, and wire-rope drums are kept to small fleet angles (about 0.5-2 degrees for a smooth drum, up to 4 degrees for a grooved drum with ordinary 6- and 8-strand rope; LiftQuip).
- **access_needed**: not applicable: it is mounted on a frame, post, wall or deck; you need room to swing the crank and a straight lead for the rope
- **materials**: Drums and frames of steel, cast iron, bronze or aluminium (historic ones were largely of timber, e.g. the early capstan was a timber turned with bars; Wikipedia 'Capstan (nautical)'); rope of natural or synthetic fibre, polyester strap, or steel wire rope. Only winches made for steel cable should use it, because steel needs a larger drum hub to limit bending fatigue (Fulton). Maker versions use PVC and iron pipe on a wooden frame (Make), or 3D-printed drums and frames in ABS or PETG with steel bolts, threaded rod or a wooden dowel as the axle (Printables models).
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to moderate: a drum, crank and bearings are easy to print or build from pipe and wood, but a reliable ratchet or brake takes care, and for lifting or heavy pulls you should buy a rated winch

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - 3D print (FDM): drums, frames, cranks and pawls for light loads (Printables hand-cranked and compact winches)
  - woodwork: frames, uprights and traditional well windlasses (Make's Chinese windlass uses a wooden base and uprights)
  - CNC: a lathe-turned steel differential drum, one end turned slightly smaller (suggested in Make's article)
  - off-the-shelf part: trailer winches, brake winches, sailing winches, rope, cable, straps and bearings
- **print_in_place**: no: the drum, frame, crank and pawl are printed separately and assembled on a bolt, threaded rod or wooden dowel axle (Printables 'Hand cranked winch for 6mm rope' and 'Compact winch for 6mm rope')
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Use a metal or wooden axle and a real bearing rather than a printed axle: the 'Compact winch for 6mm rope' uses a 608 bearing and M8 bolts for the axle and handle and an M6 bolt as the pawl pivot; the 'Hand cranked winch for 6mm rope' uses a 24 mm broomstick as the main axle and M5 and M3 threaded rod (Printables). 2) Avoid PLA if the rope will stay loaded: the compact-winch designer warns the drum might fail under constant load; the hand-cranked winch was printed in carbon-filled ABS with 3-4 walls, 3 top and bottom layers and 20% infill and lifts about 20 kg without gears (Printables). PLA also softens in a hot car (Printables 'Ratchet strap reel' notes). 3) Use many walls and orient parts so the pull runs along the layers, since prints are stronger in one direction, like wood (Prusa). 4) Clearances: Prusa suggests starting at about 0.3 mm for parts that must move; the hand-cranked winch used 0.05-0.1 mm gaps and reamed the holes afterwards, so test-fit on your printer. 5) Make the flanges tall enough: a loaded rope should never come closer than two rope diameters to the top of the flange (Curt). 6) Anchor the rope through a hole in the drum (the hand-cranked winch threads 6 mm rope through a 6 mm hole that locks it under tension) but never rely on the anchor: keep at least 3-5 turns on the drum (Fulton; Larson; Curt). 7) Add a ratchet pawl on the frame (gravity-dropped like the hand-cranked winch, or spring-loaded) and check it holds before letting go of the crank (Curt). 9) For a capstan, flare the base of the drum slightly so the rope turns can't slide down, as ship capstans and jib winches do (Wikipedia 'Capstan equation'). 10) For a differential windlass, print both drums on one part so they can't slip against each other, keep the diameter difference small for a big gain, and wind the rope in opposite senses so one drum pays out while the other takes in; it gives no gain if the rope is arranged wrongly (Make). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Wikipedia 'Winch'), fairlead or rope guide, come-along (lever hoist). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Ideal force gain of a crank and drum = crank radius / drum radius; real gain = efficiency x that ratio (Wikipedia 'Wheel and axle').
  - Differential windlass: each turn raises the hanging pulley by pi x (big radius - small radius), i.e. half the difference of the two circumferences (Wikipedia 'Windlass'; 507 Mechanical Movements #129). Make's purchase formula (crank radius / big-drum radius) x (big-drum circumference / difference in circumferences) x 2 gives 96:1 for a 6 in crank on 3 in and 2.75 in diameter barrels (Make: 'The Chinese Windlass').
  - Differential windlass rope length (arithmetic from the formula above): to lift a height h the hanging loop must be at least 2h long, and the big drum winds on 2h x R / (R - r) of rope, which is 24 times the lift height with Make's 3 in and 2.75 in drums, so both drums need room for a lot of rope.
  - Capstan friction: holding force x e^(friction coefficient x wrap angle in radians). With a friction coefficient of 0.3, one turn multiplies the grip about 6.6 times, two turns 43 times and three turns 286 times (Wikipedia 'Capstan equation' table). Sailing sheets are seldom wound more than three turns because extra turns risk a riding turn (same article); Harken says to make three or four turns before cranking.
  - Feed the rope onto a sailing winch at 95-100 degrees to the drum to avoid a riding turn (override) (Harken). For wire rope on a smooth drum keep the fleet angle between about 0.5 and 2 degrees; grooved drums allow up to about 4 degrees with 6- and 8-strand rope (LiftQuip).
  - Always keep turns on the drum: at least three full turns (Fulton; Larson) or at least five (Curt); the anchor on the drum is not rated for the full load (Curt).
  - Winch capacity is rated on the first layer of line and falls as line builds up on the drum (Fulton; Larson). Keep at least two rope diameters of flange above the last layer (Curt).
  - Mounting: the winch stand must support at least five times the winch's rated capacity (Fulton); a winch strap should be rated at 1.5 times the winch capacity and its hook at 1.5 times the strap's (Curt).
  - If a hand winch can't be cranked easily with one hand it is probably overloaded; this brake winch needs at least 50 lb (23 kg) of load for its brake to work when letting out (Larson Electronics manual).
  - Rope or cable life rises steeply with drum-to-rope diameter ratio (D/d): in Mazzella's example a wire rope's relative service life goes from 17 at D/d 26 to 32 at D/d 35 (Mazzella).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - A boat-trailer or wall-mounted hand winch that clicks as you crank it (Curt; Fulton)
  - A well with a bucket on a rope wound up by a crank (Wikipedia 'Windlass')
  - Sailing-yacht winches, where one person cranks and another tails the rope (Wikipedia 'Winch'; Harken)
  - A climber holding (belaying) a heavier partner because the rope bends round the gear: the same capstan effect (Wikipedia 'Capstan equation')
- **when_to_use**: Use a windlass or hand winch when you need to pull or lift something much heavier than you could by hand, slowly, over a long run of rope: hauling a boat onto a trailer, tensioning a line, raising a bucket, opening a hatch, rolling a shade net over a vegetable bed (the use of the Printables hand-cranked winch), or driving a model crane. Use a capstan or a few turns round a post or snubbing winch when you need to hold or ease a heavily loaded line with little effort, then tie it off. Use a differential windlass when you want a huge force gain from simple parts and don't mind slow lifting and a long loop of rope (Make).
- **when_not_to_use**: Don't lift people, or loads over people, with a hand winch; use a rated hoist (Curt: never for lifting equipment or humans; Larson: not a hoist for people). Don't rely on the winch to hold a load for a long time; tie or block the load once it is in place (Curt; Larson). For precise positioning use a lead screw or rack instead. For small tension adjustments on a fixed line a turnbuckle or ratchet strap is simpler. A differential windlass is a poor choice when you need speed or a large lift height, because it needs so much rope. Never use a drum with no ratchet or brake under load.
- **environment_limits**: In freezing, icy conditions the pawl can stick: Larson suggests silicone spray on the pawl but no oil or grease on the brake parts. Dirty or wet use calls for more frequent inspection (Larson: at least once a year). Printed plastic drums creep under constant load, especially PLA (Printables 'Compact winch for 6mm rope'), and PLA can soften in a hot car (Printables 'Ratchet strap reel'). Steel cable corrodes and fatigues if bent round too small a drum (Fulton; Mazzella). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Stand back and stay out of the line the cable is pulling: if it slips or breaks it whips back along that line (Curt). Never let go of the handle before the ratchet pawl is engaged; the handle can spin dangerously and the load can drop (Curt). Hand winches are not for lifting people and many are not for lifting at all (Curt; Larson). Never put a brake winch into freewheel if the cable might take a load (Larson). Keep hands, gloves, hair and loose clothing away from the drum and the rope turns, which can slip under a sudden load (Harken; Curt); wear impact safety glasses and heavy work gloves (Curt). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: 1) Rope runs out to the anchor and the anchor fails: always keep 3-5 turns on the drum (Curt; Fulton; Larson). 2) Riding turn (override): the rope crosses over itself and jams, often because it feeds onto the drum at a bad angle; lead it on nearly square (Harken; Wikipedia 'Capstan equation'). 3) Pawl not engaged or slipping, so the handle spins and the load drops: listen for the click and release the handle slowly (Curt). 4) Overload: bent frames or stripped gears; if it can't be cranked easily with one hand it is probably overloaded (Larson). 5) Rope piled at one flange or climbing over it from off-angle pulls or overfilling (Curt; LiftQuip). 6) Kinked, frayed or fatigued cable from messy winding or too small a drum (Fulton). 7) Printed drum cracking or creeping under a constant load (Printables 'Compact winch for 6mm rope'). 8) Capstan slipping because there are too few turns or the tail was let go (Wikipedia 'Capstan equation'). 9) Brake winch won't let out light loads because the brake needs a minimum load (Larson). 10) Differential windlass gives no gain because the rope is wound the wrong way on one drum, or set screws slip on the axle (Make).
- **related**: 
  - Pulley system (block and tackle, differential hoist): gains force with rope running through pulleys instead of winding on a drum; often combined with a winch through a snatch block
  - Ratchet tie-down strap (ratchet buckle): a tiny ratchet winch with a slotted spool for webbing, built into a strap
  - Capstan cable drive (tendon drive): uses the same wrapped-rope friction, but the cable is anchored to a second drum to pass on limited rotation with no backlash
  - Constant-force spring (spring-return reel): winds the line back with a spring instead of a crank
  - Ratchet and pawl: the holding part inside most winches
  - Turnbuckle: tightens a line by a short, fixed amount with a screw; stores no rope
  - Cam cleat and clam cleat: hold the tail of a rope after a winch or capstan has tightened it
  - Come-along (lever hoist): a ratcheting, lever-worked winch for manual pulling (listed alongside winches in Wikipedia 'Winch')
  - Spanish windlass: tightens a rope loop by twisting a stick in it; no drum or ratchet (Wikipedia 'Windlass') *(unverified parts omitted)*

## 152 Shock-corded sectional pole

*tensioning, reels & take-up.* Tube sections with elastic cord running through them that pulls each joint home, so a tent pole or wand almost assembles itself

### What it is

- **plain_name**: Short hollow tubes with a stretchy cord running through all of them, so when you shake them out the cord pulls each tube into the next and the pole almost puts itself together
- **term**: Shock-corded pole (shock-corded tent pole; sectional pole with ferrule joints)
- **aliases**: 
  - shock-cord tent pole / shock-corded tent pole
  - bungee-corded pole / elastic-corded pole
  - folding tent pole / collapsible pole
  - sectional or segmented pole (US 4,979,531 calls it a 'flexible, multi-segmented support structure')
  - ferrule-jointed pole (the joint sleeve is the ferrule; a plugged, narrowed end is also called a spigot or insert)
  - folding cane (a white cane whose sections are held together by an elastic cord; Ambutech; Wikipedia 'Assistive cane')
  - shock cord = bungee cord = elastic cord ('occy strap' in Australia) for the cord itself (Wikipedia 'Bungee cord')
- **family**: tensioning, reels & take-up
- **other_families**: quick-release & tool-free, sliding, telescoping & linear motion, springs & energy storage, cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware
- **one_liner**: A pole made of short tubes that plug into one another (socket, spigot or ferrule joints), with an elastic cord running through all of them under light tension, so the sections stay together as one set, pull themselves into line and home, and fold up small.
- **complexity**: A few kinds of part, repeated: tube sections (aluminium, fibreglass or carbon fibre); a joint at each section end, either a ferrule sleeve glued onto the tube or a narrowed insert that plugs into the next tube; one elastic shock cord running the whole length; and two end tips or end-stops that anchor the knotted cord ends (US 4,979,531; Hilleberg; NEMO).
- **how_it_works**: The elastic cord is anchored at both ends of the pole and is shorter than the pole (about 70% of its length; Hilleberg), so it is always slightly stretched and pulls every section towards its neighbours. When you pull the sections apart and fold them side by side, the cord stretches further; when you unfold them, it pulls each section into the socket of the next, so you only need to guide each joint and push it fully home. The cord does not make the pole strong: once assembled, the overlapping tube at each joint carries the bending and pushing loads, and the cord just keeps the joints closed and all the sections together so none get lost (US 4,979,531).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - join and hold: turns a bundle of short tubes into one long pole that can be bent into an arch or pushed on
  - tighten and hold tension: the stretched cord keeps pulling the joints closed and keeps all the sections together as one set (US 4,979,531)
- **motion**: none once assembled (each joint is a rigid push-fit); while folding, each section slides out of its joint along the pole and swings round beside the next, held on the cord
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 when assembled: each joint is a snug tube-in-tube fit held closed by the cord. Pulled apart, a section can move about on its loop of cord, limited only by how far the cord stretches
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): the pre-stretched cord keeps pulling sections towards each other, so the pole almost assembles itself and a folded pole stays in one bundle (US 4,979,531)
  - self-aligning or self-centring: the cord lines each section up with the next and the ferrule or spigot guides it in; you only push it fully home
  - quick-release: pull a joint apart against the cord and fold it; no tools, buttons or latches
- **holding_principle**: 
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the spigot or ferrule overlaps the next tube and carries bending and pushing loads
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): the stretched elastic cord pulls every joint closed
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: pull each joint apart against the cord and fold
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: the tubes last for years, but the elastic cord slowly stretches out or snaps and is replaced when it goes limp (NEMO; Kelty); folding-cane cord has a natural rubber core that deteriorates with age (Ambutech)
- **tools_needed**: None to use. To restring: scissors, a clamp or locking pliers to hold the stretched cord, pliers to pull out the end tips, a lighter to heat-seal the cord ends, and a bent wire, bobby pin or string to fish the cord through (NEMO; Hilleberg; iFixit; Backpacking Light forum)
- **seal**: none
- **load**: Light to medium. Strong in bending and pushing (compression) when every joint is fully seated, which is why tent poles can be bent into arches; weak in pure pulling apart, because only the elastic holds a joint closed in tension; and weakest in bending right at the end of a stiff metal ferrule on a fibreglass pole, where 'virtually all breakage of such tent poles occurs' (US 4,979,531).
- **adjustable**: Cord tension only: retie the knot at an end tip to shorten or lengthen the cord (iFixit; Backpacking Light forum). The pole's length is fixed by its sections, though a broken section can be swapped for a spare (Hilleberg).
- **play_and_precision**: The cord returns the sections to the same order and alignment every time; sections must be restrung in their original order (Kelty; Hilleberg). A seated joint tolerates almost no angle, which is the point. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **access_needed**: end of a tube or shaft: the cord is anchored, tensioned and replaced at the pole's end tips
- **materials**: Tubes of aluminium alloy (the better poles), pultruded fibreglass (cheap poles, usually under 1 cm outside diameter; Wikipedia 'Tent') or carbon fibre; ferrules usually steel, glued onto the sections (US 4,979,531). Cord: shock (bungee) cord of rubber strands inside a woven cotton or polypropylene cover (Wikipedia 'Bungee cord'), usually 1/8 in (about 3 mm) thick for tent poles (NEMO; Kelty). End tips of plastic or metal. Maker versions: PVC, aluminium or carbon tube with printed joiners, end caps and cord anchors.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: buy tube and shock cord, cut the sections, glue in or print joiners and end caps, and string the cord; restringing a tent pole takes a few minutes (NEMO; Hilleberg)

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: tent-pole sections, ferrules, shock cord, end tips and repair sleeves are sold as spares (Tent Pole Technologies; Hilleberg)
  - 3D print (FDM): end caps, cord anchors and joiners for PVC, aluminium or fibreglass tube (Printables models)
- **print_in_place**: no: the tubes are usually bought, and printed joiners, end caps and cord anchors are separate parts glued or pushed onto the tube and then strung with cord
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 2) Anchor the cord with a knot trapped under a cap: feed the cord through the cap's hole, tie a knot, fit the cap and glue it with a flexible glue, optionally adding heat-shrink tube over it (Printables 'Tent Pole Bungee End Caps'); or stuff a double-knotted cord end into a slotted plug that fits inside the tube (Printables 'Aluminum Pole Shock Cord Retainer Insert', sized for tubes of about 10 mm inside diameter). 3) Use heat-resistant plastic for parts that live in a car or in the sun: the retainer-insert designer recommends ABS and 100% infill and printed it with the end cap on the bed and supports. 4) Make every surface the cord rubs on smooth and rounded, to stop the elastic wearing through (Printables 'Terminator for Bungee Cord'). 5) Fit: aim for a joiner that slides in snugly with no wobble; 8) Parametric models such as the OpenSCAD end-cap generator come pre-sized for 6-16 mm poles; measure your tube's outside diameter first (Printables 'Tent Pole Bungee End Caps'). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Shock cord (elastic cord, bungee cord) in 1/8 in (3 mm) or 3/32 in; replacement tent-pole sections; ferrules sized by pole diameter (e.g. a ferrule for 8.5 mm aluminium poles); tent-pole end tips, end caps or end-stops; pole repair sleeves (splints); shock-cord replacement kits; aluminium, carbon-fibre or fibreglass tube; folding-cane repair kits (Ambutech).
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Relaxed cord length about 70% of the assembled pole length (Hilleberg). Kelty's version: pull out about 25% of the pole length as stretch before tying off (about 75%); Backpacking Light forum users report about 65-75%, with around 75% described as the industry standard.
  - Cord thickness: 1/8 in (about 3 mm) is standard for tent poles (NEMO; Kelty; Backpacking Light forum); 3/32 in saves a little weight, and 1/16 in is too light to be useful (Backpacking Light forum, Dan Durston).
  - Flexible tent poles are typically 3-6 m long, made in sections about 30-60 cm long, each with a socket at one end for the next section (Wikipedia 'Tent').
  - On fibreglass poles, the stiff steel ferrule causes a severe stress concentration at its end, where 'virtually all breakage' happens (US 4,979,531): keep the stiffness change at a joint gentle and don't bend a pole until every joint is seated.
  - Heat-seal the cut ends of the new cord so it won't fray inside the pole (Hilleberg), and pull the knots very tight so they don't slip (NEMO).
  - Keep the sections in their original order while restringing (Kelty; Hilleberg), and tie a peg or clamp to the stretched cord so it can't shoot back into the pole (Hilleberg; NEMO).
  - Repair a cracked section with a repair sleeve slid over the damage, centred and taped at both ends, then replace the section later (Hilleberg pole repair sheet).
  - Scale: a replacement ferrule for 8.5 mm aluminium poles is listed as about 3 in (76 mm) long (Tent Pole Technologies), i.e. roughly 4-5 tube diameters of overlap on each side of the joint (arithmetic from the listing).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Dome and tunnel tent poles (Wikipedia 'Tent')
  - Folding white canes, whose sections are held together by an elastic cord (Ambutech FAQ; Wikipedia 'Assistive cane')
  - Folding camp chairs and other camp gear with shock-corded aluminium poles (the Printables 'Aluminum Pole Shock Cord Retainer Insert' is meant for poles on 'tents, chairs, etc')
- **when_to_use**: When a long, light pole or rod has to pack short and go up quickly, often in the dark, cold or wind: tent and tarp poles, folding canes, collapsible props and staffs, camera or lighting poles, kite spars and portable antenna masts. The cord keeps every section with its neighbours, so none are lost and the order is always right (US 4,979,531).
- **when_not_to_use**: When the joint must resist being pulled apart along the pole, use threaded joiners or spring-button (snap button) locks, because only the elastic holds a shock-corded joint closed in tension. When the length must be adjustable, use a telescoping pole with a twist lock or flip lock. For rigid, heavily loaded frames that stay up for years (furniture, ladders), use bolted or glued joints. Avoid cheap fibreglass poles where they will be bent hard, since they tend to break at the ferrules (US 4,979,531).
- **environment_limits**: The cord's rubber core deteriorates with age (Ambutech) and faster when stored hot, such as in an attic (Backpacking Light forum); tent-pole cord eventually stretches out or snaps and needs replacing (NEMO; Kelty). Printed end caps and plugs kept in hot cars or sun should be ABS or similar rather than PLA (Printables 'Aluminum Pole Shock Cord Retainer Insert'). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Elastic cords that snap or slip free can whip back and cause serious eye injuries (Wikipedia 'Bungee cord'), so hold the stretched cord with a clamp or peg while restringing (Hilleberg; NEMO). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: 1) Cord stretched out or perished, so joints no longer pull together: replace it with new cord at about 70-75% of the pole length (NEMO; Kelty; Hilleberg; Ambutech). 2) Cord snaps or a knot slips back inside the pole: pull knots tight, heat-seal the ends and use a clamp or peg while working (NEMO; Hilleberg). 3) Pole breaks at the end of a ferrule, especially fibreglass (US 4,979,531): seat every joint before bending, then splint the break with a repair sleeve and replace the section (Hilleberg). 5) Sections restrung in the wrong order so the pole doesn't fit the tent (Kelty). 6) Printed end caps pulling off, softening or wearing through the cord: glue with flexible glue, add heat-shrink, print in ABS and round every cord edge (Printables models). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **related**: 
  - Spring button lock (snap button, push-button tube lock): a spring-loaded button locks joined or telescoping tubes so they can't pull apart
  - Telescoping tube twist lock or lever (flip) lock: sections slide inside each other and clamp at any length
  - Threaded (screw-together) pole joints: resist pulling apart but are slower to assemble (e.g. Printables 'Alastor's Staff - 4-Parts Threaded Pole')
  - Poles linked only by a chain or non-stretch cord: keeps sections together without pulling them home (Wikipedia 'Tent' mentions poles 'linked by chain or cord')
  - Constant-force spring (spring-return reel): another way to keep a line under steady tension
  - Guy-line slider (friction line tensioner): tensions a tent's cords rather than holding its poles together
