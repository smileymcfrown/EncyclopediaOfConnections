## 1 Ratcheting reel closure (dial lacing system)

*tensioning, reels & take-up.* A knob that winds a lace or cable onto a spool to pull two sides together, clicks as it tightens, stays locked, and releases only when you pull or turn the knob by hand (as on snowboard boots and cycling shoes)

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A turn-to-tighten knob that winds a thin lace onto a small hidden reel, clicks as it tightens, holds tight by itself, and lets go only when you pull the knob out or turn it back by hand
- **term**: Reel-based closure (ratcheting reel lacing system, dial lacing system)
- **aliases**: 
  - dial lacing system
  - reel lacing / reel-based lacing system
  - reel-based closure system
  - rotary closure
  - ratchet dial closure
  - lace reel
  - cable reel closure
  - micro-adjust dial
  - ratchet dial fit adjuster (on helmets)
  - ratcheting shoelace tensioner (maker term)
  - BOA (brand), BOA Fit System (brand), 'Boa laces' / 'Boa dial' (brand used as a generic name)
- **family**: tensioning, reels & take-up
- **other_families**: one-way & ratchet, cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware, quick-release & tool-free, self-locking & anti-back-drive
- **one_liner**: A knob on a small reel that you turn to wind in a thin lace and pull two sides of a shoe, boot, brace or bag together; springy catches (pawls) click over ratchet teeth so it cannot unwind by itself, and it releases when you pull the knob up or turn it backwards.
- **complexity**: A small assembly that acts as one unit: base/housing with a ring of ratchet teeth and lace holes, a spool (reel) that the lace winds onto, 2-3 springy catches (pawls, often flexible plastic arms moulded onto the spool or knob), a knob (dial) with drive lugs, a centre screw or clip, the lace itself (coated steel cable, polymer cable or cord) and low-friction lace guides on the far side of the opening.
- **how_it_works**: Turning the knob turns the spool, which winds the lace in through holes in the housing and pulls the two sides of the opening together (rotary motion turned into a straight pull). Springy catches (pawls) ride over sloped ratchet teeth inside the housing, which makes the clicks; when you stop, lace tension tries to turn the spool back but the catches drop into the teeth and block it, so it stays tight with no effort (a one-way ratchet). To loosen, some knobs are lifted or pulled out, which uncouples the spool so the lace pays out all at once; others turn backwards, and ramps on the knob push the catches out of the teeth one click at a time (incremental release). In the patented design examined, three flexible pawl arms on the spool sit in about 33 triangular notches in the housing wall.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: winds in a lace and keeps it tight
  - stop it moving back (one-way): the ratchet stops the lace unwinding by itself
  - lock, and release by hand: stays locked under load until you pull the knob up or turn it back
  - join and hold: pulls two edges of a shoe, boot, brace, helmet cradle or bag together
- **motion**: multiple: the knob and spool rotate (unlimited turns, one way only while locked) and the lace slides through its guides as it winds in; pull-to-release knobs also move a few millimetres along their axis to unlock
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1 in normal use: the spool turns about one axis and the lace travel is tied to that turn. Pull-to-release knobs add a second, on/off axial movement that uncouples the spool.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): each turn winds more lace onto the spool
  - one-way (moves freely one way, locks the other): turns freely to tighten, blocked from unwinding by the pawls
  - hand-release only: lace tension cannot open it; only a person pulling the knob or turning it back releases it
  - self-locking (won't back-drive): the load on the lace cannot turn the knob backwards
  - indexing (clicks into set positions): tension changes in small repeatable click steps
  - quick-release: pull-to-release knobs dump all the tension at once
  - motion conversion (rotary to linear): turning the knob becomes a straight pull on the lace
  - torque-limiting (some designs only): patented versions add a slipping clutch so the lace cannot be over-tightened, e.g. for medical braces
- **holding_principle**: shape (the pawls interlock with the ratchet teeth; form-fit), with spring force (the pawls' own springiness) keeping them engaged
- **input_output**: Input: rotation of the knob -> output: straight-line pull on the lace (rotary to linear). Lace taken in per knob turn = spool circumference; per click = spool circumference / number of ratchet teeth (about 33 teeth in one patented design). Knob and spool are in line (coaxial). Back-drivable: no, lace tension cannot unwind the spool while the pawls are engaged. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **power**: passive (no power needed). Motorised 'powered lacing' shoes exist but are a different type (see related).
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: the lace is released by hand without tools (pull the knob or turn it back); the reel housing itself is sewn, riveted or screwed onto the item and is semi-permanent
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: tightening and releasing is its everyday job; the lace is the wearing part and is replaced when it frays (repair kits exist), while the dial is covered by a lifetime guarantee on the product it is fitted to according to one retailer
- **tools_needed**: none to use. Fitting: needle and thread, rivets or small screws (M3 screw and nut or heat-set insert on printed versions). Lace replacement: small pliers and a replacement lace kit
- **seal**: none (it closes an opening in a shoe, brace or bag but does not seal anything)
- **load**: light to medium. Enough for shoes, boots, braces, helmets and bag compression straps. A maker's fully 3D-printed PLA reel held about 45 lb (about 200 N) in a weight test. Strong along the lace pull (taken by the pawls in the teeth); weak where the lace rubs over a sharp edge (it frays and snaps) and, on pull-to-release knobs, against an accidental upward knock of the knob. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **adjustable**: yes: tension can be changed at any time in small click steps. Two-way (bi-directional) dials loosen one click at a time by turning back; pull-to-release dials release all at once and must be re-tightened.
- **play_and_precision**: small: tension can only be set to the nearest tooth (one click) and there is a little springback as the pawl settles into its notch. Repeatable enough that the same number of clicks gives much the same fit. Tolerates plenty of misalignment because the lace is flexible and runs through guides; the knob itself must turn on its own axis.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): operated from the outside; the base is fixed to the outer surface, and the lace is routed through guides on the item
- **materials**: Lace: stranded stainless steel cable (e.g. 7x7) coated with a slippery plastic such as nylon or PTFE, single-strand nitinol wire, multi-strand polymer cable, or textile laces. Guides: low-friction polymer or metal tubes. Maker versions: PLA or PETG/ABS/ASA for the body, PA12 (nylon) for the springy click part, 0.8-1 mm UHMWPE (Dyneema) or UV-resistant cord for the lace. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate: fully printable designs exist and work, but they need fine layers, a tough springy material for the pawls (nylon/PA12 or PETG), a strong lace anchored inside the spool and protection where the cord rubs; buying a replacement dial and lace kit is easier for footwear

### Making it

- **processes**: 3D print (FDM): the whole reel can be printed; pawls/click ring in PA12 or PETG, off-the-shelf part: replacement reel and lace kits, injection moulding (how commercial reels are made; not a home process)
- **print_in_place**: no: printed as separate parts (base, spool, click/pawl ring, spacer, spring, knob) and assembled with a centre M3 screw; some designs glue the pawls in separately for strength
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Proven printed designs: (1) Noel Swede's 'BOA style ratchet' (Printables 818537, CC BY) is fully printed in PLA, held about 45 lb in a weight test, and winds over 40 inches of 0.8 mm Dyneema (UHMWPE) cord. It needs a 0.4 mm nozzle and 0.2 mm or 0.1 mm layers (the author warns not to use 0.25 mm). The pawls are printed separately and glued in to make them stronger. The cap is held by an M3x10 screw in a heat-set insert, and pieces of 1/16 in welding wire act as detents that keep the cap down and as wear strips where the cord rubs over an edge. (2) MakerT's 'One-Hand Ratchet Lace Closure' (Printables 1743611, CC BY) turns to tighten and pulls to release. It was tested with a PA12 (nylon) click mechanism, PLA for the other parts and 1 mm UV-resistant cord. Recommended settings: 0.16 mm layers, 3-4 perimeters, 20-40% infill, Arachne on, supports only where needed. PETG, ABS or ASA are suggested for better durability. The click part is glued into its grooves one lip at a time with CA glue, so test that the glue works on your filament first. The lace is knotted and mechanically anchored inside the spool, so it does not rely on friction. Check that the M3 screw slides freely through the spacer and base and only threads into its captured nut. General advice: fine teeth and a small spool give finer adjustment. A prosthetic-hand builder found that only 5-6 clicks over the full range was too coarse, and that the cable bound up when released under tension. The fix was a larger ratchet wheel with more teeth and a smaller pull radius. Put the springy pawls in a tough material (PA12/PETG) rather than PLA, which Prusa describes as breaking into shards on impact. PLA also softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV, so use PETG (fine below about 80 °C) or ASA for footwear and outdoor gear. Round off or reinforce every edge the lace passes over.
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Replacement dial/reel kits (search 'lace reel replacement kit', 'dial lacing replacement kit', 'BOA replacement kit' (brand)); coated stainless-steel lace cable; lace guides; UHMWPE (Dyneema) or braided polyester cord 0.8-1 mm; *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Lace pulled in per click = spool circumference / number of ratchet teeth; one patented reel uses about 33 triangular notches (about 100 degree notch angle) and three flexible pawl arms (Boa Technology patent US8468657B2, https://patents.google.com/patent/US8468657B2/en). Worked example, illustrative only: a 10 mm spool with 33 teeth gives about 1 mm of lace per click.
  - Too few clicks over the working range makes fine adjustment impossible: a printed tendon ratchet with 5-6 clicks from open to closed was too coarse, and the fix was more teeth and a smaller pull radius (https://hackaday.io/project/162231-hintha-modular-prosthetic-hand/log/156107-prototype-version-01-review-part-2-ratchet-system).
  - Use a slippery lace in slippery guides: the patent examples use stranded stainless-steel cable (e.g. 7x7) coated with nylon or PTFE, running through tube-like guides of lubricious polymer or metal (https://patents.google.com/patent/US20120246974A1/en).
  - Make the knob need a push to engage before it turns, so that it is not knocked loose by accident during wear (https://www.snowandrock.com/expert-advice-and-inspiration/buying-guides/what-is-the-boa-fit-system.html).
  - Where people can over-tighten (for example braces for diabetic patients), add a tension-limiting slip clutch to the reel (Boa Technology patent US9629417B2, https://patents.google.com/patent/US9629417B2/en).
  - A printed PLA reel with 0.8 mm Dyneema held about 45 lb (about 200 N); reinforce where the cord rubs, using metal wire as in that design (https://www.printables.com/model/818537-boa-style-ratchet, read via the Printables API).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - snowboard boots with one or two dials (one for the shin, one for the forefoot)
  - cycling and golf shoes with a dial instead of laces
  - medical braces and orthoses with a dial
  - bike helmet fit dials at the back of the head, which use the same click-and-hold ratchet idea
- **when_to_use**: When you want quick, even, repeatable tightening with one hand and no knots, and release in one movement: shoes, boots, braces, splints, helmet cradles, prosthetic sockets, bag and backpack compression, or tying down a bundle. Also good for fine tension adjustment of a cord over a long travel, such as winding up a tendon in a printed prosthetic hand. Useful for people who find tying laces hard (a maker designed a printed version for one-handed use).
- **when_not_to_use**: Not for heavy or safety-critical loads such as lifting, tie-downs, climbing or load securing: use a ratchet strap, winch or rated hardware instead. Not where the lace would rub on sharp or abrasive edges (snowboard edges fray steel laces), unless you add guides. Not where an accidental knock on a pull-to-release knob would be dangerous; choose a turn-back (incremental) release or a latch. For a simple fixed-tension closure, a buckle, cam cleat or cord lock is simpler. If the tension must never exceed a limit (medical use), use a reel with a built-in tension limiter.
- **environment_limits**: Printed reels: PLA softens and deforms above about 60 °C (a hot car or a boot by a heater) and degrades in UV light, while PETG handles up to about 80 °C and outdoor use (Prusa material guides). Cord: one maker chose UV-resistant 1 mm cord for shoes. Steel laces fray and snap when a sharp steel edge rubs them, for example a snowboard edge resting on the boot on a chairlift. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Over-tightening can cut off blood flow. A reel patent points out that for diabetic patients in braces this can lead to serious complications, which is why tension-limiting reels exist. Pull-to-release knobs can release suddenly if caught on something, so don't use them where a sudden release is dangerous. Keep fingers out of the lace path while winding. Small parts are a choking risk for young children. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: (1) The lace frays and snaps, mostly where it rubs a sharp edge; carry a spare lace kit, as reels are designed to be serviced. (2) The pawls wear, crack or lose their spring, so the knob slips back or will not click; on printed versions make the pawls from nylon/PETG and glue them in as separate parts. (3) The knob pops off or releases by accident; use push-to-engage knobs, detents or a screw-held cap. (4) Coarse adjustment (too few clicks) or binding when released under load; use more, finer teeth and a smaller spool radius. (5) The lace slips at its anchor; anchor it mechanically inside the spool (knot in a pocket) rather than by friction. (6) Guides with high friction give uneven tension; use low-friction guides and coated lace.
- **related**: 
  - Ratchet and pawl: the basic one-way mechanism inside the reel, without the spool and lace
  - Ratchet strap / linear ratchet (cable tie): also tightens and holds, but the ratchet acts on a strap or toothed strip, not on a wound lace
  - Winch or capstan with pawl: the same wind-up-and-hold idea at much higher loads
  - Spring-return reel (constant-force spring reel): winds cord back in automatically instead of holding it locked
  - Cam cleat / cord lock: holds a cord at any position by pinching it, with no winding or clicking
  - Friction slip clutch / ratchet-tooth torque limiter: some reels include one so they cannot be over-tightened
  - Quick-lace pull cord with locking slider (as on some snowboard boots): pull by hand and lock, with no dial
  - Motorised (powered) self-lacing shoes: a motor does the winding

## 128 Belt tensioner (spring-loaded idler)

*tensioning, reels & take-up.* A pulley or roller pushed against a belt or chain by a spring or screw to keep it tight as it stretches

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A little wheel on a springy arm (or on a sliding bracket) that presses on the side of a belt or chain to keep it tight as it stretches
- **term**: Belt tensioner (spring-loaded idler; automatic tensioner)
- **aliases**: 
  - belt tensioner / chain tensioner
  - idler pulley / idler sprocket / idler wheel (a pulley that carries no load)
  - tensioner pulley / tensioner arm
  - jockey pulley / jockey wheel
  - belt tightener / tightening pulley (older name)
  - automatic tensioner / auto-tensioner / spring-actuated idler
  - rotary tensioner (spring acts through a pivoting arm) and linear tensioner (idler on a spring-loaded plunger)
  - inside idler / backside (outside) idler (named by which face of the belt it presses)
  - singlespeed chain tensioner (bicycles)
  - Kettenspanner (German, chain tensioner) *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **family**: tensioning, reels & take-up
- **other_families**: springs & energy storage, gear trains, gearboxes & transmissions, clutches & couplings
- **one_liner**: A free-spinning pulley, sprocket or plastic shoe pushed against the slack side of a belt or chain by a spring (or set by a screw) so the drive stays snug as the belt or chain stretches and wears.
- **complexity**: A small assembly that acts as one unit: an idler pulley, sprocket or plastic wear shoe on a ball bearing; an arm that pivots on a bolt or bushing (rotary type) or a plunger or slide (linear type); a spring (coil, torsion or compression) or an adjusting screw; and a base or bracket bolted to the frame. Car engine tensioners also have a damper; manual versions have slots and clamp bolts instead of a spring.
- **how_it_works**: A belt or chain only grips and runs quietly if it is kept tight, but it stretches in use. The tensioner's wheel runs on the belt's slack (unloaded) side and a spring keeps pushing it in, so as the belt gets longer the arm simply moves further and the tension stays roughly the same, with no need to move the motor or shafts. Because the idler only rolls with the belt, it passes on no power. A screw-set (fixed) idler works the same way, but you push it in by hand, lock it, and re-adjust it later as the belt stretches.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: keeps a belt or chain snug as it stretches, wears and warms up
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): the idler spins with the belt, and the arm swings or slides to follow the slack
- **motion**: multiple: the idler rotates continuously with the belt; the arm pivots (rotary tensioner) or slides (linear tensioner) through a short range as the belt stretches
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 2: the idler spins on its bearing (1) and the spring-loaded arm swings or slides to take up slack (1). A screw-set idler has only the spin (1) once its bracket is clamped.
- **special_behaviour**: tensioning (takes up slack and holds): the spring keeps pushing the idler into the belt, so tension is kept up automatically as the belt stretches; screw-set versions hold a tension you set by hand
- **holding_principle**: spring force (a spring presses the idler against the belt); screw-set versions hold by friction or clamping (bolts clamp the bracket in its slot). Otherwise it is not a holding joint: it passes on motion.
- **input_output**: Not a transmission: the idler turns freely with the belt and passes on no power. It keeps the belt gripping the working pulleys; slack V-belts slip, which can cut drive efficiency by as much as 10% (Machine Design). Shaft arrangement: the idler axle is parallel to the drive shafts. Back-drivable: yes; a spring-loaded arm gives way if the belt on its side goes tight (for example when a drive reverses, brakes or is back-pedalled), which is why spring tensioners are not used on reversing drives.
- **power**: passive (no power needed): a spring or weight does the work. Some engine timing drives use hydraulic tensioners instead (Wikipedia 'Tensioner').
- **removability**: removable: bolted to the frame or engine; to change a belt you swing the arm back against its spring or slacken its bolts
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: the tensioner can be released and reset at every belt change, but its spring, pivot bushing and bearing wear out, so car tensioners are often replaced along with the belt
- **tools_needed**: spanners, sockets or Allen keys to mount it and to swing or lock the arm; for screw-set idlers, a way to measure belt tension (Gates suggests the force-deflection method or a sonic tension meter). Bicycle tensioners fit the derailleur hanger with an Allen key.
- **seal**: none (bought idler pulleys usually have sealed ball bearings, but the tensioner does not seal anything)
- **load**: light to heavy depending on size: light for 3D-printer GT2 belts, medium for bicycle chains and small machines, heavy for car serpentine belts and industrial drives. Strong in the direction it pushes (into the slack span); weak against a load that pushes it back out, because only the spring resists: if its side of the belt becomes the tight side the spring force may be 'inadequate to maintain proper tension' (Machine Design).
- **adjustable**: yes: spring-loaded versions adjust themselves as the belt stretches, and some let you set the spring's preload with a screw; screw-set idlers are moved along a slot or pivoted and then clamped (Gates' adjustable idler brackets can do both)
- **play_and_precision**: Noticeable springiness: the spring lets the idler move, so the belt span is less stiff and its tension varies a little with load. Fine for passing on power, but it can let a toothed-belt positioning drive shift or skip; Gates advises that on synchronous drives 'All idlers should be rigidly mounted in place to minimize movement or deflection'. Flat idlers let a belt wander slightly sideways (Bestorq), and a chain tensioner must line up with the chain line.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind): usually bolted to one face of a frame, bracket or engine; you need room to reach the arm to release it when fitting a belt
- **materials**: Arms and brackets: steel, cast iron or aluminium; idler pulleys: steel, aluminium or plastic (nylon or acetal) running on ball bearings; chain tensioners may use a sprocket or a low-friction polymer wear shoe (Machine Design); springs: steel coil or torsion springs. 3D-printed arms and brackets: ASA, ABS, PETG or nylon, not PLA near motors or heated chambers, used with a bought bearing or GT2 idler.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to moderate: easy with a bought idler pulley or bearing and a spring on a printed or sheet-metal arm; moderate to get the spring force, alignment and placement (slack side) right; car and industrial automatic tensioners should be bought

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part (idler pulleys, idler sprockets, automatic tensioners, bicycle chain tensioners, adjustable idler brackets)
  - 3D print (FDM): arms, brackets and sliders, with a bought bearing or idler pulley, a pivot bolt and a spring
  - sheet metal or CNC: brackets and arms *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **print_in_place**: no: printed arms or brackets are assembled with a bought ball bearing or idler pulley, a pivot bolt and a spring (or an adjusting screw)
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Buy the rolling part: use a toothed idler (e.g. a GT2 20-tooth idler) or bearing-based pulley inside the belt and a smooth, flat, uncrowned idler on the belt's back (Gates GT3 manual). Size it: a flat idler on the back of the belt should be at least 1.5 times the small pulley's diameter and at least the belt's minimum (e.g. 0.3 in for MXL, 0.6 in for 3M, 0.8 in for XL belts); an inside idler should be about the size of the smallest pulley (Bestorq). Place it on the slack side, preferably inside the belt, giving nearly equal wrap on both pulleys (Machine Design, Bestorq). Material: 3D-printer tensioner designers say 'DO NOT PRINT OUT OF PLA' and use ASA, ABS, nylon or PETG, especially near a hot motor or heated chamber, and one recommends at least 2 mm walls or 100% infill to reduce creep (Printables 1066464 and 1702885). Pivot: make the pivot hole a close fit on a shoulder bolt; one designer tightened his after the first version was sloppy (Printables 437104). Springs: salvage a rear-derailleur spring for a bike chain tensioner (Printables 437104) or reuse a printer's stock springs in a screw-adjustable holder (Printables 1066464, 1702885). A spring-loaded belt on a fast printer is softer than a locked one; one engineer who locked his spring tensioners with a screw raised the axes' resonance frequencies by a few hertz. Take care when removing stock spring tensioners: 'things can go flying fast and far'.
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search terms: 'idler pulley' (flat, flanged or V-groove), 'GT2 idler pulley 20T 3 mm or 5 mm bore' (toothed or smooth), 'idler sprocket', 'belt tensioner', 'automatic belt tensioner' or 'serpentine belt tensioner' (car), 'spring-loaded tensioner arm', 'rotary tensioner' and 'linear chain tensioner' (with polymer shoe), 'singlespeed chain tensioner' (bicycle; fits the derailleur hanger), 'adjustable idler bracket' (Gates, brand), 'torsion spring', 'belt tension meter' (force-deflection or sonic). Idler pulleys usually have sealed deep-groove ball bearings (e.g. 6304-6310 on Gates industrial idlers; *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Put the tensioner on the slack (unloaded) side, on the inside of a belt, placed so both pulleys get nearly equal wrap; on a chain, put the idler outside the loop on the slack side, midway between the sprockets (Machine Design, 'Belt and chain tensioners', https://www.machinedesign.com/archive/article/21828669/belt-and-chain-tensioners).
  - Order of preference for idler position: inside slack side, outside slack side, inside tight side, outside tight side; the matching service-factor add-ons are 0.0, 0.1, 0.1 and 0.2 (Bestorq 'Idlers', https://www.bestorq.com/wp-content/uploads/Idlers.pdf).
  - Never use a spring-loaded tensioner on a reversing drive, because the slack side becomes the tight side (Machine Design; Bestorq). On bicycles, chain tensioners 'must not be used with fixed gears or coaster brakes' (Sheldon Brown, https://www.sheldonbrown.com/no-derailers.html).
  - Automatic tensioners are 'not recommended for use on synchronous belts unless the drive is intended strictly for motion transfer' (Machine Design); Gates says idlers on synchronous drives should be on the slack side and 'rigidly mounted' (Gates PowerGrip GT3 Drive Design Manual, section 12 'Use of Idlers').
  - A flat idler on the back of the belt: at least 1.5 x the small pulley's diameter, placed close to the small pulley to increase its wrap; minimum flat outside idler diameters include MXL 0.3 in, XL 0.8 in, 3M 0.6 in, 5M 1.1 in, 8M 2.9 in and A-section V-belt 4.3 in. An inside idler should be the diameter of the smallest pulley and should not be flanged (Bestorq).
  - Gates minimum idler sizes for 5M PowerGrip GT3: inside grooved 14 grooves, inside flat 2.50 in OD, backside 1.25 in OD; for XL timing belts: 12 grooves, 2.50 in and 1.00 in OD (Gates PowerGrip GT3 Drive Design Manual, idler size table, https://www.gatesaustralia.com.au/~/media/files/gates-au/industrial/catalogues/powergrip-gt3-design-manual-us-january-2014.pdf).
  - Flat idler width: a flanged one about 15% wider than the pulley face, an unflanged one at least 25% wider; flange height at least 25% of belt thickness (Bestorq).
  - Chains: slack should not exceed 4% of the span (under 2% for heavy loads or frequent starts and stops); a span of more than 50 chain pitches typically needs a tensioner; an idler sprocket should have at least three teeth engaged (Machine Design).
  - Adjustable shaft centres are usually preferred over idlers, because every idler adds a bending cycle that shortens belt life (Bestorq; Gates: use idlers only when 'functionally necessary').

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - the spring-loaded arm and pulley pressing on a car engine's serpentine belt
  - a single-speed or internal-gear bicycle's chain tensioner (and a rear derailleur, which is also a spring-loaded chain tensioner)
  - a belt sander's spring-loaded idler drum that keeps the sanding belt tight
  - spring or screw belt tensioners on 3D printers
- **when_to_use**: When the shaft positions cannot be moved to tighten a belt or chain (fixed motor mounts, vertical bicycle dropouts, several accessories on one engine belt), when the belt or chain will stretch or wear and you want tension kept up automatically, to damp whip on long spans, to route a belt around an obstruction or increase wrap on a small pulley, or to make fitting a new belt easy. Spring tensioners suit one-direction power drives (engines, fans, pumps, conveyors, bike freewheels); screw-set idlers suit drives that need a fixed, stiff tension.
- **when_not_to_use**: Not on drives that reverse, brake through the belt or are back-pedalled (fixed-gear bikes, coaster brakes, reversing machines): the spring gives way when the belt on its side goes tight, so use adjustable centres (sliding motor mount, horizontal dropouts, eccentric bottom bracket) or a locked (screw-set) idler instead. Not for precise toothed-belt positioning drives (CNC, 3D printers) where stiffness matters: use a rigidly mounted idler or adjustable centres. Avoid an idler altogether if the shaft centres can be adjusted, because idlers add bending and shorten belt life; avoid backside idlers on V-belts where possible, because reverse bending is especially bad for them (Bestorq).
- **environment_limits**: Heat: springs lose tension over time when exposed to heat (Brake & Front End). Rust, corrosion, dirt or mud can jam the pivot so the arm cannot follow the belt; road salt is a common cause (Brake & Front End). Debris packed into toothed-belt pulleys causes bad tooth engagement, so shield synchronous drives in dirty places (Gates GT3 manual). Temperature changes: install timing-belt tensioners with the engine and tensioner at the same room temperature, as tension set hot or cold will be wrong (Litens installation procedure). 3D-printed arms creep under constant spring load, especially PLA near heat; use ASA, ABS, nylon or PETG and thick walls (Printables 1066464, 1702885).
- **safety_notes**: Belts meeting pulleys and chains meeting sprockets form in-running nip points that can pull in fingers, hair or clothing; guard them (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.212 lists 'ingoing nip points' among hazards that need guarding). The spring stores energy: when removing or releasing a tensioner, hold the arm with a tool, because parts can 'go flying fast and far' (Printables 1066464). Never turn a drive by hand with fingers near the belt and idler. If the worn bushing in a car tensioner pulley seizes it may snap the belt, and a car whose single serpentine belt breaks instantly loses several critical functions at once (Brake & Front End; Wikipedia 'Serpentine belt').
- **common_failures**: (1) A weak or broken spring lets the belt slip, glaze and squeal; replace the spring or tensioner. (2) A worn pivot bushing lets the arm rock, causing belt flutter, noise and misalignment. (3) Worn idler bearings make the pulley wobble or growl, and a worn pulley bushing causes vibration and noise; if the bushing seizes it may snap the belt (all Brake & Front End). (4) The tensioner is on the tight side, or the drive reverses, so the spring is overpowered and the belt jumps teeth or the chain falls off; move it to the slack side or use a locked idler. (5) An idler that is too small, or a backside idler on a V-belt, shortens belt life through extra or reverse bending; follow minimum diameters. (6) A misaligned or crowned idler lets the belt walk off; align it and use flat, uncrowned backside idlers. (7) A printed arm cracks or creeps; print thicker, denser and in a heat-resistant plastic.
- **related**: 
  - Belt drives (timing, round and V-belt): the drives a tensioner serves
  - Eccentric adjuster: tensions a belt or chain by turning an off-centre bushing and clamping it; set once, not automatic
  - Adjustable shaft centres (slotted motor mount, jacking screw, horizontal dropouts): move a working pulley instead of adding an idler; preferred where possible
  - Hydraulic tensioner: an oil-damped plunger used on engine timing chains and belts
  - Rear derailleur: a spring-loaded chain tensioner that also shifts the chain between sprockets
  - Belt-tightener clutch: an idler pressed in by a lever to engage a slack belt drive and released to stop it (507 Mechanical Movements no. 5)
  - Constant-force spring (spring-return reel): another way to keep a steady pull on a cord or tape
  - Turnbuckle: tensions a rod, wire or cable by screwing, with no rolling idler

## 129 Eccentric adjuster (eccentric nut, spacer or bushing)

*tensioning, reels & take-up.* An off-centre spacer or bushing that moves a wheel, pulley or shaft sideways as you turn it, for setting tension or taking up play

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A round spacer, nut or sleeve with its hole drilled off-centre: turn it with a spanner and the wheel, pulley or shaft riding on it shifts a little sideways, then clamp it so it stays put
- **term**: Eccentric adjuster (eccentric spacer, eccentric bushing or eccentric cam bolt)
- **aliases**: 
  - eccentric nut (common 3D-printer name, e.g. on V-wheel carriages)
  - eccentric spacer (V-slot and OpenBuilds systems)
  - eccentric bushing / eccentric bush / offset bushing / eccentric sleeve
  - eccentric bottom bracket, EBB (bicycles) / 'the eccentric' (tandems)
  - eccentric hub (bicycle rear hubs; motorcycles with single-sided swingarms)
  - cam bolt / camber bolt / eccentric bolt with eccentric or offset washer (car wheel alignment)
  - eccentric stud cam follower / eccentric type cam follower (bearing catalogues)
  - cam height adjustment (cabinet hinge mounting plates)
  - eccentric-mounted tensioner pulley (engine timing belts)
  - Exzenter (German)
- **family**: tensioning, reels & take-up
- **other_families**: locating & alignment, clamping, magnetic & friction connections, sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **one_liner**: A spacer, bushing or bolt whose hole is off-centre, so turning it moves the part mounted on it sideways by up to twice the offset; you turn it until a belt, chain, wheel or gear is just right and then clamp it to lock the setting.
- **complexity**: A few parts: the eccentric itself (a round or hex-headed spacer, sleeve, plug or bolt with an off-centre bore or shank, often marked on its thin side); the axle, bolt or bearing that runs through the off-centre hole; the part being adjusted (a wheel, pulley, sprocket, bottom bracket or gear); and a clamp to lock it (a nut, pinch bolts, set screws or a wedge). Example: 'eccentric spacer, M5 bolt, V-wheel, lock nut'.
- **how_it_works**: The eccentric sits in a round hole or on a bolt and can turn about its own centre, but the part it carries sits in the off-centre hole, so as you turn it the carried part travels around a small circle whose radius is the offset (eccentricity). Half a turn takes it from one side to the other, giving a total sideways shift of twice the offset; turning further brings it back again, so unlike a normal nut it never gets 'tighter and tighter'. Once the belt, chain or wheel is at the right tightness you clamp the eccentric (nut, pinch bolt, set screws or wedge) so friction stops it turning. Because the carried part moves on an arc, it also moves slightly at right angles to the direction you want, for example an eccentric bottom bracket also changes the pedal height.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - tighten and hold tension: takes up slack in a chain or belt, or presses rollers onto a rail
  - line parts up exactly: sets a wheel, bearing, gear or hinge to the right position and takes up play
  - join and hold: once clamped it is also the mounting for the axle or bolt it carries
- **motion**: rotate: you turn the eccentric through up to half a turn (180°) while adjusting, which shifts the carried part sideways along a short arc; none once it is clamped
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 0 once clamped. While adjusting, 1: turning the eccentric, which moves the carried part along an arc (mostly sideways, a little up or down).
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): set once and clamped; it does not re-adjust itself as a belt or chain stretches (unlike a spring tensioner), so it must be reset as parts wear
  - motion conversion (e.g. rotary to linear): a turn of the eccentric becomes a small, finely controllable sideways movement
- **holding_principle**: friction or clamping (force-fit): a nut, pinch bolt, set screws or a wedge clamps the eccentric so it cannot turn; plus shape (form-fit): the off-centre bore holds the axle or bolt
- **input_output**: An adjuster, not a drive: a rotation of the eccentric (up to 180°) gives a sideways shift of up to twice the offset, e.g. about 0.79 mm in total for an OpenBuilds V-slot eccentric spacer, and 0.25-1.5 mm eccentricity on catalogue eccentric cam followers. The ratio is not constant: by geometry, the shift per degree is largest in the middle of the range and smallest near the two ends. Back-drivable: yes when unclamped, because a sideways load on the carried part tries to turn the eccentric; clamped, it is held only by friction, so under-tightened eccentrics can slip.
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: removable: unclamp, then slide off or unscrew; set screws can dent the eccentric so it is harder to re-set finely
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: metal eccentrics can be re-adjusted and re-used indefinitely if not corroded; printed eccentrics wear and can break at thin sections
- **tools_needed**: a spanner to turn it (10 mm for full-size OpenBuilds spacers and Ender 3 eccentric nuts, 8 mm for 'mini' spacers), plus a spanner or Allen key for the lock nut, ideally holding the eccentric with one tool while tightening the nut with another; a pin spanner (e.g. Park Tool SPA-1) for bicycle eccentric bottom brackets; a special hook spanner for motorcycle eccentric hubs; a screwdriver for cabinet hinge cams
- **seal**: none
- **load**: light to heavy depending on size: V-slot wheel spacers and hinge cams are light-duty; bicycle eccentric bottom brackets, motorcycle eccentric hubs, car camber bolts and bearing cam followers carry heavy loads (catalogue eccentric cam followers have static load ratings of kilonewtons). Strong in straight shear across the bolt or axle; the weak direction is rotation: a load pushing the carried part sideways makes a turning force on the eccentric that only the clamp's friction resists, which is why under-clamped eccentrics creep, creak or slip.
- **adjustable**: yes: that is its purpose; loosen the clamp, turn the eccentric to shift the part (total range twice the offset), and re-clamp
- **play_and_precision**: none once clamped: it is used to remove play (e.g. between V-wheels and a rail, or in a chain). Very fine adjustment, down to a fraction of a millimetre, especially near the ends of its range. Tolerates only small misalignment corrections (at most twice the offset, typically well under a few millimetres, except large bicycle and motorcycle eccentrics). The carried part moves on an arc, so there is a small unwanted shift at right angles.
- **access_needed**: one side only (blind) in most cases: V-slot spacers, cam bolts and hinge cams are turned from the head side; bicycle eccentric bottom brackets and motorcycle eccentric hubs are reached from the side of the frame or swingarm
- **materials**: Stainless steel (OpenBuilds eccentric spacers), steel (car cam bolts, cam-follower studs, cabinet hinge plates), aluminium or steel (bicycle eccentric bottom brackets and hubs), bronze or steel (industrial eccentric bushes). 3D-printed eccentrics in PETG or similar for light, low-stress uses, always with a metal bolt through them. The part around it can be metal, printed plastic, acrylic or wood; a metal eccentric spacer's collar needs a round hole of about 7.1 mm.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to use bought ones (V-slot eccentric spacers are cheap and fit a round hole in a printed or cut plate); moderate to make your own: the off-centre hole must be accurate, which needs a lathe or careful printing, and printed ones are weak

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part (most common: eccentric spacers, eccentric bushings, eccentric cam followers, eccentric bottom brackets, cam bolts)
  - 3D print (FDM): design round pockets in printed plates to take bought eccentric spacers, or print light-duty eccentric spacers or bushings
  - CNC or lathe: turning an off-centre bore (metal eccentric bushings)
  - laser cut: round holes in acrylic or plywood gantry plates to take eccentric spacers
- **print_in_place**: no: the eccentric is a separate part fitted onto a bolt or into a hole; a printed eccentric still needs a metal bolt and nut
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: Easiest: print or cut the gantry plate or carriage with round holes that fit bought metal eccentric spacers (OpenBuilds spacers have a collar that 'fits into a 7.12mm hole'; one maker drilled acrylic plates to 7.1 mm), and use them on only one side of each set of wheels, with plain spacers on the other side. Print a hole-size test gauge first to find the right printed hole for M5 bolts and 7 mm eccentric collars on your printer (Printables 466199). If you print the eccentric spacer itself, keep it for light duty: one designer notes that the neck of his printed long eccentric spacer 'is a bit weak so the spacer shouldn't be turned under the stress' (Printables 79881). Mark the thin side of a home-made eccentric as OpenBuilds does with a divot, so you always know where the offset points. Leave room for a spanner (or print a long eccentric-nut wrench, Printables 890104). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Search terms: 'eccentric spacer' (V-slot, 5 mm bore, 6 mm or 1/4 in tall, 8 mm or 10 mm hex), 'eccentric nut' (3D printer V-wheels), 'eccentric bushing' or 'eccentric bush', 'eccentric cam follower' or 'eccentric stud cam follower' (e.g. IKO CFE and CFES, THK CFH-A and CFH-AB series; brands), 'eccentric bottom bracket' (bicycles), 'eccentric hub' (singlespeed bicycles with vertical dropouts), 'camber bolt' or 'cam bolt kit' (cars), 'cam adjustable mounting plate' (cabinet hinges, e.g. Blum CLIP top, brand). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Total adjustment is twice the eccentricity: half a turn moves the carried part from one side to the other (geometry). Examples: an OpenBuilds eccentric spacer gives 'a total range of adjustment of approximately 0.79mm' (MakerTechStore, https://www.makertechstore.com/products/eccentric-spacer); THK eccentric cam followers have 0.25-1.0 mm eccentricity (https://www.thk.com/jp/en/products/other_rotary_guides/cam_follower/eccentric/); IKO CFE 0.4-1.5 mm (https://www.ikont.co.jp/eg/product/needle/ndl0603.html); IKO CFES 0.25-0.6 mm (https://www.mrosupply.com/bearings/862859_cfes16_iko-bearing/).
  - V-wheel carriages: turn eccentric nuts in very small steps until the wheels roll smoothly and can easily be pushed both ways but the carriage never wobbles; too tight and the wheels grind and damage themselves and the rail (3D Print Beast, https://www.3dprintbeast.com/eccentric-nuts-ender-3-v2-pro/). Over-tight V-wheels develop flat spots (FlixMySwitch video title, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=I6NI7lGAv44).
  - Tandem front chain set with an eccentric: about 1/2 inch (12 mm) of up-and-down play halfway between the axles, checked all the way round the crank rotation (Park Tool, https://www.parktool.com/en-int/blog/repair-help/chain-tension-for-tandem-front-end); for a synchronising chain, set it taut at the tightest point of the crank rotation (Sheldon Brown, https://www.sheldonbrown.com/synchain.html).
  - Choose the clamp to suit the frame: pinch-bolt eccentrics are easiest to adjust but are 'normally only used on steel frames because of the risk of breaking off the ears'; set screws are simple but dent the eccentric; wedge types must be kept greased or they corrode in place (Sheldon Brown, https://www.sheldonbrown.com/synchain.html).
  - Cabinet hinge mounting plates with a cam give about +/-2 mm of height adjustment (Blum catalogue 2022/2023, https://publications.blum.com/2022/catalogue/en/144/).
  - Car camber bolts with eccentric or offset washers can change camber by up to about two degrees (Wikipedia 'Camber angle', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camber_angle).
  - Setting gear backlash by turning an eccentric hub to change the centre distance shifts the tooth contact zone, can lower load capacity and efficiency, and causes start-up wear (Wikipedia 'Duplex worm', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duplex_worm).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - the 'eccentric nuts' you turn with a 10 mm spanner to snug up the wheels on a 3D printer such as the Ender 3
  - the eccentric bottom bracket that tensions the chain or belt on a tandem, single-speed or belt-drive bicycle
  - cam bolts used in car wheel alignment to set camber
  - kitchen cabinet hinge mounting plates with a small cam screw for moving the door up or down
- **when_to_use**: When you need a small, precise, lockable adjustment of where a wheel, pulley, sprocket, bearing or shaft sits: taking up play between rollers and a rail (V-slot carriages), setting chain or belt tension where the axles cannot slide (vertical bicycle dropouts, tandem timing chains, belt-drive bikes, motorcycles with single-sided swingarms), setting gear mesh or roller contact, or trimming alignment (car camber, cabinet doors, cam followers). It is compact, needs no slots, and holds its setting firmly once clamped.
- **when_not_to_use**: When tension must be kept up automatically as a belt or chain stretches: use a spring-loaded tensioner. When you need more travel than about twice the offset: use a slotted mount, sliding motor plate or jacking screw. When the part must not move sideways at all while you adjust it in one direction (the eccentric moves it on an arc): use a straight slide or screw adjuster. For precise, repeatable gear backlash: an eccentric centre-distance change disturbs tooth contact, so consider anti-backlash or duplex-worm gearing. In weak, soft or printed parts under heavy load: printed eccentrics can break and plastic creeps under the clamp.
- **environment_limits**: Corrosion can seize an eccentric in its housing: wedge-type bicycle eccentrics left unserviced 'can become corroded in place' (Sheldon Brown), and owners report having to tap stuck eccentric bottom brackets to turn them (Montague Bikes blog comments), so grease the outside of the eccentric. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Eccentrics set things that matter for safety, such as a car's wheel alignment, a motorcycle's chain tension or a bicycle's chain: re-tighten the clamp or pinch bolts to the maker's torque after adjusting, and check that the setting has not moved. When tightening the lock nut, hold the eccentric with a second tool so it does not turn and change the setting (VW Vortex forum, on an eccentric-mounted timing belt tensioner). Pinch-bolt eccentric shells on non-steel frames risk breaking the bolt ears (Sheldon Brown). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: (1) It slips and loses tension because the clamp was not tight enough or the load keeps trying to turn it; tighten to the proper torque and hold the eccentric while tightening. (2) It is seized by corrosion and will not turn; grease it at assembly (Sheldon Brown), and free a stuck one with gentle taps rather than by forcing the spanner, as bicycle owners report (Montague Bikes blog comments). (3) Set screws dent the eccentric, making fine re-adjustment difficult (Park Tool; Sheldon Brown). (4) Over-tightened V-wheels grind or develop flat spots; back it off until the carriage just runs without wobble. (5) Confusion because a full turn returns to the start: work in small steps and watch the effect, and use the thin-side mark. (7) A printed eccentric breaks at its thin neck when turned under load (Printables 79881). (8) Adjusting the eccentric also changes another dimension (pedal height, wheel toe) because it moves on an arc; check both after adjusting. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **related**: 
  - Belt tensioner (spring-loaded idler): keeps tension automatically as the belt stretches, instead of a set-and-lock adjustment
  - Eccentric cam lever (cam clamp, quick-release skewer): also uses an off-centre shape, but to clamp quickly with a lever rather than to set a position
  - Cam and follower: an off-centre or shaped disc that moves a follower continuously as it turns (the same geometry used for motion)
  - Slotted mount or jacking screw (adjustable centres): a straight, longer adjustment range; less compact
  - Lead screw and nut (incl. anti-backlash nut): fine straight-line adjustment with a screw
  - Dovetail slide with gib strip: takes up play in a slide with an adjustable strip instead of an eccentric
  - Kinematic coupling: locates parts exactly without any adjustment
  - Turnbuckle: sets tension in a rod, wire or cable by screwing
