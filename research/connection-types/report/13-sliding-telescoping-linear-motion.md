## 70 Telescoping tube twist lock (cam or collet twist lock)

*sliding, telescoping & linear motion.* Tubes that slide inside each other and lock at any length when you twist a section, by squeezing a split collet or turning a cam

### What it is

- **plain_name**: Two tubes that slide one inside the other and lock at any length when you twist them: the twist makes a plastic plug swell inside the tube, or makes a ring squeeze the inner tube, so friction holds them
- **term**: Telescoping tube twist lock (internal expander or eccentric-cam twist lock; external collet twist collar)
- **aliases**: 
  - twist lock / twist-lock pole
  - internal lock, internal expander lock or expanding plug lock (trekking poles)
  - twist collar lock or collet leg lock (camera tripods)
  - cam twist lock or eccentric twist lock (tent poles, curtain rods)
  - screw-to-lock telescoping tube
  - friction twist lock
  - Super Lock System (SLS) and Easy Lock System (ELS) (Leki (brand) names for its internal expander locks)
  - twist lock plug (Valco (brand) TL series)
  - twist-lock extension pole or whisker pole (painting, pool and boat poles)
- **family**: sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **other_families**: clamping, magnetic & friction connections, quick-release & tool-free
- **one_liner**: A lock for sliding tubes that holds them at any length by friction when you twist one section against the other (or turn a collar), and lets them slide again when you twist back.
- **complexity**: A small assembly that acts as one unit. Internal type: inner tube, a threaded rod or screw fixed in its end, a cone-shaped or split plastic expander (plug) that rides on the thread, and the outer tube (Leki: 'a hard plastic expander screws on to a threaded alloy spike'). Eccentric-cam type: an off-centre cam fixed to the inner tube and a loose off-centre ring or cam follower. External type: a threaded collar on the end of the outer tube, a split plastic ring (collet or 'C-ring') and the inner tube.
- **how_it_works**: Internal expander: turning one tube against the other screws the threaded rod into a cone-shaped plastic plug, which swells outward and presses on the inside wall of the outer tube; friction then stops the tubes sliding (Leki; Ryder-Walker; Wright patent: rotation 'will cause radial expansion of the collar' and 'frictional engagement' with the inner wall). Eccentric cam: an off-centre cam on the inner tube turns inside an off-centre ring; about a quarter turn (about 90 degrees) pushes the ring sideways against the outer tube wall and wedges it there (Levi and Quarberg patents; the printable tent-pole lock uses 'a circle offset by 1mm from center'). External collet: turning a threaded collar on the end of the outer tube squeezes a split plastic ring (a collet, 'C-ring') down onto the inner tube (Ulanzi; Wikipedia 'Collet'). Twisting back releases the squeeze so the tube slides.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: let it move (turn, slide or bend): the sections slide to change length, lock, and release by hand: a twist locks the length anywhere along the slide, and a twist back frees it
- **motion**: slide: in and out along the tube, over the length the overlap allows; when unlocked, round tubes can also turn (rotate+slide), and that turning is what works the lock
- **degrees_of_freedom**: Unlocked: 2 (slide along the tube and turn around it); the turn is used to lock. Locked: 0, held only by friction, so a big enough push, pull or twist can still make it slip.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): once tightened, pushing on the pole does not undo the thread or cam, so it stays locked by friction; a cam design gives 'a large multiple' of force for a modest twist (Levi and Quarberg, US5011319)
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): it only unlocks when someone twists the sections or the collar back
  - quick-release: no tools, but slower than a lever lock; a tripod twist collar needs about half a turn (Benro guide), an eccentric cam about a quarter turn (Levi and Quarberg, US5549407)
  - motion conversion (inside the lock only): the twist is turned into a sideways squeeze by a screw thread, cone or cam; no motion is passed on
- **holding_principle**: 
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): a swelling plug, off-centre cam or squeezed collet presses the tube walls together and friction holds the length
  - spring force (partly): the plastic expander or collet is squashed a little and pushes back like a spring, which keeps up the pressure
- **input_output**: not applicable (the twist is turned into a sideways squeeze inside the lock, but no motion is passed on)
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: twist to unlock and slide; the sections can usually be pulled fully apart past a 'stop' line for cleaning (Ryder-Walker)
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: it is adjusted every trip, but the plastic expanders wear or get polished and need replacing now and then (Leki FAQ: 'purchase new expanders as yours might be worn'; Backpacker: high-mileage hikers may need to service them yearly)
- **tools_needed**: None to use. To service: a cloth or old toothbrush and a light grease (Backpacker). To make your own: a saw and file for the tubes, and glue to fix the printed body into the inner tube so it can't turn (the printable tent-pole design uses Araldite).
- **seal**: none as a rule: dirt, sand and water get onto the threads and friction surfaces and make it slip or seize (Backpacker; Wright patent: 'sand or grit entering the mechanical apparatus causing seizure'). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **load**: Light to medium, depending on size. It is strongest against a push or pull along the tube (that is the job), but the length is held only by friction, so an overload makes it slip rather than break. It is weakest in twist: turning the sections the 'unlock' way loosens it. The printable eccentric tent-pole lock 'easily holds body mass' in its largest size (Flex, Printables). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **adjustable**: yes: the length can be set anywhere along the slide (stepless adjustment). How hard it grips depends on how far you twist it, and on worn or dirty expanders.
- **play_and_precision**: No play when locked, because the plug or collet takes up the gap between the tubes; a long overlap is still needed to stop wobble (the printable PVC collet adds slide-on shims: 'Two appear sufficient to eliminate nearly all wobble with approximately 12" of extension', jslaker). The length is not repeatable unless you mark it (many poles have a printed scale). The tubes must be round and a close fit; it tolerates almost no sideways offset or angle.
- **access_needed**: end of a tube or shaft: the expander or cam is fitted into the end of the inner tube, and a collar goes on the end of the outer tube; once built it is worked from the outside by twisting.
- **materials**: Tubes: aluminium, carbon fibre, steel or PVC. Expanders and collets: hard or soft plastics, nylon or rubber (Leki; poolpole.com); threaded rod or screw: aluminium alloy or steel; collars: plastic or aluminium. Printed versions: PETG for the collet and forcing cone, PLA for handles and retainers (jslaker). Best with smooth, round, straight tubes; poor with rough or dented tubes, and with welded tube that has a seam inside (Speedy Metals).
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate: easy to buy (replacement expanders and twist-lock plugs are sold), but a printed collet or cam 'needs to be a relatively precise fit for the tubes, so it pretty much has to be generated for your material' (sdk, Printables).

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: replacement expanders, twist-lock plugs and complete twist-lock poles
  - 3D print (FDM): printed collets, forcing cones, eccentric cams and collars for bought aluminium, carbon or PVC tubes
  - CNC: turned metal collets and threaded collars (lathe work) *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **print_in_place**: partly: a mostly print-in-place telescoping webcam stand that 'screw-locks' to height exists, but its top and cam screw are printed separately (BRE3DLOVE, Printables). Most printed twist locks are separate parts fitted to bought tubes.
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Measure your tubes first and print a test piece: the printed tent-pole cam is 'just a circle offset by 1mm from center', its outside diameter is made 'exactly the same size as ID of the larger tube so it needs a tiny squeeze to fit', and the gap between the cam's hole and the body is 'about 1mm but you may need to adjust'; 'the smaller the tube diameters, the harder to get the tolerance correct' (Flex, Printables 232086). 2) Fix the printed body to the inner tube with glue (Araldite) or it 'will rotate when trying to lock the cam' (Flex). 3) Print the parts that take the squeeze solid: 'Forcing cone and collet printed at 100% infill PETG'; handles can be about 35% infill (jslaker, Printables 145516). 4) Use a parametric model (e.g. the OpenSCAD collet by sdk) so you can regenerate it for your tube sizes; its thin clamping 'wings' are delicate until assembled. 5) Allow at least 0.3 mm clearance for parts that must turn or slide (Prusa), and check vertical holes, which FDM prints undersize; drill them to size if they matter (Hubs). 7) Choose PETG over PLA for anything that stays tight: PLA 'gets soft and deforms at temperatures over 60 °C' and degrades in UV (Prusa PLA), while PETG suits use below 80 °C (Prusa PETG); even so, a PETG clamp can 'lose compression over hours or days' (goodprints3d). 8) Leave long overlap: the ultralight printed tripod warns to keep the inner tube inside the outer one by at least the length of the lock housing 'otherwise it won't be rigid enough' (danec, Printables 911528). 9) Put a stop on the inner tube so the lock cannot be pulled out or slammed against the end: impact against the stop 'can cause irreversible damage' (Wright patent). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Trekking-pole expander plugs (twist-lock expanders) sold by tube diameter, e.g. 14-18 mm (Leki Easy Lock and Super Lock expanders (brand)); twist-lock plugs made to join two given tube IDs (Valco TL series (brand), e.g. Search terms: 'twist lock expander', 'telescopic pole twist lock', 'twist lock plug', 'tripod leg twist lock', 'internal expander trekking pole'. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - An eccentric-cam twist lock needs only about a quarter turn (about 90 degrees) to lock (Levi and Quarberg, US5549407); a tripod twist collar about half a turn (180 degrees) to release (Benro tripod guide).
  - Printable eccentric cam: offset the cam circle about 1 mm from the tube centre, make the cam's outside diameter equal to the outer tube's inside diameter, and leave about 1 mm between cam bore and body, then tune (Flex, Adjustable twist lock tent pole, Printables 232086).
  - Glue the fixed half of the lock into its tube, or it will spin instead of locking (Flex, Printables 232086).
  - Overlap: FRC robotics teams reserve about one quarter to one fifth of each stage's length for overlap (the older rule was 30%), and wobble at the tip grows with extended length divided by overlap: 0.01 in of play with 20 in out and 4 in of overlap gives about 0.04 in at the tip (Chief Delphi thread 'Telescoping Tubes Overlap').
  - Tube fit: allow at least 0.010 in between outer and inner diameters for steel DOM tube, and even then test, because many tubes have a weld seam inside (Speedy Metals); a supplier suggests 0.33-0.38 mm total clearance for 1 in aluminium tube in 7/8 in tube (Chalco, vendor guide).
  - Never oil the friction parts: grease the threads very lightly with a non-running lubricant, 'DO NOT use WD40 or oil', and roughen polished plugs and tube walls with sandpaper to restore grip (Backpacker, 'How to Fix Trekking Poles').
  - Older cam twist locks held only as hard as the user could twist; a cam with mechanical advantage turns a modest twist into a large locking force (Levi and Quarberg, US5011319).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Trekking and walking poles that you twist to lengthen (internal expander locks)
  - Camera tripod legs with twist collars (Benro, Really Right Stuff and others)
  - Telescopic painting, window-cleaning and pool poles, and boat whisker poles (Forespar 'internal twist lock device')
  - Tent poles and extendable curtain rods with a small eccentric cam inside
- **when_to_use**: Use a twist lock when you want tubes that set to any length (not just fixed holes), look clean with nothing sticking out, and don't snag: trekking poles, tent and awning poles, light stands, camera or phone tripods, extendable curtain rods, pinch rods, and printed or PVC 'extension' projects. It is cheap, light and slim, and can be hidden entirely inside the tube, so wires or cords can still run past some designs (the tubular cam patent routes wires through the middle).
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid it when the length must be changed quickly with gloves or cold hands, or when you can't see whether it is locked (a lever lock (flip lock) shows its state and is easier to work: Ulanzi; Backpacker). Avoid it for heavy or safety-critical loads, for loads that twist the tube, or for vibration: use a spring button lock or a pin through holes for set positions, or a set screw or bolted clamp for a permanent setting. Avoid it in sandy, salty or muddy places unless you can clean it often (use a lever lock or a sealed design). A square or keyed tube can't use it, because the tubes must turn to lock.
- **environment_limits**: Water, salt and grit corrode the threads and polish or gum up the plug, so it slips (Backpacker); sand or grit can seize it (Wright patent). Twist-type locks can freeze in the cold or be overtightened until they jam (Tomm patent: 'known to become overtightened and freeze in position'; Johnson and O'Connor patent: twisting devices can 'strip, jam, or in cold weather, freeze'). Heat and sun: Benro advises not leaving a tripod in the sun or heat. Printed parts: PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV (Prusa); PETG suits below about 80 °C (Prusa); plastic expanders and printed collets relax (creep) under constant squeeze, so they need retightening (goodprints3d).
- **safety_notes**: It can slip suddenly if worn, wet or not tight enough, so don't trust your full weight or an expensive camera to it without testing (Benro: make sure leg locks are tightly engaged before mounting gear, and remove the camera before adjusting leg sections). Overtightening can crack the collar (poolpole.com) or jam the lock, and a jammed lock needs high twisting force that strains the wrists (Tomm patent). Pinch point where the tubes slide together. None of the stored-energy hazards of springs.
- **common_failures**: 1) Slipping under load because the plug is worn, polished, wet or greasy: clean, dry, lightly roughen the plug and tube wall, and keep oil off (Backpacker). 2) Jammed or seized from overtightening, corrosion or grit: twist firmly but not hard, clean after sandy or salty use, and grease the threads lightly (Backpacker; Wright patent; Benro). 3) Expander spins with the tube and won't tighten (worn threads or plug): replace the expander (Leki). 4) Pulled apart past the stop, or lost expander: many poles have a 'Stop Max' line; don't extend past it (Ryder-Walker); printed replacement expanders exist (Komperdell replacement on Printables). 6) Cracked collar or collet from overtightening (poolpole.com). 7) Printed versions: the body turns in the tube if not glued, and loose tolerances stop the cam gripping (Flex). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **related**: 
  - Telescoping tube lever lock (flip lock): an outside clamp collar squeezed by a flip lever; faster, works with gloves and shows whether it is locked, but sticks out and can snag
  - Spring button lock (snap button): a spring button pops through holes, so it locks positively (by shape) but only at set lengths
  - Keyless locking bush (taper-lock bush): the same idea of a cone or taper wedging parts together, used to fix hubs to shafts
  - Internal tube anchors (star nut, expansion plug): an expanding plug that is tightened once to fix a part in a tube end, not used as a sliding lock
  - Eccentric cam lever (cam clamp): the off-centre cam principle, turned by a lever instead of by twisting the tube
  - Self-locking wedge (wedge clamp): holds by a shallow wedge, as the cone inside a twist lock does
  - Shaft-to-hub joints (clamp hub, set screw): a set screw through the outer tube is the simplest 'any length' lock but needs a tool and dents the tube
  - Drawer slide (telescopic ball-bearing slide): a sliding telescope with no lock, for smooth motion

## 71 Telescoping tube lever lock (flip lock)

*sliding, telescoping & linear motion.* A clamp collar with a flip lever that squeezes an outer tube onto an inner one, as on tripod legs and trekking poles

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A ring with a slot and a little flip-up lever at the end of the outer tube: flip the lever open and the inner tube slides; snap it shut and the ring squeezes tight so the tubes can't move
- **term**: Telescoping tube lever lock (flip lock; cam-lever clamp collar)
- **aliases**: 
  - flip lock / flip-lock leg
  - lever lock / external lever lock
  - flick lock; FlickLock (Black Diamond (brand))
  - Speed Lock (Leki (brand))
  - DynaLock (MSR (brand))
  - snap lever (tripod leg lock)
  - cam lock, cam-lever collar or cam clamp (for tubes)
  - quick-release clamp (for posts and poles)
  - leg lock lever (tripods, saw stands)
  - seatpost quick-release clamp (the same idea on a bicycle seat post)
- **family**: sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **other_families**: over-centre, toggle & cam clamps, clamping, magnetic & friction connections, quick-release & tool-free
- **one_liner**: A split clamp ring on the end of the outer tube with a lever that snaps shut over centre, squeezing the outer tube onto the inner one so the length is held at any position until you flip the lever open.
- **complexity**: A few parts acting as one unit: a clamp collar (a ring with a slot or split, fixed to the end of the outer tube), a lever with a cam shape at its pivot end, a pivot pin or screw through the collar's two ears, a tension screw, nut or dial that sets how tight it closes, and the two tubes (the outer tube's end is often slotted too).
- **how_it_works**: The collar is a ring with a gap in it, so it can be squeezed smaller (the same way a bicycle seat tube has 'a vertical slit' so a ring can squeeze it 'without crumpling', Wikipedia 'Seatpost'). The lever pivots on a pin or screw across the two ears beside the gap. Its end is a cam (an off-centre curve), so closing the lever pulls the ears together and squeezes the collar, and the outer tube, onto the inner tube; friction then holds the length ('close the lever and it grips the tube firmly', Leki). The lever swings just past its tightest point (over-centre, past 'a top dead center pivot point', Easton patent US8006711B2), so the squeeze itself holds it shut. A small screw, nut or dial on the pivot sets how tight it closes, so it can be retightened as parts wear.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: let it move (turn, slide or bend): the inner tube slides to change length, lock, and release by hand: snapping the lever shut locks the length anywhere along the slide; flipping it open frees it
- **motion**: slide: in and out along the tube while the lever is open, over the length the overlap allows (round tubes can also turn, so rotate+slide when open)
- **degrees_of_freedom**: Unlocked: 2 for round tubes (slide along and turn around), 1 for square or keyed tubes (slide only). Locked: 0, held by friction.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - over-centre (snaps past a point and stays): the cam lever passes its tightest point and the clamping force keeps it closed (Easton patent US8006711B2: 'a top dead center pivot point' gives 'a positive lock condition')
  - quick-release: one flick opens or closes it, with no tools, and it works with gloves on (Ulanzi; Backpacker)
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): pushing on the pole does not open the lever; only flipping it does
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): the over-centre lever and friction hold without any effort from the user
- **holding_principle**: friction or clamping (force-fit): the squeezed collar presses the tube walls together and friction holds the length
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **removability**: tool-free quick release: flip the lever to unlock; the tubes can then slide or be pulled apart
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: it is opened and closed at every adjustment; clamp force drops as parts settle and wear, so the tension screw needs occasional retightening (Leki; Backpacker)
- **tools_needed**: None to use. To set the clamping force: a screwdriver, Allen key or nut driver on the tension screw (Benro: a 3 mm Allen key; Manfrotto 055: an 8 mm nut driver; Black Diamond FlickLock: a Phillips screwdriver, per Enwild), or no tool at all on designs with a finger dial (MSR DynaLock).
- **seal**: none: sand or grit in the lever joint can jam it (Ulanzi: 'a single grain of sand in a lever joint'), and dirt or polishing inside the collar makes it slip (Backpacker)
- **load**: Medium. Brand claims for trekking-pole lever locks: Leki Speed Lock Plus holds 'more than 75 kg' and Speed Lock 2 Plus 'more than 90 kg' per lock (Leki UK blog); Speed Lock 2 a minimum of 95 kg (Leki help centre). A vendor explainer gives 'up to 15+ kg' for general telescoping poles (poolpole.com, no test method). The friction resists both sliding and twisting; bending is carried by the overlap between tubes. An overload makes it slip rather than break. Too much clamping can crush a thin or carbon-fibre tube (Manfrotto parts blog: carbon fibre is vulnerable to squeezing forces).
- **adjustable**: yes, twice over: the length is stepless (any position along the slide), and the clamping force is set with the tension screw or dial (Leki; Benro; MSR: 'adjust the clamping force on the fly').
- **play_and_precision**: No play when closed, as long as the overlap is long enough (the ultralight printed tripod warns the inner tube must overlap by at least the lock housing length or 'it won't be rigid enough', danec). The length is not indexed, so it only returns to the same length if you use a printed scale or mark. It tolerates small changes in tube size because the tension can be adjusted (Easton patent: adjustable for 'pole members of different cross-section'), but the tubes must be round (or matching shapes) and a close fit.
- **access_needed**: end of a tube or shaft: the collar fits over the open end of the outer tube; once built it is worked from the outside with one hand.
- **materials**: Collar: moulded plastic or aluminium alloy (Leki Speed Lock 2 uses 'an enhanced alloy locking collar' for more holding force); lever: plastic or anodised aluminium; cam: stainless steel (MSR); pivot and tension screw: steel. Tubes: aluminium or carbon fibre (carbon needs gentler clamping). Printed versions: PETG or ABS collars and levers with steel bolts (Jori; Bebe, Printables).
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate: a printed split collar with a bought bolt is easy, but a lever that snaps shut over centre with the right force, and plastic that keeps its grip, takes some tuning. Reusing a bicycle seatpost quick-release clamp or buying a replacement lever is the easy route.

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: complete lever locks, replacement levers and collars, bicycle seatpost quick-release clamps
  - 3D print (FDM): split collars, levers and replacement lever-lock parts, used with steel pivot bolts
  - CNC: machined aluminium collars and levers
- **print_in_place**: no: the collar and lever are printed as separate parts and joined with a pin or bolt; the lever usually needs supports (Jori, Printables 110310).
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Use a metal bolt or pin as the pivot and tension screw: Jori's 22 mm post clamp taps M5 threads into the printed ball head and clamp and uses an M5 x 50 screw (Printables 110310); Bebe's clamp uses an M6 furniture connector bolt (Printables 860826); the ultralight tripod uses M3 hardware (danec, Printables 911528). Add a tension nut or screw so you can tune the snap-shut force. 2) Give the slot in the collar a generous rounded end: 'a narrow split with a sharp inside corner creates a crack starter' (goodprints3d). 3) Orient the collar so continuous extrusion paths go round the ring and carry the hoop tension, rather than relying on layer bonds (goodprints3d; Hubs: FDM parts are weaker across layers). 4) Spread bolt loads with wide ears and washers or metal sleeves, because a screw bearing on a small printed ledge sinks in and makes the clamp go loose (goodprints3d). 5) Use tough material and enough walls: PETG for levers (Bogen and Harbor Freight replacement levers, Printables), ABS (Bebe), and more shells or infill for leg locks 'since it needs to be somewhat strong' (Pete Prodoehl, Printables 40021); default slicer settings in PLA gave 'a very flexible lever' (gjpc, Printables 34970). 6) Plan for creep: plastic under a constant squeeze relaxes, so a PETG clamp can 'lose compression over hours or days' (goodprints3d); nylon recovers better; PLA softens above 60 °C (Prusa) and PETG suits use below 80 °C (Prusa). 7) Leave at least 0.3 mm clearance on moving parts such as the lever pivot (Prusa), and drill critical holes to size because FDM prints vertical holes undersize (Hubs). 8) Keep plenty of overlap: at least the length of the lock housing (danec). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Trekking-pole and tripod replacement lever locks sold by tube diameter (e.g. Leki Speed Lock 2 lever with its thumb-wheel tension adjuster, sold for 18/16 mm and other pole sizes (brand)); bicycle seatpost quick-release clamps (seat clamp with cam lever) sold by seat-tube diameter; cam levers and quick-release skewers; eccentric clamping levers; M3-M6 bolts, nuts and washers for printed collars. Search terms: 'flip lock', 'lever lock replacement', 'tripod leg lock', 'seatpost quick release clamp', 'cam lever clamp', 'quick release clamp tube'.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Close the lever past its tightest point (top dead centre) so it locks positively and stays shut (Easton patent US8006711B2).
  - A cam acting on both ears of the split ring clamps better than one acting on one side only, which 'occasionally proves to be inadequate for intensive use' (Rossignol patent US6676329B2).
  - Adjust clamping force in small steps and test after each: Black Diamond suggests a quarter to half turn at a time (Enwild); for a Manfrotto 055 leg, 'never more than a third of a turn' (ManfrottoTripodParts); Leki: 'turn the tension screw a little tighter, then test again; if it will not close, loosen it slightly'.
  - A correctly set lever needs 'a bit of force to close' (Enwild, on Black Diamond FlickLocks); if it suddenly gets hard to close, loosen it rather than forcing it (Leki).
  - Benchmarks for a good trekking-pole lever lock: more than 75 kg (Speed Lock Plus) to more than 90 kg (Speed Lock 2 Plus) holding force per lock (Leki UK blog, brand claims).
  - Mark and respect a minimum insertion (maximum extension) depth, as bicycle seatposts do (Wikipedia 'Seatpost'); FRC robotics teams reserve about one quarter to one fifth of each stage's length for overlap (Chief Delphi).
  - Don't overtighten: it can bend or break the clamp or strip its threads (Wikipedia 'Seatpost'), and on carbon tubes 'wear out and damage the snap lever and maybe even crack the leg lock' (ManfrottoTripodParts).
  - If a lever lock slips, tighten the adjustment screw (thread-lock it if it keeps loosening), then lightly sand the inside of the collar and the outside of the inner tube; don't oil it (Backpacker).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Trekking poles with flip-up levers (Black Diamond FlickLock, Leki Speed Lock (brands))
  - Camera tripod and light-stand legs with flip locks
  - A bicycle seat post held by a quick-release clamp
  - Folding work stands and saw-stand legs, and sun-umbrella pole clamps (printed replacement levers for these are on Printables)
- **when_to_use**: Use a lever lock when the length must be changed often and fast, by feel, with gloves or cold hands, and when you want to see at a glance whether it is locked (Ulanzi; Backpacker; Leki). Good for tripod and light-stand legs, trekking and camera poles, adjustable stands and robot or rig frames where you tune a length on the spot, and for fixing a post or pole in a holder at any height. It holds more reliably than a twist lock, and its tension can be set with a screw or dial.
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid it where a lever sticking out would snag or break (packs, crowded mechanisms): a twist lock is slimmer (Ulanzi). Avoid it for exact, repeatable set lengths or for loads that must never slip: use a spring button lock or a pin through holes. Avoid it on thin-walled or carbon tubes unless the clamp force is controlled, because squeezing can crush them. For a setting that is almost never changed, a bolted clamp collar or set screw is simpler. For long-term constant clamping in hot places, a printed plastic collar may creep loose: use metal or check it often.
- **environment_limits**: Grit or sand in the lever joint can jam it (Ulanzi); dirt, polishing and wear make it slip (Backpacker); clean and dry after wet, sandy or salty use (Benro). Temperature changes make pole materials swell or shrink slightly, which 'can lead to your pole sections slipping or collapsing' unless the tension is readjusted (MSR). Vibration can loosen the tension screw over time: use thread-locker (Backpacker). Printed collars: PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV (Prusa); PETG suits below about 80 °C (Prusa) but still relaxes under constant squeeze (goodprints3d).
- **safety_notes**: A lock set too loose can let a tripod or pole collapse under load: check it holds before mounting a camera or leaning on it (Benro: ensure leg locks are tightly engaged before mounting gear). Overtightening can crack the collar or crush a carbon tube (ManfrottoTripodParts). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: 1) Slips because the tension has loosened or the tube is polished or gunky: tighten the tension screw a quarter turn at a time and test, thread-lock it, and lightly sand the collar and inner tube (Backpacker; Enwild; Leki). 2) Lever won't close or is very stiff: tension too high; back it off (Leki). 3) Broken or lost lever, often from knocks in transport: replace it, or print one (gjpc's tripod lever, Bogen and Harbor Freight replacement levers, Printables). 4) Jammed by sand in the lever joint (Ulanzi): rinse and clean. 5) Cracked collar or crushed tube from overtightening, especially on carbon fibre (ManfrottoTripodParts). 6) Snagging the lever on gear. 7) Printed collars creeping loose over hours or days, or cracking at a sharp slot corner (goodprints3d): use a rounded slot end, washers and a tougher material.
- **related**: 
  - Telescoping tube twist lock (cam or collet twist lock): locks by twisting; slimmer and cheaper, but slower, harder with gloves and more prone to slipping or jamming
  - Spring button lock (snap button): locks positively at set lengths through holes; no slipping, but no in-between lengths
  - Eccentric cam lever (cam clamp, quick-release skewer): the same over-centre cam lever, used to clamp wheels and plates rather than tubes
  - Toggle clamp: another over-centre clamp, using a linkage instead of a cam
  - Clamp-on tube fittings (slip-on structural fittings): tube connectors that also clamp by squeezing, usually with a screw
  - Shaft-to-hub joints (clamp hub): a split hub squeezed by a screw, the bolted version of the same squeeze
  - Quick-release plate (dovetail clamp): a lever or knob clamp for quick camera mounting
  - Indexable clamping lever (ratchet handle): a lever whose handle can be repositioned, used on screw clamps

## 72 Spring button lock (snap button, push-button tube lock)

*sliding, telescoping & linear motion.* A spring-loaded button in an inner tube pops out through holes in the outer tube to lock set lengths; press it to slide

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A springy metal clip hidden inside the thinner tube pushes a little button out through a hole; slide the outer tube over it and the button pops out through one of a row of holes to lock the length, and you press the button in to slide again
- **term**: Spring button lock (snap button, spring button clip for telescoping tubes)
- **aliases**: 
  - snap button / snap-button
  - spring button / spring button clip
  - push-button spring clip / push button tube lock
  - button clip / tube button clip
  - tubing button (McMaster-Carr catalogue wording)
  - V-clip, V-spring clip or V-shaped button clip (after the shape of the spring)
  - single-button or double-button clip; single end or double end snap button
  - tube locking pin clip / spring lock button (tent poles, kayak paddles)
  - snap-button ferrule (kayak paddles)
  - auto-depressing or ramp spring button (one-way version)
  - Valco snap-button (brand)
- **family**: sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **other_families**: indexing & detents, quick-release & tool-free, snap & clip fits, locks, latches & catches
- **one_liner**: A spring-loaded button inside the inner tube that snaps out through holes in the outer tube, locking two sliding tubes at set lengths until you press the button in.
- **complexity**: A few parts: a bent strip of spring steel (the spring leg, often V- or U-shaped) with one or two buttons on it, fitted inside the inner tube with its button poking out through a hole; and a row of holes in the outer tube. Some versions have a solid button head and a separate music-wire spring, or a plastic button (Valco catalogue).
- **how_it_works**: The spring clip is squeezed and pushed into the inner tube until its button springs out through a hole near the end ('Install them in the smaller tube, then slip the larger tube over the smaller tube until the button pops into a hole', McMaster-Carr). With the button out through both tubes, the tubes can't slide or turn, because the button physically blocks them (a shape lock). To change length, press the button flush with a finger, slide the outer tube, and the spring pushes the button out again when it reaches the next hole. Ramp-headed buttons press themselves in when the tube slides one way and lock the other way ('one-way locking self-depressing operation', Valco catalogue).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - click into set positions: the tubes lock at each hole, so the length is chosen from fixed steps
  - lock, and release by hand: press the button to unlock, and it relocks by itself at the next hole
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): the tubes slide between positions
  - join and hold: also used to join two tube parts or snap on attachments and handles (Valco: 'adjustable legs, interchangeable attachments, common handle')
- **motion**: slide: along the tube between holes, only while the button is held in; locked (none) otherwise
- **degrees_of_freedom**: Unlocked (button pressed): 2 for round tubes (slide and turn), 1 for square or keyed tubes. Locked: 0, apart from the small rattle allowed by the clearance between button and hole.
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - indexing (clicks into set positions): the button snaps out at each hole, giving fixed, repeatable lengths
  - quick-release: one finger presses the button; no tools
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): pushing or pulling on the tubes does not free the button; it has to be pressed in
  - one-way (moves freely one way, locks the other): only in the ramp-headed or auto-depressing versions (Valco RR and RAA ramp buttons; Testrite T-131: 'automatically' depress the spring button head in one direction)
  - self-aligning or self-centring: only when the tubes can't turn (square or keyed tubes), so buttons and holes line up by themselves (Testrite: 'Non-rotating inside tubes' make 'lining up the spring buttons with the holes automatic')
- **holding_principle**: shape (form-fit): the button sitting in the hole blocks sliding and turning, spring force: the spring leg keeps the button pushed out into the hole
- **input_output**: not applicable
- **power**: passive (no power needed)
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: it relocks at every adjustment; springs slowly tire and hole edges can wear, and plain steel clips rust in wet use (Bending Branches)
- **tools_needed**: None to use. To fit: a drill for the holes (and a deburring tool), calipers to measure the inner tube's inside diameter; 'Drilling is required but adhesive is not' (Rock West). For thick-walled tube, Valco suggests drilling through and recessing the tube wall with a 1 in (25.4 mm) ball mill so the button can be pressed flush.
- **seal**: none: the holes are open, so water, sand and grit get in, and debris and rust are its main maintenance problems (Bending Branches)
- **load**: Light to medium. Holes are stress raisers and buttons can break under sideways (off-axis) loads (poolpole.com, vendor page). A supplier recommends spring buttons for 'lightweight equipment' and a pin through holes for heavy-duty or vibration use (Chalco). Plastic snap buttons are 'for use in lighter load applications' (Valco catalogue). Unlike friction locks it does not slip, so it holds its length until the button or tube breaks. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **adjustable**: yes, in steps only: the length can be set at each hole; the step size is the hole spacing, fixed when the holes are drilled.
- **play_and_precision**: Noticeable play: the button must be smaller than its hole, so the tubes can rattle and click. A crutch clicks because 'the push button used to adjust the length of the crutch is not snug in its hole' (Heinsalu); a tapered button whose big end is larger than the hole wedges in and removes the rattle (Temco patent US3947140A), and a rubber band or spring pulling the tubes one way keeps the button on one side of its hole (Heinsalu). Repeatability is very good: it always returns to the same holes. It needs the holes lined up exactly, both along and around the tube; tubes that can't turn line up automatically (Testrite).
- **access_needed**: end of a tube or shaft: the clip is pushed into the open end of the inner tube; once fitted it is worked with one finger from the outside.
- **materials**: Clips: heat-treated 1050 spring steel with zinc plating as standard, 410 stainless steel (magnetic, resists wear and corrosion) or 301 stainless steel (most corrosion resistant) (McMaster-Carr; Valco catalogue); solid button heads in steel, brass, aluminium, stainless or nylon on music-wire springs; plastic buttons in Delrin, nylon or PVC for lighter loads (Valco catalogue). Tubes: round, square or rectangular aluminium, steel, PVC or carbon-fibre tube (Valco; Rock West; McMaster-Carr).
- **maker_difficulty**: easy: buy a clip sized to the inner tube's inside diameter and drill the holes; the main skills are drilling holes that line up and choosing a button tall enough for the tube wall.

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: steel or stainless snap-button clips, fitted into bought tube
  - 3D print (FDM): printed spring clips and buttons (light duty), and printed tubes or sleeves with holes for bought clips
  - CNC: accurate rows of holes, and recessing thick tube walls
  - sheet metal: the clip itself is a formed spring-steel strip (made by suppliers; a steel strip from a binder clip can be reused)
- **print_in_place**: no: the spring clip is a separate part that is squeezed into the tube; printed clips are printed flat and then inserted (BlueThunder05, Printables 942717).
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Best combination: print the tubes, sleeves or brackets and buy a steel snap-button clip; plastic makes a poor long-term spring ('PLA makes for a very poor spring material', Hackaday). 2) Choose the clip by the inside diameter of the inner tube (McMaster-Carr; Rock West) and make sure the button sticks out far enough past the outer wall to press: Rock West warns that with a short button in 0.062 in walls 'large fingers may have difficult time depressing button'. Thick printed walls need a taller button or a recess around the hole (Valco: recess thick walls with a 1 in ball mill). 3) If you print the spring clip, print it lying flat so the springy leg's layers run along its length; Hubs advises avoiding snap-fit cantilevers 'built vertically in the Z direction', with at least 1 mm thickness at the base, a fillet of at least half the thickness and at least 5 mm width, in a tough material such as PETG, ABS or nylon rather than PLA. The pool-pole clip was 'modified so it could be printed flat' (BlueThunder05). 4) A printed spring stays squeezed inside the tube all the time, so it will creep and lose force; designs work best when the spring is relaxed most of the time (Hackaday), so check and replace printed clips. 6) Give printed tubes a flat, rib or square section so they can't turn and the holes always line up (Testrite's non-rotating tubes). 7) Allow at least 0.3 mm (Prusa) to 0.5 mm (Hubs, FDM) sliding clearance between printed tubes. 9) Heat: PLA softens above about 60 °C (Prusa); PETG suits use below about 80 °C (Prusa). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Snap buttons / tubing buttons / button clips: single-button and double-button (opposite side or same side), straight-leg and dog-leg (McMaster-Carr); single end (A-style) and double end (D-style) snap buttons, solid-head wire-spring buttons, ramp (one-way) buttons and plastic buttons (Valco (brand) catalogue); stainless button clips for composite tubes sized by tube ID (Rock West Composites (brand)); auto-depressing spring buttons (Testrite (brand) T-131); ready-made telescoping tube sets with spring-button locks (Testrite F style, round 1/2 in to 1-3/8 in). Search terms: 'snap button', 'spring button clip', 'tubing button', 'V clip tube', 'push button spring clip', 'kayak paddle spring clip'.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Pick the clip by the inner tube's inside diameter: e.g. button 0.19 in for 0.375-0.625 in ID, 0.25 in for 0.50-0.85 in, 0.312 in for 0.59-1.25 in, 0.365 in for 1.00-1.50 in and 0.50 in for 1.14-2.25 in (Rock West Composites button clips).
  - Catalogue range: round tube ID 3/8 to 1 7/8 in and square tube inside width 5/16 to 1 3/8 in, button heights 3/16 to 3/8 in, leg lengths 1 1/4 to 3 1/2 in (McMaster-Carr 'Tubing Buttons').
  - The button must stick out well past the outer tube wall so a finger can press it; short buttons in 0.062 in walls are hard to press (Rock West); in thick-walled tube, recess the wall around the hole (Valco catalogue).
  - Button clips work best with two tubes, which can then collapse fully inside each other (Rock West Composites).
  - Keep the tubes from turning (square, rectangular or keyed tubes) so buttons and holes line up by themselves (Testrite).
  - Remove rattle with a tapered button whose large end is bigger than the hole (Temco patent US3947140A) or by preloading the tubes one way, e.g. with an elastic band (Heinsalu, 'How to stop crutches from clicking').
  - Use spring buttons for light, portable equipment and a full pin through holes for heavy loads or vibration (Chalco, vendor guide); plastic buttons only for lighter loads (Valco).
  - Overlap: FRC robotics teams reserve about one quarter to one fifth of each stage's length for overlap (Chief Delphi). *(unverified parts omitted)*

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Height-adjustable crutches (press the button, slide, click) (Heinsalu)
  - Two-piece kayak paddles with a snap-button joint (ferrule) (Bending Branches)
  - Tent and canopy poles (Tent & Table push button spring clips)
  - Telescopic pool-cleaning poles and adjustable furniture or equipment legs (Valco; Printables pool-pole clip)
- **when_to_use**: Use a spring button when you want a few fixed lengths that are always the same, a lock that cannot slip, and one-finger operation: crutches, adjustable legs and stands, tent and canopy poles, paddles and handles that come apart, robot or rig frames with set sizes, and quick-fit attachments on the end of a tube. It is cheap, light, needs no adhesive and is easy to fit with a drill.
- **when_not_to_use**: Don't use it when you need any length in between (use a twist lock or lever lock), when rattle is unacceptable (use a clamp, or a tapered or preloaded button), or for heavy, shock or vibration loads and anything where people could be hurt if the button shears (use a through-pin such as a ball-lock pin, or a bolt). Avoid plain steel clips in salt water (use 301 or 410 stainless). Avoid it where holes would weaken a thin or composite tube too much.
- **environment_limits**: Water and salt rust plain steel clips, and grit gets in through the holes (Bending Branches); stainless versions resist corrosion, 301 most (McMaster-Carr). Plastic buttons don't rust (Valco). Printed clips and tubes: PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV (Prusa); PETG suits below about 80 °C (Prusa); printed springs creep and lose force over time (Hackaday). Vibration makes a loose button rattle and wear its hole (Heinsalu; Temco patent).
- **safety_notes**: Make sure the button has fully popped out before loading (walkers, crutches, stands). The spring clip is under tension while you squeeze it into the tube and can spring out. Holes are stress concentrations, so a heavily loaded tube can crack at a hole (poolpole.com, vendor page). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: 1) Rattle and clicking from clearance between button and hole (Heinsalu): use a tapered button (Temco patent) or preload the tubes. 2) Hard to press because the button is too short for the wall thickness (Rock West): choose a taller button or recess the wall (Valco). 3) Holes don't line up because the tubes rotated: use square or keyed tubes (Testrite), or drill both tubes together. 4) Rust or grit jamming the button (Bending Branches): use stainless and rinse after use. 6) Button or clip breaking under side loads (poolpole.com) or the spring losing force: replace the clip; printed clips wear out sooner (Hackaday). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **related**: 
  - Telescoping tube twist lock (cam or collet twist lock): stepless friction lock worked by twisting; can slip
  - Telescoping tube lever lock (flip lock): stepless friction clamp with a flip lever; faster to set any length but can slip
  - Quick-release pin (ball-lock pin): a separate pin pushed through holes and locked by balls; much stronger, but it is a loose part that can be lost
  - Indexing plunger (pull-knob index pin): a spring pin in a threaded body that drops into holes; pulled out by a knob instead of pressed in
  - Ball detent (spring plunger): a spring ball that clicks into dimples; gives a light hold, not a lock
  - Spring bar (spring-loaded telescoping pin): a tiny spring-loaded telescoping pin, as in watch straps
  - Snap pin (split-leg push-in pin, rabbit clip): a printable pin held in by springy legs
  - Tool-free fasteners (hitch pin and R-clip): a plain pin through holes kept in by a clip, the simplest step lock
  - Bayonet mount (push-and-twist lock): another way to lock tubes at set positions, by a twist into a slot

## 73 Dovetail slide with gib strip (dovetail ways)

*sliding, telescoping & linear motion.* A carriage with angled edges that slides along a matching angled track, with a tapered strip (gib) that is adjusted to take out any play

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A sliding table whose edges are cut at a slant so it hooks under a matching slanted track and can only slide one way, with a thin adjustable strip squeezed in along one side to take out any wobble
- **term**: Dovetail slide with gib (dovetail ways and gib strip)
- **aliases**: 
  - dovetail slide
  - dovetail ways / dovetail slideway / dovetail guide
  - gib strip / gib (the adjustable strip)
  - tapered gib / taper gib (a long thin wedge slid lengthwise)
  - straight gib / parallel gib / flat gib (pushed in by set screws)
  - gibless dovetail (small slides that rely on a close fit and a locking grub screw)
  - cross slide / compound slide / saddle (on a lathe)
  - dovetail stage / dovetail translation stage (optics and lab positioning)
  - cross-slide vise / X-Y compound vise (drill press)
  - Schwalbenschwanzfuehrung (German, 'swallow-tail guide')
- **family**: sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **other_families**: locating & alignment, interlocking joints & joinery
- **one_liner**: A carriage with angled (dovetail) edges slides along a matching angled track that stops it lifting off or rocking, and a thin strip (the gib), pushed in by screws or slid along as a wedge, takes up the play so it slides snugly without wobble and can be re-tightened as it wears.
- **complexity**: A few parts: the base with the dovetail track (the ways), the sliding part (saddle or carriage), the gib strip, and either several gib screws with lock nuts (straight gib) or one adjusting screw and a lock (tapered gib). Usually also a lead screw and nut to drive it, and sometimes a lock screw to clamp it in place.
- **how_it_works**: The sliding part has a groove with sloping sides that wraps around a matching slanted rail, so it can slide along the rail but cannot lift off, rock or move sideways (dovetail ways constrain both cross directions and stop the carriage lifting, unlike flat or V ways). The groove is made slightly wider than the rail and the gap on one side is filled by a gib strip. With a straight (parallel) gib, set screws along the side push the strip against the rail; with a tapered gib, the strip is a long thin wedge that an end screw slides lengthwise, so on a 1-in-100 taper moving it 10 mm changes the fit by only about 0.1 mm. Tightening removes the play; the gib is then locked, and it can be re-adjusted as the sliding surfaces wear.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): guides a straight-line sliding motion
  - line parts up exactly: keeps the carriage square to the track, with the play adjusted out by the gib
  - lock, and release by hand: small dovetails can be locked with a grub screw, and tightening the gib (or a separate lock screw) clamps the slide
- **motion**: slide: straight-line travel only, limited by the length of the ways (e.g. 50 mm on the_buff's printed 80 x 80 mm stage)
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1: the carriage can only slide along the track; the dovetail blocks sideways movement, lifting and rocking
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - none: it is a plain sliding guide with no clever behaviour of its own
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds), loosely: the gib is an adjustable take-up that removes play and can be re-set as the surfaces wear
- **holding_principle**: 
  - shape (form-fit): the slanted sides interlock, so the carriage can't lift or move sideways
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the gib presses the sliding surfaces together; tightened hard, or with a lock screw, it clamps the slide in place
  - not a holding joint along the track: along the sliding direction it lets the carriage move freely unless locked
- **input_output**: not applicable: it guides motion but does not convert or pass it on (a separate lead screw, rack or hand push moves the carriage)
- **power**: passive (no power needed): the slide itself has no powered parts; it is pushed by hand or by a separate screw drive
- **removability**: removable: once the drive screw and gib are taken out, the carriage slides off the end of the ways
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: it can be dismantled for cleaning and refitted repeatedly; the gib is a wear part that is re-adjusted, shimmed or replaced (Sherline)
- **tools_needed**: Screwdriver or hex keys for the gib screws and a small spanner for their lock nuts; an oil can. Making a metal one needs a milling machine with a dovetail cutter plus files or a scraper for fitting. The printed stage by the_buff needs M6 and M3 taps and hex keys for M3 screws.
- **seal**: none: the sliding surfaces are open; felt wipers are sometimes fitted to keep the oil in (Wikipedia 'Way (machine tool element)')
- **load**: Heavy for its size: full surface contact gives higher stiffness and load capacity than similar-sized ball or roller guides, and it soaks up shocks and vibration that would damage rolling bearings (linearmotiontips). It takes loads from several directions, but downward loads better than upward (lifting) loads. Friction is higher than rolling guides, so it is not for fast motion.
- **adjustable**: yes: the gib screws (straight gib) or the lengthwise position of a wedge-shaped gib (tapered gib) set the running clearance; a 1-in-100 tapered gib gives fine control (10 mm of gib travel = about 0.1 mm change in fit, john f's workshop). A worn tapered gib that has gone in as far as it can be built up with one or two layers of Mylar tape on its back (Sherline).
- **play_and_precision**: Play: small to none once the gib is set; on a hobby lathe cross slide the target is under about 0.001 in (0.025 mm) of sideways shake measured with a dial indicator (Hobby-Machinist forum). Positioning repeatability depends mostly on the drive screw. Misalignment: tolerates almost none; the ways must be straight and parallel. A slide that is loose in one place and binds in another has worn ways (Sherline), and one that is tighter in one direction has a tapered (worn) dovetail (Hobby-Machinist forum).
- **access_needed**: end of a tube or shaft: the carriage has to be slid on from an open end of the track (a dovetail in the middle of a surface needs an extra entry slot, as BOSL2's dovetail module notes); the gib screws are then reached from one side only
- **materials**: Machine slides: close-grained cast iron normalised to limit distortion, also hard-coat aluminium, stainless steel or acetal (Wikipedia 'Linear-motion bearing'; linearmotiontips). Gibs: ground low-carbon steel, cast iron or brass, or a moulded self-lubricating composite (Sherline's tapered plastic gibs). Sliding faces may carry bonded low-friction plastic liners such as Turcite or Rulon (brands) as sacrificial wear surfaces. Printed versions: PETG body (the_buff) with steel screws pushing the gib.
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate to print: the dovetail itself prints easily, but printed surfaces are rough, so it needs a gib, tapped holes, grease and some tuning to slide smoothly without play; hard in metal (needs a milling machine, a dovetail cutter and careful fitting or scraping), so buy a ready-made slide when precision matters

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - off-the-shelf part: dovetail slides, lead-screw positioning slides and dovetail stages
  - CNC / machining: milled with a dovetail cutter, then ground or hand-scraped
  - 3D print (FDM): printed linear stages and slides with metal gib screws in tapped holes
- **print_in_place**: no: the base, carriage and gib are printed as separate parts, slid together, and the gib screws are then fitted into tapped holes (as in the_buff's linear stage)
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Follow a proven printed stage: the_buff's 80 x 80 mm dovetail stage (50 mm travel) prints in PETG at 0.2 mm layers, 4 perimeters, 6 top and 4 bottom layers, 20% gyroid infill and no supports; its holes are printed undersize for tapping (5 mm for M6, 2.5 mm for M3) and three M3 x 14 mm socket screws press on the gib (the author cut the gib screws from four to three). 2) Lay the base and carriage flat with the dovetail running along the bed so the slanted faces are gentle overhangs; Prusa says 45-60 degree overhangs print cleanly, and a 60 degree dovetail (the usual angle for small slides, john f's workshop) leans only 30 degrees from vertical. 3) Clearance: give moving parts at least 0.3 mm to start with (Prusa); BOSL2's dovetail() adds a $slop clearance on all three faces of the female part. Err on the loose side, because the gib takes up the rest. 4) Make the gib a separate strip that fills the gap on one side; give each screw a flat or a dimple to push on so the screw point doesn't dig in and the strip can't creep along (john f's workshop recommends milling a flat at right angles to each screw; the Model Engineer forum recommends ball- or cone-ended screws and one or two locating dowels). A strip of acetal or steel is smoother and stiffer than a printed one. 5) Printed surfaces are rough: a remix of the_buff's stage recommends thick silicone grease on the slide and screw to make it much smoother (Hungry_Hank's remix, Printables model 886997, read through the Printables API). 6) Thin printed features break: the_buff had to redesign a front screw clip that was too thin, and Hungry_Hank found the original parts broke easily in PLA and thickened them; PETG or glass-filled nylon hold up better. 7) Chamfer the edges of parts that slot together to make assembly easier (Prusa). 8) Keep printed slides away from heat: PLA softens above about 60 C and PETG is fine below about 80 C (Prusa). 9) BOSL2's dovetail() can also taper the dovetail (taper= or back_width=) so it slides freely until nearly home and then wedges tight, which is useful for a clamp-type dovetail rather than a running slide.
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Dovetail slides (manual, lead-screw 'positioning slides' and cylinder-powered versions, e.g. Gilman (brand)); dovetail translation stages with micrometer or lead-screw drive for optics and lab work; drill press cross-slide (X-Y compound) vises; lathe cross slides and compound rests; replacement gib strips (e.g. Sherline P/N 4098 and 4099 moulded composite gibs (brand)); gib screws (set screws with ball, cone or dog points) and lock nuts; dovetail cutters (45 and 60 degree) for milling your own; slideway (way) oil such as Mobil Vactra No. 2 (brand); bonded low-friction way liners such as Turcite and Rulon (brands).
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Dovetail angle: usually 45 degrees on large industrial machines and 60 degrees on smaller dovetails; angles below 60 degrees make the adjusting parts hard to fit on small slides (john f's workshop, 'points applying to most types of dovetail').
  - Tapered gib: with a taper of about 1 in 100, moving the gib 10 mm changes the fit by about 0.1 mm and moving it 1 mm changes it by 0.01 mm; both tapered faces must match exactly and the gib must touch evenly all along (john f's workshop, 'tapered gib').
  - Tapered gibs bear on both surfaces over their full length, giving even pressure; straight gibs pushed by set screws press only at the screw points and wear unevenly, which is why better machines use tapered gibs (Hobby-Machinist forum, 'Tapered Gibs').
  - Adjust in tiny steps ('1/16 is a tremendous turn on a gib'): with the drive screw removed and the ways oiled, the slide should move by hand with little shake, ideally under about 0.001 in of sideways movement on a dial indicator at each end (Hobby-Machinist forum, 'Gibb adjustments').
  - If a new straight gib won't bed in, bow it slightly (0.5-1.0 mm over 300 mm) so it touches the middle of the dovetail first, then adjust the screws from the middle outwards (Model Engineer & Workshop forum, 'Gib strips').
  - Oil sliding ways, don't grease them (john f's workshop; linearmotiontips specifies a slideway oil such as Mobil Vactra No. 2 and forbids grease). Exception: tiny microscope dovetails use thick grease on purpose to add friction (john f's workshop).
  - Halve a manual slide's published dynamic load rating unless low-friction bearing material is bonded to the saddle and gib; that material cuts friction by about two-thirds and, because its static and moving friction are similar, reduces stick-slip (linearmotiontips, 'How to pick dovetail slides').
  - A dovetail slide cannot be preloaded like a ball rail, but its clearance can be adjusted after assembly with the gib (linearmotiontips, 'benefits of dovetail slides').
  - For printed moving parts, start with at least 0.3 mm clearance (Prusa 'Modeling with 3D printing in mind') and let the gib take up the rest.

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - The cross slide and compound slide on a metal lathe: turning the handles moves the tool on dovetails that have gib screws along one side
  - A drill-press cross-slide (X-Y) vise, whose two dovetail slides have small gib screws to remove slop
  - The table and column of a small milling machine or hobby lathe (Sherline machines use tapered plastic gibs on every dovetailed axis)
  - Dovetail rails that mount telescopes and rifle scopes (the same shape used as a clamp mount, without a gib)
- **when_to_use**: Use it when you need a stiff, strong, straight slide that can be tightened up as it wears: machine tool tables and tool slides, positioning stages, jigs and fixtures, camera, microscope and lab focusing stages, and slides that take shocks or vibration that would damage ball bearings. It suits hand-adjusted or slow screw-driven moves, long travels at low cost, and dusty workshops, since it has few parts to clog. For makers, a printed dovetail stage with a screw-pushed gib is a practical home-made X, XY or Z positioning stage.
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid it for fast, frequent or very low-friction motion: sliding friction is higher than ball or roller guides and, without the right oil, it can stick then jump (stick-slip); use a linear ball rail, linear bearings on round rods or V-wheels on extrusion instead. Avoid it where you can't lubricate or re-adjust it. For a tiny travel with no friction or play at all, use a flexure stage. For attaching and removing something quickly rather than sliding it, use a dovetail clamp (quick-release plate) instead.
- **environment_limits**: Needs a film of oil to slide smoothly and resist wear; old oil collects grit and 'crud' that makes it stiff (Model Engineer forum), so clean and re-oil. Dovetail slides are largely immune to chemicals, dust and dirt compared with rolling-element guides (Wikipedia 'Linear-motion bearing'). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: On machine tools the slide carries a cutting tool or workpiece next to spinning parts, so adjust gibs with the machine stopped. Otherwise none known. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: 1) Wear makes the slide sloppy: re-adjust the gib; a tapered gib at the end of its travel can be shimmed with Mylar tape (Sherline). 2) Loose in the middle but tight at the ends: the ways are worn where most use happens and need scraping or re-machining, not just a tighter gib (Sherline; Hobby-Machinist forum). 3) A straight gib pressing only at the screw points, flexing and wearing unevenly, or creeping along the dovetail: use ball- or cone-ended screws in matching dimples or flats, dowel the strip, or change to a tapered gib (Model Engineer forum; john f's workshop; Hobby-Machinist forum). 4) Stiff or jerky motion (stick-slip) from dirt or the wrong lubricant: clean it and use way oil, not grease (Model Engineer forum; linearmotiontips). 5) Over-tightening binds the slide: adjust in tiny increments (Hobby-Machinist forum). 6) Printed slides: rough faces bind and thin parts crack; add grease, print in PETG and thicken weak features (Hungry_Hank; the_buff).
- **related**: 
  - Linear ball-bearing rail (profile rail and carriage): much lower friction and faster, but less tolerant of shocks and dearer
  - Box ways (square ways): heavier and stiffer, used on large machines; clearance can't be adjusted after assembly the way a gib allows (linearmotiontips)
  - V-ways and flat ways (lathe beds): V-ways guide sideways but, unlike a dovetail, don't stop the carriage lifting (Wikipedia 'Way')
  - Linear bearings on round rods: cheap and easy to design printed parts around, but less stiff
  - Parallel leaf-spring flexure (linear flexure stage): no friction or play at all, but only a short travel
  - Quick-release plate (camera plate, dovetail clamp): the same dovetail shape used as a clamp-on mount rather than a running slide
  - Split-part joints for large prints (dovetail keys): a dovetail used to join parts for good rather than to slide
  - Lead screw and nut: the usual drive that moves a dovetail slide

## 74 Threadless ball screw (rolling-ring drive)

*sliding, telescoping & linear motion.* Three tilted bearings pressed onto a smooth spinning rod act like a nut and move along it; printable and adjustable, and it slips instead of jamming

### What it is

- **plain_name**: Three ordinary ball bearings clamped at a slant around a smooth steel rod: when the rod spins, the bearings roll round it in a spiral and walk along it like a nut on a screw, but with no thread, so it slips instead of jamming
- **term**: Rolling-ring drive (threadless ball screw)
- **aliases**: 
  - threadless ballscrew / threadless ball screw
  - rolling ring drive / rolling-ring linear drive
  - rolling ring traverse (in winding machines)
  - rolling nut on a smooth shaft
  - Rollringgetriebe (German)
  - Uhing linear drive (brand; sold in the US by Amacoil)
  - Roh'Lix linear actuator (brand, Zero-Max)
- **family**: sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **other_families**: motion conversion, torque limiting & slip
- **one_liner**: Bearings pressed at an angle against a smooth spinning rod roll round it in a spiral and so carry their housing (the 'nut') along the rod, turning rotation into straight-line motion with no backlash, and slipping harmlessly instead of jamming when overloaded.
- **complexity**: A small assembly that acts as one unit: a smooth hardened steel rod turned by a motor through a coupler, a nut body, three (sometimes four or more) ball bearings mounted on bolts at a slight tilt around the rod, and something that sets how hard they press (the bolts themselves, a spring or a set screw). Commercial units add a pitch-setting lever, a free-movement lever and a spring-loaded reversing mechanism.
- **how_it_works**: Each bearing presses on the rod at a slight tilt, like a wheel steered a little to one side. When the rod spins it rolls every bearing round, and because they are tilted they roll in a spiral path, so the nut moves along the rod a fixed distance per turn, as if on a thread (Hackaday likens the contact to a temporary thread pressed into the rod). The drive comes only from friction at the contact points, so the clamping force sets how hard it can push before it slips, and the tilt angle sets the travel per turn: more tilt, faster travel. In commercial units the shaft passes through special bearings with a ridged inner race; tilting them the other way reverses the direction of travel while the shaft keeps turning the same way.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - change the kind of motion: continuous rotation of a smooth rod into straight-line travel of the nut
  - slip or let go when overloaded: the nut slips along the rod instead of jamming if it hits an obstruction or end stop
  - change speed or turning force: the tilt angle sets the travel per turn; commercial units adjust it with a lever and can reverse direction without reversing the motor
- **motion**: rotate+slide: the rod turns and the nut slides along it; travel is limited only by the rod length (Uhing units reach up to 16 ft / about 5 m of stroke, Amacoil)
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1: in normal running the nut's position follows the rod's rotation; any slip adds unwanted, uncontrolled sliding along the rod
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - motion conversion (rotary to linear): the spinning rod drives the nut along it
  - torque-limiting (slips at a set force): the push is limited by friction, so the nut slips at a thrust set by how tightly the bearings are clamped
  - fail-safe or breakaway: if travel is blocked, the shaft simply slips until the blockage is cleared, instead of jamming or stripping a thread (Machine Design)
- **holding_principle**: 
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): only friction at the bearing-to-rod contacts moves the nut; there is no interlocking thread
  - spring force: commercial units keep the bearings pressed on with a factory-set thrust screw or springs; many printed nuts use a fixed interference set by the bolts instead
- **input_output**: Continuous rotation of a smooth rod -> straight-line motion of the nut along the same axis (in line). Travel per turn rises with the bearing tilt (the Uhing patent gives feed proportional to tan of the tilt angle): a printed nut with about 10 degrees of tilt on an 8 mm rod moved about 4.5 mm per turn (2n2r5), and Uhing drives cover roughly 6-70 mm per turn depending on size, adjustable over about 10:1. Thrust: about 30 N to 3,600 N across Uhing sizes (15-80 mm shafts); Amacoil quotes up to 800 lb, and four-ring units give twice the thrust of three-ring units (Machine Design). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **power**: passive (no power needed) as a mechanism, but it is normally motor-driven. Uhing warns that operating the free-movement lever can let a load drop in an uncontrolled way *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **removability**: removable: loosen the bearing bolts (or, on commercial units, flip the free-movement lever that releases the preload) and slide the nut off the end of the rod
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: nothing is permanently deformed when it is fitted; the bearings and rod are wear parts
- **tools_needed**: Hex keys and spanners for the bearing bolts (M5 in 2n2r5's printed nut) and for the coupler set screws; a rag and suitable grease for the shaft. No special tools.
- **seal**: none: the rod and bearings are open; commercial units can be fitted with rubber scrapers at the shaft openings to wipe off dirt (Amacoil)
- **load**: Light to medium along the rod: thrust is limited by friction, from a few newtons for printed nuts to about 3,600 N for the largest commercial units, and it slips beyond that. Weak against side loads and twisting (moment) loads: these cut the thrust sharply and wear the bearings, so the payload should ride on its own guide rails or slide and only be pushed by the nut (Machine Design; Amacoil).
- **adjustable**: yes: the slip force is set by the bearing clamping (tighten the bolts, or the factory thrust screw on commercial units); the travel per turn is set by the tilt angle (fixed in printed designs, lever-adjustable about 10:1 in 100 notched steps on Uhing RG drives); the direction is reversed by tilting the bearings the other way, automatically at adjustable end stops on commercial units
- **play_and_precision**: Backlash: none, because the bearings are always pressed onto the rod. Repeatability: poor unless you add feedback, because small slips add up: Maker's Muse's printed unit drifted badly within a few minutes (Hackaday 2026), printed Z-axes slipped more going up than down (Soliforum), and Uhing sets the pitch difference between the two directions only to within 2.5%. Commercial units are typically accurate to about +/-0.005 in (+/-0.13 mm), premium ones +/-0.0004 in (Amacoil); Uhing suggests slowdown cams where +/-0.5 mm stopping accuracy is enough. Misalignment: needs a straight, round, hardened rod and a twist-free coupling to any separately guided load.
- **access_needed**: end of a tube or shaft: the nut is slid on from an end of the rod (printed nuts are fitted with the bearings backed off, then tightened onto the rod)
- **materials**: Rod: induction-hardened, ground steel; Uhing requires at least 50 HRC, h6 diameter tolerance, Ra 0.35 um or smoother and no more than 0.1 mm/m runout, and supplies Cf53 steel (60-64 HRC) or rust-resistant 1.4034 and 1.4112 stainless shafts. Bearings: standard deep-groove ball bearings pressing with their outer edge in printed versions (e.g. 625ZZ on an 8 mm rod); specially machined rolling-ring bearings with a ridged inner race in commercial units. Nut body: printed PLA or PETG (100% infill recommended) or metal housings.
- **maker_difficulty**: easy to moderate: a printed nut, three small bearings, bolts and an 8 mm smooth rod are cheap and simple, but getting the angle and clamping right takes fiddling, and it will slip and drift; buy a commercial rolling-ring unit for continuous industrial duty

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - 3D print (FDM): the nut body holding the tilted bearings
  - off-the-shelf part: hardened smooth rod, ball bearings, bolts and coupler; or a complete commercial rolling-ring drive (Uhing, Amacoil, Roh'Lix (brands))
- **print_in_place**: no: the nut body is printed, then standard ball bearings and bolts are fitted and it is clamped onto a bought steel rod
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Start from a customisable design: 2n2r5's OpenSCAD threadless ballscrew (Thingiverse thing:124706) supports up to 5 bearings, many standard bearing sizes (603 to 696 series) and several rod sizes, and outputs both the pitch and the bearing angle; he settled on 625ZZ bearings (5 x 16 x 5 mm) on an 8 mm smooth rod with M5 x 15-20 mm bolts, washers and nuts. 2) Print strong: at most 0.15 mm layers, at least 3 perimeters and 100% infill, because there is a lot of force on the bearings and bolts; with less infill the plastic flexes, heat builds up and the bearings work loose, and nuts can pull through the plastic (2n2r5, Soliforum). 3) Tilt: most builders set the bearings at about 10 degrees; about 10 degrees on an 8 mm rod gave about 4.5 mm of travel per turn (2n2r5). Printing tolerances make the real angle, and so the pitch, only roughly right, so measure travel over many turns and calibrate your steps per mm. 4) Fitting: start with the bearings loose, about 5 mm proud of the nut body, slide the rod through, then tighten each bearing bolt one turn at a time until they seat; 2n2r5 offset each bearing 0.4 mm towards the rod in the model for a very tight grip. 5) If it slips, add bearings: one builder cured slipping on a lifting bed with a four-bearing nut plus a second offset set underneath (8 bearings), but another found a very tight four-bearing nut cut a shallow groove in the rod, so don't over-tighten (Soliforum). 6) Guide the load separately: let the nut only push, and carry the carriage on its own rails with a short, twist-free connection (Uhing; Machine Design). 7) Use a hardened, ground rod (a normal 3D-printer smooth rod), not soft steel or threaded rod. 8) Keep oil, silicone and graphite off the contact; a thin film of MoS2-free ball-bearing grease is what Uhing specifies (see environment_limits). 9) Expect drift: add an end-stop switch and home often, or use an encoder, for anything that must return to the same place (Hackaday comments; Maker's Muse).
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Deep-groove ball bearings (625ZZ, 608ZZ skate bearings), M5 bolts, washers and nuts; hardened, ground linear shaft ('smooth rod', e.g. 8 mm h6 case-hardened shaft as used on 3D-printer axes); flexible or rigid shaft coupler (e.g. 5 mm to 8 mm); complete rolling-ring linear drives: Uhing RG (variable pitch), RS (fixed pitch) and KI units (brand), sold in the US by Amacoil (brand), and Roh'Lix linear actuators (brand, Zero-Max); Uhing precision shafts, rubber shaft scrapers and load-carrier slides such as the Amacoil L4 (brand).
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Most builders set the bearing angle to about 10 degrees (2n2r5, Soliforum).
  - Print the nut at 0.15 mm layers or finer, with at least 3 perimeters and 100% infill (2n2r5, Soliforum).
  - Thrust depends on bearing size and count: four-ring drives give twice the axial thrust of three-ring drives, and a large unit gives up to about 800 lb (Machine Design); Uhing sizes run from about 30 N (15 mm shaft) to 3,600 N (80 mm shaft) (Uhing).
  - Shaft: at least 50 HRC surface hardness, h6 diameter tolerance, Ra 0.35 um or better and no more than 0.1 mm/m runout (Uhing RG technical information, via Amacoil).
  - Don't let the nut stall on a turning shaft: Uhing recommends switching off the shaft drive if the unit stands still for more than 5 s at full rated thrust, to protect the shaft (Uhing RG technical information).
  - Recommended shaft speed for Uhing units is 5 to 3,000 rpm; reversal needs a travel of about one shaft diameter at the end stop (Uhing RG technical information).
  - Carry heavy or off-centre loads on a separate slide: moment loads dramatically reduce thrust and increase bearing wear (Machine Design).
  - No silicone lubricants: they cause slipping (Amacoil FAQ). Uhing specifies a thin film of MoS2-free ball-bearing grease on the shaft, monthly (Uhing technical information) or weekly (Amacoil FAQ). *(unverified parts omitted)*

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - Industrial wire, cable and yarn winding machines, where a rolling-ring traverse lays the product evenly on a spool and reverses at each end by itself (Uhing; Amacoil)
  - Home-made 3D-printer Z axes from the RepRap era, which used printed threadless ball screws instead of lead screws (Hackaday) *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **when_to_use**: Use it when you want smooth, quiet, backlash-free straight-line motion from a spinning rod and would rather it slipped than jammed or broke: moving a carriage back and forth for spraying, feeding or winding, long strokes where a long smooth rod is simpler than a long screw, and machines where an obstruction or bottoming out must not cause damage. Commercial units are the classic choice for level-winding wire and cable, because they reverse at the ends and change pitch mechanically without any electronics. It is also a fun, cheap printable demonstration of friction drive.
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid it where position must be exact and repeatable without feedback (CNC axes, printer axes without homing): it drifts; use a lead screw, ball screw, belt or rack. Avoid it for lifting or holding heavy loads, especially vertically: it slips, most of all going up, and a released load can drop; use a lead screw (self-locking with a fine lead) or a worm drive. Avoid heavy side loads on the nut, and oily or dirty conditions where the contact loses grip. For back-and-forth traversing with a fixed stroke, a reversing (diamond) screw is simpler to print and cannot slip.
- **environment_limits**: Uhing units are rated for -10 to +80 C (RGK types to +50 C) (Uhing technical information) and have worked in Antarctica at -20 C (Uhing). Lubricants matter: silicone, graphite and MoS2 lubricants cause slip; a 2013 Amacoil article in Machine Design said no lubricant should be used at all, while Uhing's current instructions call for a thin film of MoS2-free ball-bearing grease, so follow the maker's own instructions. The smooth rod doesn't trap debris, but dirt between rod and bearings hurts grip, so scrapers are used in dirty places. Printed nuts: heat build-up loosens the bearings unless printed with 100% infill (2n2r5), and PLA softens above about 60 C (Prusa).
- **safety_notes**: The moving traverse can crush, and must be guarded like the rotating shaft (Uhing security advice). On units with a free-movement lever, make sure the load cannot drop in an uncontrolled way before releasing it, or injury could result (Uhing). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: 1) Slipping: caused by the wrong lubricant, excessive load, poor mounting or long mounting screws, or worn bearings (Amacoil FAQ); printed nuts slip mostly when lifting (Soliforum). Fix with more or better-clamped bearings, less load, a separate guide and a clean rod. 2) Lost position (drift): small slips add up; home against a switch or add an encoder (Hackaday; Maker's Muse). 3) Worn rod: over-tight bearings or long periods of slipping on a turning shaft wear a groove in the rod (Soliforum; Uhing's 5-second stall rule). 5) Printed nut loosening or flexing over time: print with 100% infill and re-check the bolts (2n2r5). 6) Wobble or binding from a bent rod or a misaligned motor coupler (Soliforum). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **related**: 
  - Lead screw and nut: a positive threaded drive that doesn't slip, holds position and can be self-locking, but has backlash and needs a threaded rod
  - Ball screw: balls in a threaded groove; precise and efficient but costly, and it jams or breaks rather than slipping
  - Reversing screw (diamond-thread or level-wind screw): crossing grooves give automatic back-and-forth traverse with a fixed stroke and no slip
  - Half nut (split nut): a threaded nut that can be opened off the screw for free sliding
  - Ring screw (Roy Featherstone): rolling rings running in a threaded rod, a positive rolling-contact screw reported at 91% efficiency
  - Friction slip clutch (slip coupling): the same 'slip instead of break' idea, for rotating drives
  - Rack and pinion or timing belt: positive, fast linear drives that don't slip

## 75 Reversing screw (diamond-thread or level-wind screw)

*sliding, telescoping & linear motion.* A shaft with two crossing grooves so a follower travels back and forth along it while the shaft keeps turning one way, as in fishing reels and filament winders

### What it is

- **plain_name**: A rod with two spiral grooves cut in opposite directions that cross each other in a diamond pattern; a little swivelling tooth riding in the groove travels to one end, turns round and comes back, over and over, while the rod keeps turning the same way
- **term**: Reversing screw (self-reversing screw, diamond screw, level-wind screw)
- **aliases**: 
  - self-reversing screw / self-reversing lead screw
  - diamond screw / diamond thread screw / diamond groove shaft
  - level-wind screw / levelwind screw / level winder screw
  - reverse spooling screw
  - right-and-left-hand screw nut traverse
  - worm gear / worm shaft (fishing-reel repair name for the level-wind shaft)
  - traverse screw
  - Kreuzgewindespindel / Umkehrspindel (German)
  - pawl / level-wind pawl / shoe / follower blade (the part that rides in the groove)
- **family**: sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **other_families**: motion conversion, tensioning, reels & take-up
- **one_liner**: A shaft with a right-hand and a left-hand spiral groove crossing each other and joined at the ends, so a swivelling follower in the groove runs back and forth along the shaft while the shaft keeps turning one way, which is how fishing reels and winders lay line evenly across a spool.
- **complexity**: A few parts: the grooved shaft (the diamond screw), a follower (pawl, shoe or blade) that sits in the groove and can swivel, a carriage or line guide holding the follower, a guide rod or rail that stops the carriage turning with the shaft, end bearings, and gears, a chain or belt that turn the screw in step with the spool.
- **how_it_works**: Two spiral (helical) grooves with the same lead but opposite hand are cut along the same shaft, so they cross each other many times, and at each end they join in a smooth U-turn. The follower sits in the groove and is free to swivel in its carriage. As the shaft turns, the groove pushes the follower along like a nut on a screw; at the end it runs round the U-turn, swivels to line up with the opposite-hand groove, and is pushed back the other way, all without the shaft changing direction. The follower is long and slim along the groove (a blade, boat or lens shape rather than a round pin), so at each crossing it bridges the gap and keeps following its own groove instead of jumping into the other one.

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - change the kind of motion: continuous one-way rotation into steady back-and-forth straight-line motion that reverses by itself
  - line parts up exactly: guides line, cable, hose or filament so it is laid evenly across a spool (level winding)
- **motion**: rotate+slide: the shaft turns continuously one way while the follower and carriage slide back and forth; the stroke is fixed by the length of the grooved section
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1: the follower's position along the shaft is tied to the shaft's rotation; the follower also swivels in its carriage, but only to track the groove
- **special_behaviour**: motion conversion (rotary to linear): continuous one-way rotation becomes back-and-forth sliding that reverses automatically at each end, with no clutch, switch or reversing motor
- **holding_principle**: 
  - shape (form-fit): the follower is trapped in the groove, so the carriage is tied positively to the shaft's rotation and cannot slip (unlike a rolling-ring drive)
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): its job is to drive the traverse
- **input_output**: Continuous one-way rotation -> back-and-forth straight-line traverse of the follower, parallel to the shaft (in line), reversing automatically at each end, with a fixed stroke equal to the grooved length. Travel per screw turn = the groove lead; for level winding the drum-to-screw gear or sprocket ratio is chosen so the guide moves about one line diameter per spool turn (e.g. Bruncione's printed guide for 3/4 in garden hose uses a 6.5 mm step). Speed is steady along the stroke apart from a short slow-down and turn at each end. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **power**: passive (no power needed): usually driven from the same crank, motor or drum that turns the spool, so it stays in step automatically; if the drive stops, the carriage simply stops where it is
- **removability**: removable: the follower lifts out of the groove or the carriage slides off, and the shaft comes out of its end bearings
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: the follower (pawl) is the main wear part and is replaceable (Kolstrand supplies a spare pawl with its level winds)
- **tools_needed**: Screwdriver or hex keys for the carriage and end-bearing screws (e.g. two M3 bolts in chuyskywalker's printed level winder); for fishing reels, reel-cleaning degreaser, a small brush and light oil or grease. No special tools.
- **seal**: none: the grooves are open to dirt, which is why grit and sand are a main cause of sticking in fishing-reel level winds
- **load**: Light to medium: the follower normally only pushes a line guide sideways. It is positive (it can't slip), but the small follower tip and the sharp points where the grooves cross are the weak spots. Heavy steel versions guide wire rope thousands of metres long on offshore winches (Bornemann). Side loads should be taken by a separate guide rod or rail, not by the screw.
- **adjustable**: no, as a rule: the stroke and the lead are fixed by the groove shape; the traverse rate relative to the spool can only be changed by changing the gear or sprocket ratio between drum and screw (Wikipedia 'Wire rope spooling technology'), and a different line size may need a different screw (Bruncione)
- **play_and_precision**: Play: small; the follower tip must fit the groove precisely (Mike's Reel Repair), and it wears. Repeatability: good, because it cannot slip and the carriage position is always tied to the shaft angle. The carriage pauses briefly at each end while the follower turns round. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **access_needed**: end of a tube or shaft: the shaft is fitted into end bearings and the follower is dropped into the groove through the carriage
- **materials**: Industrial level-winder screws: stainless steels, alloy steels, Inconel or titanium (Bornemann); stainless-steel diamond screws on fishing-boat level winds (Kolstrand). Fishing reels: a metal worm shaft with a small metal pawl, and line guides lined with ceramics such as silicon carbide on premium reels (Mike's Reel Repair). Printed: PETG (chuyskywalker; Bruncione suggests PETG with carbon fibre is best), PLA or PLA+ for light duty (juh; Pastamatic), Tough PLA for small precise screws (gzumwalt).
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate: it prints in one piece without supports if printed upright with fine layers, but the crossing grooves and end U-turns must be accurate and the follower must swivel freely; drawing the groove path in CAD is the hard part, so start from an existing model

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - 3D print (FDM): the grooved shaft and the follower, printed upright
  - off-the-shelf part: machined self-reversing screws and level-wind screw and pawl sets
  - CNC: turned and milled in steel for winches and industrial winders
- **print_in_place**: no: the screw, follower and carriage are printed separately, then assembled with bearings and a guide rod, and the follower is dropped into the groove
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Print the screw standing upright: all of juh's parts print upright, and Bruncione prints his hose screw vertically at 0.2 mm with a 10 mm brim and slowed to 30 mm/s, with no supports. 2) Fine layers help the groove overhangs: 0.12 mm (chuyskywalker; juh says 0.12 mm or less lets the spindle overhangs print without support), or 0.1 mm layers with a 0.25 mm nozzle for a small screw (gzumwalt). 3) Cool well: use part cooling from two or more sides, or the steep overhangs sag as they solidify (juh). 4) Long screws: split them into sections. juh's fischertechnik screw uses two end pieces (27.4 mm of travel) plus any number of 30 mm middle pieces; IrishUkulele found the Pastamatic screw hard to print cleanly and split it, joining the sections with slightly non-square hex connectors so they only fit one way and stay aligned. 5) Material: PETG for working winders (chuyskywalker; Bruncione, with PETG-CF better), PLA+ is fine for a filament winder (Pastamatic); print the driven spindle with 100% concentric infill (Pastamatic). 6) Follower: make it a round disc or pin that can rotate freely in the carriage, carrying a slim tooth that fits the groove; it must 'turn' at each end to follow the return path (chuyskywalker). Make the tooth long along the groove so it bridges each crossing (see rules_of_thumb). 7) Support the screw on ball bearings (two 608s in chuyskywalker's design; Pastamatic uses fourteen 608s in total) and run the carriage on a separate smooth rod or linear bearing (Bruncione) so the follower only pushes. 8) Clearances: prefer generous clearances and simple mechanisms (Pastamatic's design goal), then trim, file and polish the groove and follower until they run smoothly (gzumwalt). 9) Match the lead to what you wind: Bruncione's 6.5 mm step suits 3/4 in garden hose, and other sizes need a different step. 10) Keep printed winders away from heat: PLA softens above about 60 C (Prusa).
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Self-reversing screws / level winder screws / reverse spooling screws made to order (Bornemann (brand), Abssac (brand)); levelwind screw and pawl sets (Rempco (brand)); complete diamond-screw level winds for winch and net drums (Lebus (brand), Kolstrand (brand)); replacement level-wind worm shafts, pawls and line guides for baitcasting reels; 608 skate bearings, stainless steel guide rods and linear bearings for printed winders; rolling-ring traverse units (Uhing (brand)) as the adjustable alternative.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Make the follower long along the groove so it straddles each crossing: a follower that bridges the intersections won't jump from one groove into the opposite-hand groove under load (US patent 3779094, background). Nguyen Duc Thang's crossed-groove cam animation uses pins with an elliptical section, long axis along the groove, so they run stably through the crossings.
  - The follower must be free to swivel so it can turn round at each end and follow the return groove (chuyskywalker's round-disc pawl).
  - For even side-by-side layers, the guide should move about one line diameter per spool turn, so that wraps lie without gaps (Samson Rope: wind evenly, without spaces across the drum); the rate is set by the chain-and-sprocket (or gear) ratio between drum and lead screw (Wikipedia 'Wire rope spooling technology').
  - The sharp points where the grooves cross are the weak spot: on dual-threaded level-wind screws they can fracture under vibration and make the follower reverse early, causing a miswrap (US patent 10640346B2, which uses two single-thread screws to avoid them). Print in a tough material such as PETG.
  - Print upright, at 0.12 mm layers or finer, with strong cooling, to print the grooves without supports (juh; chuyskywalker).
  - Keep the grooves clean and lightly lubricated: dirt build-up causes sticking and binding, and the pawl is a critical wear item whose tip must fit the groove (Mike's Reel Repair).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - The line guide on a baitcasting fishing reel that slides left and right as you wind, driven by a crossed-groove worm shaft and a small pawl (the 'level wind')
  - Level winders on winch and net drums, from fishing boats to offshore winches that spool wire rope thousands of metres long
  - 3D-printer filament respooling winders such as the popular printed Pastamatic
  - Yarn and thread winding on textile machines, and cable winding in cranes (Bornemann)
- **when_to_use**: Use it to wind line, cable, hose, rope, yarn or filament evenly onto a spool, or anywhere you need a steady back-and-forth sliding motion with a fixed stroke from a shaft that only turns one way. It needs no electronics, switches or reversing motor, cannot slip, and stays in step with the spool automatically if it is geared from the same drive. It also makes a great printable kinetic toy or demonstrator (marble lifts, walkers).
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid it when the stroke or pitch must change, for example for different spool widths or line sizes: use a rolling-ring (threadless ball screw) traverse with adjustable end stops and pitch, or a stepper-driven lead screw with end switches. It is not for precise positioning or holding loads (use a lead screw), nor for heavy shock loads where the crossing points would chip. For a short back-and-forth stroke where uneven speed doesn't matter, a crank (slider-crank or Scotch yoke) or a cam is simpler.
- **environment_limits**: Dirt, sand and salt in the open grooves cause sticking and wear, so fishing reels need regular cleaning and light lubrication (Mike's Reel Repair); marine and offshore screws are made of stainless steel, Inconel or titanium (Bornemann; Kolstrand). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: Drill-driven printed winders should be started slowly (the Pastamatic instructions say to accelerate slowly). Otherwise none known. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: 1) Sticking or binding from dirt build-up, and a worn pawl: clean, lightly lubricate and replace the pawl (Mike's Reel Repair; Kolstrand ships a spare pawl). 2) The follower jumping into the other groove at a crossing and reversing early: use a longer, slimmer follower that straddles the crossings (US3779094); chipped crossing points on dual-thread screws cause early reversal and miswraps (US10640346B2). 3) Printed grooves rough or sagging so the follower catches: print upright at fine layers with good cooling, or split the screw into sections (juh; IrishUkulele). 5) Line piling up or leaving gaps: the traverse rate doesn't match the line size, so change the gear ratio or use a screw with a different lead (Bruncione; Wikipedia 'Wire rope spooling technology'). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **related**: 
  - Rolling-ring drive (threadless ball screw): also reverses automatically (by flipping the bearing tilt at end stops) and its stroke and pitch can be adjusted, but it is friction-driven and can slip
  - Lead screw and nut: one-way travel per direction of rotation; the motor must reverse to come back
  - Right- and left-hand screws with switched half-nuts (507 Mechanical Movements No. 110): the old way to traverse thread guides on spooling frames, reversed by a lever
  - Twin opposite-turning single-thread screws with a shoe that swaps between them (US10640346B2): avoids the weak crossing points of a diamond screw
  - Cam and follower (barrel cam): a groove round a drum can make any motion pattern, including pauses, within one turn
  - Slider-crank and Scotch yoke: back-and-forth motion from a crank, but the speed varies along the stroke
  - Half nut (split nut): a nut that can be disengaged from a screw

## 76 Multi-stage slide rigging (cascade and continuous string rigging)

*sliding, telescoping & linear motion.* Ways of running strings or belts through nested slides so one winch extends all the stages, either together (cascade) or one after another (continuous), as in robot lifts

### What it is

- **plain_name**: Strings or belts threaded over little pulleys on a stack of nested slides, so one motor winding a reel pushes the whole stack out like a telescope and pulls it back in
- **term**: Multi-stage linear slide rigging: cascade rigging and continuous rigging (cascade elevator / continuous elevator)
- **aliases**: 
  - cascade rigging / cascading lift / cascade elevator (all stages move together)
  - continuous rigging / continuous elevator / continuous stringing (stages move one after another)
  - linear slide rigging, string rigging, stringing the slides (FTC robotics terms)
  - reverse-cascade retraction, continuous retraction, elastic retraction (ways of pulling the stages back in)
  - free-floating retraction vs 'with-slide' retraction (gm0 terms)
  - belt-driven slides, belt or chain elevator (the same routing done with a timing belt or roller chain)
  - multi-stage elevator / telescoping lift / multi-stage linear extension
  - spool and winch (the motor-driven reel the string winds onto)
  - forklift mast lift chain (the same pulley trick: the fork carriage rises twice as far as the lift cylinder)
  - Misumi SAR slides, REV linear motion kit, goBILDA Viper-Slide (brands of slide kits that are rigged this way)
- **family**: sliding, telescoping & linear motion
- **other_families**: tensioning, reels & take-up, motion conversion, cord, strap, webbing & textile hardware
- **one_liner**: A way of threading strings, belts or chains over pulleys on a set of nested sliding stages so that a single motor-driven reel extends every stage, either all at once in proportion (cascade) or one stage after another (continuous), with a second string or spring to pull them back in.
- **complexity**: A small assembly that acts as one unit: a fixed base slide plus two or more nested moving stages (drawer-style slides or extrusions on bearing blocks), a pulley or bearing at the top and bottom of each stage, a motor-driven spool (winch drum), one or more extension strings (one long string for continuous; one string per stage for cascade) or a belt or chain, a retraction string on a second spool (or surgical-tubing elastic), spring tensioners, and bolt clamps or anchors at the string ends.
- **how_it_works**: Continuous rigging uses one long string that runs from the spool over the top of the base, down to the bottom of stage 1, up over the top of stage 1, down to the bottom of stage 2 and so on to the last stage; winding it in shortens every loop at once, and the stage with the least friction (normally the last one) slides out first, then the next (gm0; YETI wiki). Cascade rigging uses a separate string for each stage: string 1 pulls stage 1 out of the base, and string 2 is anchored to the base, runs over a pulley on top of stage 1 and is tied to the bottom of stage 2, so as stage 1 rises it drags stage 2 out by the same amount relative to itself, and so on up the stack (gm0; REV). In cascade every stage moves at the same time and stage k moves k times as fast as stage 1, so the spool must pull harder but the top moves faster (gm0; Waffles wiki). Strings can only pull, so a second 'retraction' string wound the opposite way on a spool on the same shaft, a reversed set of cascade strings, elastic tubing, or a closed belt loop is needed to bring the stages back in (gm0).

### Finder attributes

- **job**: 
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): extends and retracts a stack of slides in a straight line, e.g. a robot lift or reach arm
  - change the kind of motion: a spinning motor spool becomes straight-line extension
  - change speed or turning force: in cascade rigging the top stage moves N times as fast as the first stage but the spool must pull about N times harder (N = number of moving stages); in continuous rigging force and speed stay 1:1 (gm0)
- **motion**: slide: straight-line extension and retraction; total travel is roughly the number of moving stages times each stage's travel (each stage must still overlap its neighbour, about 20% of the stage length by the YETI rule of thumb)
- **degrees_of_freedom**: 1 useful freedom (in and out along the slide). Cascade rigging ties every stage to the next, so the whole stack has exactly one position for each spool angle. Continuous rigging controls only where the last stage (the carriage) is; the in-between stages are free to move in whatever order friction allows, so they are not positively located (Chief Delphi 'Continuous vs cascade lift', 2022).
- **special_behaviour**: 
  - motion conversion: the rotation of a spool or pulley is turned into straight-line extension of the stages
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): good rigging includes spring tensioners or spring-loaded pulleys that keep the strings tight as the string path changes length during travel; gm0 'highly encourages' at least one spring tensioner per slide set
  - none of the locking behaviours: the rigging itself does not lock; a vertical lift sinks under its own weight unless the motor, its gearbox, a brake mode or counterbalance springs hold it (Chief Delphi 2019 thread)
- **holding_principle**: not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): the strings carry pulling force (tension) from the spool to the stages; position is held only as long as the spool is held
- **input_output**: Input: rotation of a motor-driven spool (or a belt pulley or sprocket). Output: straight-line extension. Continuous rigging: string wound in = carriage travel (1:1), and the force at the spool is roughly the load being lifted, which is why continuous spools 'can be powered by a system with a relatively low gear ratio' (gm0). Cascade rigging: the second stage moves 2 times as fast as the first, the third 3 times and so on (gm0), so the first string must pull about N times the load at the top (2x force on the first stage for a 2-stage lift: YETI; 'twice the speed and with half the force' at each stage: Waffles wiki). Adding stages to a cascade needs proportionally more gear reduction to keep the same speed, e.g. a 3:1 one-stage lift becomes 9:1 with three stages (gm0). Shaft arrangement: the spool shaft sits across (at right angles to) the slide direction. Back-drivable: yes, a vertical lift will drop when the motor is unpowered unless brake mode, counterbalance springs or a self-locking gearbox holds it (Chief Delphi 2019). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **power**: passive (no power needed) for the rigging itself, but it is almost always driven by an electric motor turning the spool. If power is lost the motor stops pulling, so a vertical lift can slide back down under gravity; brake mode alone may not be enough to hold it (Chief Delphi 2019).
- **removability**: removable: strings are anchored with knots or, better, clamped under a bolt head so they can be re-tensioned or replaced quickly (gm0); belts are clamped in belt clamps (Instructables cascading slide)
- **reuse_cycles**: many times: the slides, pulleys and spools can be re-rigged indefinitely; the strings themselves stretch, fray and are replaced as a wear item (gm0 notes that string loosens over time)
- **tools_needed**: A clamp, vice or arbor press to push bearings into printed pulleys (Instructables cascading slide). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **seal**: none: an open framework of slides, pulleys and strings
- **load**: Light to medium (competition robots lift game pieces and sometimes the robot itself: Brogan Pratt's printed FTC slide insert is described as lifting a 20 kg robot with two motors). Strings are only strong in pull (tension) and cannot push, so every direction you drive needs its own string or elastic. Braided UHMWPE (Dyneema/Spectra) kite line rated around 800 lb has been used on FRC elevators (Chief Delphi 2012). The weak points are sideways and bending loads on the extended slides: keep enough overlap between stages (about 20% of stage length) and at least two bearing blocks per side between stages (YETI wiki).
- **adjustable**: Yes: string tension is adjusted at bolt clamps, spring tensioners, spring-loaded pulleys, or a pulley that slides along an extrusion (gm0); speed and pulling force can be traded by changing the spool diameter instead of the gearbox (new diameter = old diameter x old ratio / new ratio) (gm0); the number of stages can be changed.
- **play_and_precision**: Noticeable. String stretches and loosens over time, and because the string path is a changing polygon the amount of string needed varies along the travel, so some slack is 'inherent in all linear extension designs using string' (gm0). If string piles up on the spool its diameter changes, the extension and retraction spools get out of step, and tension changes (gm0). Belts do not stretch and form an automatic 1:1 loop, so belt rigging is 'slightly more precise and rigid than rope' (gm0; YETI). In continuous rigging the intermediate stages are not positively located (Chief Delphi 2022). Misalignment tolerance is low: 'any amount of misalignment can lead to the string coming off of your pulley' (gm0).
- **access_needed**: both sides: you need to reach along the whole slide set, both ends of each stage, to thread the strings round the pulleys and fix the anchors; gm0 advises rigging extension strings with the slides retracted and retraction strings with them fully extended
- **materials**: Strings: braided UHMWPE line (Dyneema/Spectra (brands)), kevlar, kite or braided fishing line, or kit string such as REV (brand) string; belts: timing belt (even TPU-printed belt on the XRP two-stage elevator model); chain for heavy FRC lifts (heavy but easy to tension: YETI). Slides: aluminium extrusion with bearing blocks, steel drawer slides (e.g. Misumi SAR (brand)), or fully 3D-printed stages. Pulleys: small ball bearings (REV sells 4 mm thick, 12 mm diameter pulley bearings: gm0) or printed pulleys with bearings pressed in. Elastic retraction uses surgical tubing. PTFE tube (1/8 in) with push-to-connect fittings can route string round corners (gm0).
- **maker_difficulty**: moderate: every part is simple, but gm0 calls rigging 'an important and time-consuming requirement', and getting even tension on both sides, the right spool sizes and a retraction path takes patience

### Making it

- **processes**: 
  - 3D print (FDM): pulleys, spools, bearing blocks, string anchors, slide inserts, and even complete printed slide stages (Instructables '3D Printable Cascading Linear Slide')
  - CNC: aluminium plates and bearing blocks for FRC-scale elevators
  - off-the-shelf part: drawer slides, linear slide kits, pulley bearings, braided line, timing belts, chain and sprockets, constant-force springs *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **print_in_place**: no: the stages, pulleys and spools are printed (or bought) separately, then assembled and rigged with string or belt by hand
- **making_tips_3d_printing**: 1) Pulleys: printed pulleys can carry a pressed-in ball bearing; press it in with a clamp, vice or arbor press rather than hammering, which can crack the pulley or damage the bearing (Instructables cascading slide). 2) Abrasive lines such as kevlar cut into plastic: tie them round a washer rather than through a hole in a printed part, so the load is spread (gm0). 3) Anchor strings by clamping them under a bolt head rather than knotting them; it makes re-tensioning quick (gm0), and slippery UHMWPE line holds knots poorly (Wikipedia 'Ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene'). 4) Make the spool wide enough that the string never winds over itself, or its diameter and speed change (gm0). 5) Belts: the Instructables cascading slide loops belt over printed pulleys with about 50 mm of overlap in a printed belt clamp; the XRP two-stage elevator on Printables even prints its belt in TPU (tension is taken up with extra hardware). 6) Add a spring tensioner near the end of each string run (gm0). 7) Material: PLA softens above about 60 °C (Prusa), so use PETG (fine below about 80 °C: Prusa) for pulleys, spools and slide inserts that see load, friction heat or a hot car. 9) Line up every pulley in one plane with the string path: 'any amount of misalignment can lead to the string coming off of your pulley' (gm0). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **off_the_shelf_parts**: Telescopic linear slides or drawer slides (ball-bearing telescopic slides; Misumi SAR two- and three-stage slides (brand); REV linear motion kit (brand)); slide inserts or pulley blocks; small ball bearings used as pulleys (e.g. 3x12x4 mm V-groove bearings in Brogan Pratt's printed insert; REV 4 mm thick, 12 mm pulley bearings); spools or winch drums; braided UHMWPE line (Dyneema/Spectra (brands)) or kevlar cord; GT2 or HTD timing belt with belt clamps and pulleys; roller chain and sprockets with inline chain tensioners; extension springs or spring-loaded pulleys as tensioners; constant-force springs for counterbalancing; surgical tubing for elastic retraction; 1/8 in PTFE tube with M5 push-to-connect fittings (John Guest (brand)) for routing string; cable carriers (drag chain, e.g. igus e-chain (brand)) or coil cord to carry wires along the extension.
- **rules_of_thumb**: 
  - Cascade speed: stage 2 moves 2x as fast as stage 1 relative to the base, stage 3 3x, and so on (gm0 'Rigging').
  - Adding stages to a cascade needs the gear ratio multiplied by (new number of stages / old number) to keep the same speed; e.g. a 1-stage lift at 3:1 needs 9:1 as a 3-stage lift (gm0 'Rigging').
  - Cascade force: roughly N times the load at the top for N moving stages; YETI gives '2x force on the first stage' for a two-stage lift (YETI Elevator Design Guide).
  - Continuous rigging: the extension and retraction spools should be the same diameter, and continuous spools can use a relatively low gear ratio (gm0).
  - Mixing methods: cascade extension with continuous retraction needs the extension spool N times smaller than the retraction spool; continuous extension with cascade retraction needs the extension spool N times bigger (gm0).
  - Changing spool size instead of gear ratio: new spool diameter = old diameter x old ratio / new ratio, e.g. 2 in x 3.7 / 5 when going from 3.7:1 to 5:1 (gm0).
  - Fit at least one spring tensioner per linear slide set, near the end of the string run (gm0).
  - Rig the extension string with the slides retracted and the retraction string with them fully extended (gm0).
  - Free-floating retraction (string straight from the last stage to the spool) only for purely vertical slides; keep it parallel to the slides or it bends them (gm0).
  - Stage overlap about 20% of stage length (e.g. 6 in on a 30 in stage), and at least 2 bearing blocks per side between stages (YETI Elevator Design Guide).
  - Cascade is considered harder to rig above about three stages, whereas adding stages to a continuous lift is 'relatively trivial' (FRCDesign cascade elevator introduction).

### When to use it

- **everyday_examples**: 
  - A forklift truck's mast: the lift chain is anchored at one end, runs over a pulley on top of the rising cylinder and down to the fork carriage, so the forks rise twice as far as the cylinder (fork-lift-training.co.uk); triplex and quad masts add more nested stages (Logisnext)
  - The telescoping lifts and reach arms on FIRST Tech Challenge and FIRST Robotics Competition robots (gm0; FRCDesign)
  - Kit slide systems such as the REV DUO three-stage cascading lift (REV Robotics docs)
- **when_to_use**: Use it when something must reach much further than the space it packs into, such as a robot lift, a reach-out arm, a camera or sensor mast, or a model crane, and you want one motor to drive every stage. Choose cascade when you want smooth, predictable motion with every stage moving together, faster top-stage speed, strong pull-down for climbing from the early stages, or easy use of constant-force springs (FRCDesign; Chief Delphi 2022). Choose continuous when you want a low centre of gravity until you really need height (the heavy lower stages only move at the end), simpler gearing, or many stages (Waffles wiki; FRCDesign). Use belts instead of string when you want no re-tensioning and better precision and have room for bigger pulleys (gm0).
- **when_not_to_use**: Avoid string rigging when the load must be pushed hard, held rigidly against side forces, or positioned precisely without a sensor: use a lead screw or ball screw, a rack and pinion, or a rigid chain actuator (push chain) instead. For one short stage, a single belt or chain loop or a lead screw is simpler. Avoid elastic (surgical tubing) retraction if speed matters: it slows extension considerably and its force changes with extension (gm0). Avoid continuous rigging if the intermediate stages must be in a known position (Chief Delphi 2022).
- **environment_limits**: Braided UHMWPE line creeps (slowly stretches) under constant tension (Wikipedia), and string in general loosens over time (gm0), so expect to re-tension. UHMWPE fibre should not be used above about 90 °C for long periods (Wikipedia, citing DSM). Printed PLA pulleys and spools soften above about 60 °C (Prusa); PETG is fine below about 80 °C (Prusa). *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **safety_notes**: The sliding stages and pulleys form pinch and shear points that move quickly (in cascade the top stage moves several times faster than the first), so keep fingers clear and test at low power. A lift can drop suddenly if a string snaps, an anchor slips or the motor loses power (Chief Delphi 2019). A tall extended lift raises the centre of gravity, especially cascade lifts at mid height (Waffles wiki), so a robot or stand can tip. UHMWPE line carries far less snap-back energy than steel wire rope (Wikipedia), but any tensioned line can whip if it breaks. *(unverified parts omitted)*
- **common_failures**: 1) String jumps off a pulley because it is not pulled straight (gm0): line up pulleys and add guards or shields (a printed 'bearing shield' for line retention exists on Printables). 2) Slack from string stretch, knots slipping, and the changing string path (gm0): clamp strings under bolts and fit spring tensioners. 3) String overlapping on the spool, changing its diameter so the extension and retraction spools fight or go slack (gm0): make the spool wider. 4) Left and right slides at different tension so the stages twist and bind (racking) (gm0 'Tensioners'; Chief Delphi 2022). 5) Free-floating retraction string tangling (gm0). 6) Elastic retraction making extension slow and letting the spool unwind (gm0). 7) Intermediate stages of a continuous lift moving in an unexpected order because of uneven friction (Chief Delphi 2012; 2022). 8) Abrasive string sawing through plastic parts (gm0: use a washer). 9) Wires to the moving end snagging: use a cable carrier or coil cord (gm0).
- **related**: 
  - Drawer slide (telescopic ball-bearing slide): the nested slide hardware itself; rigging is what drives it
  - Belt or chain elevator (single stage): one closed loop bolted to a carriage; simplest powered lift but only one stage (gm0)
  - Forklift mast chain: a one-stage cascade (chain anchored to the fixed part, over a pulley on the moving part) that doubles lift height (fork-lift-training.co.uk)
  - Block and tackle (pulley system): uses pulleys the other way round, trading speed for extra pulling force
  - Lead screw or ball screw: rigid push and pull in both directions and self-holding (lead screw), but slower and single-stage
  - Rigid chain actuator (push chain, zip chain): can push as well as pull and packs away small
  - Rack and pinion: rigid and positive, but a single stage per rack
  - Constant-force spring counterbalance: often added to a rigged lift to cancel its weight
  - Telescoping tube locks (twist lock, flip lock, spring button): hold a hand-set length instead of driving the stages
