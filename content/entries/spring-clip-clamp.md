Two jaws joined by a pivot and closed by a spring (usually a coiled wire torsion spring, sometimes rubber bands or a springy printed arm), so you squeeze the handles to open it and let go to clamp it onto cloth, paper, a board edge, a rod or a cable.

**In plain words:** A clip with two jaws that pivot like scissors and are pushed shut by a spring: you pinch the handles to open it, put it over the edge, and let go so it grips, like a clothes peg, a workshop spring clamp or a clip-on lamp or phone holder. **Proper term:** Spring clamp (spring clip, spring-loaded pivoted jaw clamp; clothes peg).

**Also called:** clothes peg (UK) / clothespin or clothes pin (US) (Wikipedia 'Clothespin'); spring clothespin / sprung wooden clothes peg; pinch clamp (This Old House); spring clamp, spring cramp (UK woodworking; listed with temporary clamps in Wikipedia 'Clamp (tool)'); plastic spring clamp, e.g. Clippix (Bessey, brand); needle-nose spring clamp; ratchet spring clamp (Bessey XCR, a ratchet-locking cousin); muslin clamp / backdrop clamp / photo spring clip (photography studio names seen in Woodsmith's spring clamp review); chip clip / bag clip (kitchen use; Printables model titles); C47 / CP47 / 'bullet' (film-set slang for a wooden spring clothes peg, Wikipedia 'Clothespin'); crocodile clip / alligator clip (spring-jaw clip for temporary electrical connections, Wikipedia 'Crocodile clip'); rubber-band clamp / rubber band spring clamp (printed clamp that uses rubber bands as the spring, Printables 617245); springless clothespin / one-piece peg / dolly peg (one-piece version whose own springy prongs grip, Wikipedia; Printables 33109); clip-on holder / clip-on lamp / clamp lamp / clip-on phone holder.

## How it works

Each half is a lever with the pivot between the handle and the jaw. The spring pushes the handles apart, which pulls the jaws together, so the clip grips on its own; squeezing the handles harder than the spring opens the jaws ('By a lever action, when the two prongs are pinched at the top of the peg, the prongs open up, and when released, the spring draws the two prongs shut', Wikipedia 'Clothespin'). In the classic wooden peg one coiled wire is both the spring and the pivot the two halves rock on (Wikipedia). A coiled (torsion) spring pushes back harder the further it is twisted (Wikipedia 'Torsion spring'), and the grip at the jaws is the spring's turning force divided by the distance from the pivot (lever arithmetic), so it is greatest close to the pivot. The jaws hold by friction, helped by teeth on crocodile clips and toothed pegs; the clamping force is small (up to about 100-150 N for metal workshop spring clamps, Bessey).

## Parts

A few parts: two jaw-and-handle halves (wood, plastic or steel), a pivot (a pin or rivet, or the coil of the spring itself acting as the hinge), and a spring (a wire torsion spring, a trapped compression spring, rubber bands, or a springy part of the clip itself); often soft or swivelling jaw pads and grips on the handles. Examples: 'two wooden halves and one wire spring that is both spring and pivot' (Solon E. Moore's 1887 clothespin, Wikipedia 'Clothespin'); 'two printed halves, snap-on jaw pads, rubber bands' (Printables 617245).

## Everyday examples

- clothes pegs on a washing line (Wikipedia 'Clothespin')
- small spring clamps holding a glue-up, a drop cloth or a tarp (This Old House)
- crocodile clips on electrical test leads and car jump leads (Wikipedia 'Crocodile clip')
- chip or bag clips, and clip-on reading lamps and phone holders

## When to use it

When you need to hold something quickly with one hand and take it off just as quickly: small glue-ups (luthiers use clothes pegs to hold kerfing while the glue sets, Wikipedia), holding cloth, paper, tarps or photo backdrops, holding parts while marking or fitting, clipping a light, phone, sign or sensor onto a shelf edge, rod or strap, and holding gels on hot film lights with wooden pegs (Wikipedia). It needs no tools, doesn't mark the work if it has pads, and costs almost nothing.

## When not to use it

When you need high or adjustable pressure, use a screw clamp (C-clamp or G-clamp, F-clamp or bar clamp), or a toggle clamp for fast, repeatable, firm holding. For a semi-permanent clip-on mount on a bar, pole or desk edge, use a screw-tightened clamp-on mount or U-bolt. To hold sheets with holes in them, use temporary sheet fasteners (Cleco-type pins). For anything overhead, anything that could fall and cause harm (such as a heat lamp), any lifting and any person, use rated fixings and a chain or safety line instead; NDSU Extension says heat lamps 'should be secured as if they are permanent', with a chain and a locking chain connector. For electrical connections, crocodile clips are only for temporary test connections (see Binding post and banana plug); mains wiring must follow local law and never uses improvised clips.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: grips cloth, paper, sheet, a board edge, a rod or a cable, for a few minutes (glue-ups, laundry) or as a clip-on mount for a lamp, phone or sensor
  - lock, and release by hand: it holds by itself and lets go when you squeeze the handles
- **Movement:** rotate: the jaws pivot open about the pin; workshop spring clamps open about 20-75 mm (Bessey XC and XM ranges), and Bessey's variable-opening XV up to 170 mm
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 1 while opening and closing (the jaws turn about the pivot); 0 once clamped, as long as the grip is not overcome. Swivelling jaw pads add a small tilt at each tip.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - quick-release: pinch to open, let go to clamp, with one hand (This Old House: spring clamps 'can be operated with one hand')
  - self-aligning or self-centring: many spring clamps have swivelling pads at the jaw tips (Woodsmith mentions 'swiveling red pads at the tips of the jaw') so the pads lie flat on surfaces that are not parallel
- **How it holds:** spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place), producing friction or clamping (force-fit) at the jaws; toothed or serrated jaws add a little shape grip
- **Input and output:** not applicable (it holds rather than passing motion on; the handles are simply levers that open the jaws)
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Light: the grip comes only from the spring. Bessey's metal spring clamps give clamping forces of up to 100 N at 25 mm opening, 120 N at 50 mm and 150 N at 75 mm; This Old House says spring clamps 'aren't the best option for heavy-duty tasks'. A sliding pull along the jaw faces is resisted only by friction, so a firm tug slides it off; prying or twisting levers the jaws open. Not for lifting, holding anything overhead or holding a person.
- **Play and precision:** Not a locating joint: it holds wherever it is clipped and can shift if knocked. Swivelling jaw pads tolerate faces that are not parallel; plain jaws grip best on parallel faces near the pivot.
- **Taking it apart:** tool-free quick release: squeeze the handles and lift it off
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: metal-spring pegs and clamps last until the spring tires or the halves come apart; printed plastic springs last far fewer cycles, especially in PLA (Printables 1096058, 1707408)
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** Mostly no: the spring sets the force and you simply re-clip to change position. Rubber-band clamps get stronger as you add bands (Printables 617245, 982731), and Bessey's VarioClippix XV has a variable opening width.
- **Access needed:** both sides: the jaws close on both faces of the part, so the clip must reach over an edge (or round a rod, cable or rope) and needs room for the jaws to open
- **Materials:** Classic pegs: wood with a steel wire spring; wooden pegs are used on hot film lights because they do not pass heat on, while plastic would melt and metal would get too hot to touch (Wikipedia 'Clothespin'). Plastic pegs; stainless steel pegs with a trapped compression spring for outdoor use (Wikipedia). Workshop spring clamps: nickel-plated steel with PVC tips and handles 'to prevent marring' (Bessey XM), or fibreglass-reinforced plastic (Bessey Clippix XC, XV, XCL). Crocodile clips: steel or, for battery jump leads, solid copper (Wikipedia 'Crocodile clip'). Printed: PETG for any printed spring, PLA only for rigid halves (Printables 1096058, 1707408; Hackaday); TPU or rubber pads on the jaws. It grips almost any material; pads protect soft or finished surfaces.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Heat: wooden pegs stay safe to touch on hot film lights, whereas plastic would melt and metal would get too hot (Wikipedia 'Clothespin'); PLA starts to give at about 60 degC and PETG at about 80 degC (CNC Kitchen). Outdoors: torsion-spring pegs can twist and fall apart, which is why stainless steel pegs with a trapped compression spring are made, as they 'do not rust or decay with outdoor use' (Wikipedia). Plastic pegs left in sunlight go brittle ⚑ [uncertain: common experience, not found on an opened page]. Rubber: natural rubber bands crack from ozone when stretched (Wikipedia 'Ozone cracking'). Over time: printed springs lose force if left clamped (stress relaxation, filamentspecs). Vibration: a friction-only grip can creep or walk off a vibrating edge ⚑ [uncertain: not found on an opened page].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: clothes pegs, metal and plastic spring clamps, crocodile clips, clip-on lamps and phone holders
  - 3D print (FDM): one-piece pegs with a flexing printed spring, print-in-place pegs, two-piece rubber-band clamps, printed halves around a reclaimed metal spring, and replacement jaw pads
  - woodwork: the classic two-piece wooden peg with a wire spring, and wooden jaws for larger rubber-band or spring clamps
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** yes for one-piece and print-in-place pegs, whose spring is part of the print and which are ready to use straight off the printer (Printables 33109, 659163, 1096058; Printables 1707408 needs its centre snapped together once after printing); partly for rubber-band clamps (two printed halves plus added rubber bands, Printables 617245) and for printed clamps built around a metal spring and screws (Printables 559737)
- **Tools:** none to use. To build a printed clamp around a strong reclaimed metal spring: locking pliers, a vice to squeeze the handles together and safety glasses (Printables 559737); rubber bands for rubber-band clamps; a pin, screw or rivet for the pivot of two-part designs
- **Difficulty:** easy: buy them cheaply, or print two halves and join them with rubber bands or a pin; moderate for a one-piece or print-in-place peg, whose printed spring has to be tuned and printed in PETG, or for fitting a strong metal spring into printed halves (Printables 559737 warns the assembly 'can be tricky or dangerous')

### 3D printing tips

Spring material: print anything that has to spring back in PETG, not PLA; one designer found 'the spring from PLA doesnt really hold up that well but petg works great' (Printables 1096058), another says a PLA spring 'may wear out or crack sooner' (Printables 1707408), and Hackaday notes 'PLA makes for a very poor spring material'. Plastic held bent for a long time slowly loses force (stress relaxation: 'a clip or clamp loses holding force', filamentspecs), so don't store printed clips clamped shut on something thick, and use rubber bands or a metal spring where it must keep gripping for months. Orientation: print the halves lying flat so the bending runs along the layers; Printables 617245 'prints flat for much greater strength', and CNC Kitchen's hooks printed standing broke at only 46-55% of the load of flat ones. Settings seen on popular models: for rubber-band clamp halves, PLA at 0.2 mm layers, 3 perimeters and 15% infill (Printables 617245); for a print-in-place PETG peg, 3 perimeters and no supports (Printables 1707408); some one-piece pegs suggest 100% infill (Printables 904305). Rubber bands: add as many as you need for the force you want (Printables 617245, 982731); natural rubber bands crack quickly when kept stretched in air (ozone cracking), while EPDM and silicone O-rings resist ozone (Wikipedia 'Ozone cracking'), so O-rings make longer-lasting springs. Jaw gap: for springless pegs pick the gap to suit the thickness held; Printables 33109 comes in 1, 2, 3 and 4 mm gaps. Pivots between printed parts need at least 0.3 mm clearance to move freely (Prusa 'Modeling with 3D printing in mind'). Reusing a metal spring: Printables 559737 takes a spring with wire up to 2.5 mm thick, a coil about 10 mm high and 15 mm across and legs up to 50 mm long, prints its jaws flat with supports, and assembles the spring with locking pliers and a vice while wearing safety glasses. Heat: PLA starts to give at about 60 degC (CNC Kitchen), so a PLA clip on a lamp or left in a hot car will soften; PETG lasted to about 80 degC in the same test. Printed replacement tips and jaws for bought spring clamps are common (Printables 586755, 466832).

## Rules of thumb

- Expect light clamping force: metal spring clamps give up to about 100 N (25 mm opening), 120 N (50 mm) and 150 N (75 mm) (Bessey, https://bessey.de/getmedia/4aaf3598-88bf-4334-8867-07e665352c04/pr-bessey-xm-xv-xc-xcl-xcr__en); for big glue-ups use bar or screw clamps, which give 'immense, even pressure' (This Old House, https://www.thisoldhouse.com/house-one/clamp-guide).
- A coiled torsion spring's turning force is proportional to how far it is twisted (Wikipedia 'Torsion spring', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_spring), so a spring clamp grips a thick part harder than a thin one, and gripping close to the pivot gives more force than at the jaw tips (lever arithmetic; no clamp test opened).
- Print springy parts in PETG, not PLA (Printables 1096058 and 1707408; Hackaday, https://hackaday.com/2026/04/27/the-challenges-of-3d-printing-reliable-springs/).
- Print clamp halves and pegs lying flat, so bending loads run along the layers (Printables 617245; CNC Kitchen found standing prints broke at about half the load, https://www.cnckitchen.com/blog/comparing-pla-petg-amp-asa-feat-prusament).
- Plastic held at a fixed bend loses force over time (stress relaxation; https://filamentspecs.com/creep-resistance-3d-printed-parts/): for clips that grip for months, use a metal spring or replaceable rubber bands or O-rings.
- Rubber-band clamps: add bands until the grip is enough (Printables 617245); natural rubber cracks from ozone under very small stretch, while EPDM, silicone and fluoroelastomer rubbers resist it (Wikipedia 'Ozone cracking', https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ozone_cracking).
- Give a printed pivot at least 0.3 mm clearance (Prusa, https://help.prusa3d.com/article/modeling-with-3d-printing-in-mind_164135).

## Common failures

(1) The torsion spring twists sideways and the two halves fall apart (Wikipedia 'Clothespin'); stainless pegs with a trapped compression spring avoid this. (2) Swivelling jaw pads pop off and get lost (Woodsmith); print replacements (Printables 586755, 466832). (3) Cheap plastic handles or jaws break while the spring survives ('The only durable part in spring clamps is the spring', Printables 559737); reuse the spring in printed parts. (4) Printed PLA springs crack or go limp (Printables 1096058, 1707408; Hackaday); use PETG. (5) A printed clip left clamped slowly loses its grip (stress relaxation, filamentspecs). (6) Rubber bands perish and snap (Wikipedia 'Ozone cracking'); swap in EPDM or silicone O-rings. (7) Not enough force, so the work slips; use a screw or bar clamp (This Old House). (8) Printed halves split along layer lines when printed standing; print flat (Printables 617245; CNC Kitchen).

## Safety

Pinch points: strong metal clamps snap shut hard on fingers and skin ⚑ [uncertain: general caution]. Fitting a strong metal spring into a home-made clamp 'can be tricky or dangerous'; wear safety glasses and squeeze it together in a vice rather than by hand (Printables 559737). Never rely on a spring clip alone to hold a heat lamp or anything hot or heavy overhead: heat lamps over straw bedding add to the risk of barn fires, so NDSU Extension says to hang them with a chain and a locking chain connector so they can't fall into the straw, and Dorset & Wiltshire Fire and Rescue Service says to secure them to something solid 'using chain not twine'. Never use one to lift, to hold anything overhead or to hold a person. Small clips and loose springs are a choking hazard for small children ⚑ [uncertain: general caution]. Crocodile clips are for temporary test connections; never improvise mains connections with them.
