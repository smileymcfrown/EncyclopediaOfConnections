A strap is overlapped on itself or round a buckle, ring or slot and sewn with a dense, planned stitch pattern (bar tacks, a box with an X, W shapes or close rows) so that the pull is shared by many stitches and the joint can carry a large share of the webbing's own strength.

**In plain words:** The dense blocks of stitching you see where a strap is folded back and sewn to itself or round a buckle or ring: a tight zigzag bar, a box with an X through it, or rows of W shapes, which spread the pull over hundreds of stitches so the joint doesn't tear. **Proper term:** Load-bearing stitch pattern for webbing (bar tack, box-X stitch, W-W stitch; sewn webbing joint).

**Also called:** bar tack, bar-tack, bartack: a short, dense block of zigzag stitches (Wikipedia 'Bar tack'); on parachutes typically a '1 inch x 42 stitch bar tack' (FAA Parachute Rigger Handbook); box-X stitch, box X, X-box, boxed cross (Fire Engineering 1984; Ripstop by the Roll; Sailrite); W-W pattern, WW, double W, three-point or four-point double W (FAA; Fire Engineering 1984); Z stitch, zigzag pattern (Ripstop by the Roll); diamond stitch pattern (Sailrite); straight-stitch rows, parallel rows, reinforcement stitches (Practical Sailor; Sailrite); sewn loop, sewn eye, webbing loop, stitched webbing join, webbing junction (Sailrite; Practical Sailor; FAA); sandwich (inserted) reinforcement: an extra strip of webbing sewn inside the overlap (Practical Sailor); lockstitch (stitch type 301) and zigzag lockstitch (stitch types 304 and 308), the machine stitches used (FAA); arrowhead tack and crow's foot tack (garment relatives of the bar tack) (Wikipedia 'Bar tack').

## How it works

Each stitch is a small loop of thread through both layers; when the strap is pulled, the layers try to slide apart and every stitch is loaded across its thread, so the joint's strength is roughly the number of stitches times the strength of each (Sailrite's formula: stitches per inch x thread strength x 1.5 = pounds per inch of stitching). The patterns put many stitches into a short overlap and run lines across and along the strap so the load is spread (Ripstop by the Roll; Sailrite). Stretchy nylon webbing loads the stitches nearest the free tail end first, so joints fail from the tail row and then unzip in a moment; dense stitching close to the ends, and a stiffening strip sewn inside the overlap, make the stitches share the load more evenly (Practical Sailor; Fire Engineering 1984).

## Parts

A few parts: the webbing (doubled over or overlapped), the thread, and often the hardware it wraps round (buckle, D-ring, slide or a slot in a rigid part); sometimes an extra strip of webbing sandwiched inside the overlap (Practical Sailor). Made with a lockstitch sewing machine (with zigzag for bar tacks) or by hand with a sailmaker's needle and palm (Sailrite; Practical Sailor).

## Everyday examples

- The box-X or bar-tacked joints where backpack and bag straps are sewn round a buckle or D-ring (Ripstop by the Roll; Sailrite)
- The bar tacks on a climbing sling (Practical Sailor: climbing slings 'aren't sewn in an X; they are bar-tacked')
- The W-W stitch blocks on a parachute harness where the chest strap joins the main lift web (FAA: 'Most chest straps are installed with a 3-point WW')
- The small bar tacks at the bottom of a jeans fly and on belt loops and pocket corners (Wikipedia 'Bar tack')

## When to use it

Use a sewn pattern to make a permanent loop, eye or attachment in webbing for bags, backpack straps, camera and instrument straps, pet leads, tool rolls, cargo and storage straps, sail ties and reefing straps, and to fix a strap to a buckle, D-ring, slide or a slot in a 3D-printed or wooden part (Sailrite; Practical Sailor; Ripstop by the Roll). Sewing is the standard way to join webbing, because knots in webbing are bulky and weaken it ('reduce strength 40 percent or more'; Practical Sailor). Choose a bar tack when you have a zigzag machine and a short overlap, a box-X for a neat, sideways-strong joint on a home machine, and W or diamond patterns plus reinforcement rows when you want to calculate the strength (Sailrite; Ripstop by the Roll; Fire Engineering 1984).

## When not to use it

Never home-sew anything that holds a person or a heavy moving load: climbing slings, harnesses, tethers, tow straps or lifting straps. Climbing slings must meet EN 566 (at least 22 kN since 1994; UIAA/BMC), parachute harness work may only be done by certificated master riggers (FAA), and even Sailrite's guide, which shows DIY loops for towing and lifting dinghies, carries the disclaimer that it 'is not corroborated or verified by a safety authority'. Buy certified, factory-made gear instead. Don't use a sewn joint where the strap must be adjusted (use a ladder-lock buckle, cam buckle or tri-glide), or opened and closed often (use a side-release buckle or hook-and-loop). For wire rope use crimped sleeves or clips; for rope use a splice or a knot. Avoid random heavy stitching in place of a planned pattern: Fire Engineering (1984) says equipment sewn that way should be destroyed.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: joins a strap to itself (a loop or eye), to a buckle, ring, slide or slot, or to another strap (Sailrite; Ripstop by the Roll)
- **Movement:** none: the sewn joint doesn't move; the strap can still swivel round the bar of the hardware it is sewn round.
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 between the sewn layers; a loop sewn round a ring or bar can still turn about that bar (1).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it simply holds. (Contrasting thread makes broken stitches easy to see, which helps inspection; Practical Sailor; Sailrite.)
- **How it holds:**
  - shape (the parts interlock; [form-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): the thread passes through both layers, so they can't slide apart without breaking or tearing out the stitches
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): tensioned stitches also press the layers together, which helps share the load (FAA: thread tension affects seam strength)
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed).
- **Load:** Medium to heavy along the strap; the joint is strongest when the pull is straight along the overlap (stitches in shear) and weakest when the loop is spread open, which [peels](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear) the stitches at the throat one row at a time: Practical Sailor found any webbing eye 'will peel apart at 500 to 800 pounds' if spread wide. Published efficiencies (joint strength as a share of webbing strength) differ, side by side: hand-stitched nylon levels off at about 69% with plain stitching, 83% with more rows and 100% with a sandwiched webbing insert, and 'commercial bar tacking is known to be about 90 percent efficient' (Practical Sailor); Sailrite's machine-sewn loops always broke below unstitched webbing, e.g. two W samples at 4,300 lb (Sailrite); Fire Engineering's 1984 test on 9,000 lb webbing saw stitching start to break at 3,000 lb (W) and 3,500 lb (box), with the box pattern failing at 4,500 lb, and much lower figures with thinner bobbin thread (Fire Engineering 1984). A badly made harness of 6,000 lb webbing may hold only 4,000 lb (Fire Engineering 1984).
- **Play and precision:** not applicable
- **Taking it apart:** semi-permanent (can be undone but may damage parts): stitches can be cut out with a seam ripper or scalpel, as riggers do for repairs, but every needle hole stays, and the FAA lists 'the number of times the webbing is re-sewn' among the factors that lower the strength of the junction (FAA).
- **How often it can be reused:** once: re-sewing the same webbing is possible but weakens it each time (FAA); for anything important use new webbing.
- **Sealing:** none.
- **Adjustable:** no: the overlap length and position are fixed once sewn; add a buckle or slide for adjustment.
- **Access needed:** both sides: the needle passes through all the layers and the machine's bobbin (or a hand needle) works from underneath, so the joint must be sewn before closed hardware is trapped in a finished assembly.
- **Materials:** Webbing: nylon (stretchy, soft, strongest thread grip), polyester (better in sun and for marine use), polypropylene (cheap, for bags and crafts), Dyneema; flat or tubular (Sailrite; Practical Sailor). Thread: bonded nylon or bonded polyester, polyester only for outdoor use, nothing thinner than Tex 90 (Sailrite); parachute harnesses use 5-cord nylon (about 40 lb) and canopies E thread (about 8.5 lb) to V-T-295 (FAA); by hand, doubled 40-50 lb polyester whipping twine (Practical Sailor). Avoid cotton (a harness re-sewn with cotton thread broke at about 10 lb per thread versus 45 lb for 5-cord nylon; FAA) and Dyneema or Kevlar twine on nylon webbing (Dyneema twine 'cuts through, resulting in a 30 percent loss in strength'; Practical Sailor). Hardware sewn in: metal or plastic buckles, D-rings, slides, or a rigid 3D-printed part with slots.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Sunlight (UV) weakens nylon webbing and thread: use polyester thread outdoors and webbing with twice the needed strength (Sailrite); Practical Sailor notes nylon 'has never been very good in the sun' and found sewn failures only where webbing was sun-rotted or badly abraded, and suggests a coat of paint or a cover to protect stitching. Store webbing clean, dry and out of the sun (Sailrite; UIAA/BMC). Heat: nylon and Dyneema have low melting points; a loaded rope pulled through a nylon sling can melt it 'in as little as 3 metres of lowering' (UIAA/BMC). Wear: retire webbing with about 30% of its fibres worn, or if it crackles when flexed (Sailrite). Printed hardware: PLA [creeps](../glossary.md#creep) under steady load (maker note, Printables 415716). Exact temperature limits for webbing and thread were not checked ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - sewing by machine (lockstitch, with zigzag for bar tacks) or by hand: not one of the standard making methods, but the core process here
  - off-the-shelf part: webbing, bonded thread, buckles, D-rings and slides
  - 3D print (FDM): buckles, slides, D-rings and slotted rigid parts that the strap is sewn round (Printables 305674, 228168)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: it is sewn; only the hardware the strap is sewn to may be printed.
- **Tools:** A lockstitch sewing machine that can feed two or more layers of webbing (Sailrite says lockstitch is 'the only' type recommended for load-bearing equipment; Fire Engineering 1984 says the same), with zigzag for bar tacks (Sailrite; Ripstop by the Roll); a strong needle in good condition (FAA: blunt or damaged needles weaken webbing); or, by hand, a sailmaker's needle and sailmaker's palm (Practical Sailor). Also: scissors or a hot knife to cut and seal synthetic webbing, a marking pencil and ruler to draw the pattern, basting tape to stop the layers sliding, and a seam ripper (Sailrite; FAA).
- **Difficulty:** moderate: a home or portable walking-foot machine and the right thread can sew bar tacks and box-X joints for bags, straps and gear (Ripstop by the Roll; Sailrite), but thick layers and heavy thread may need a heavy-duty or semi-industrial machine (Sailrite), and life-safety straps must not be home-made (UIAA; FAA).

### 3D printing tips

1) Size the slot of a printed buckle, slide or D-ring from the measured webbing, not its nominal size: one printed tri-glide made for '1 inch' webbing uses 27 mm slots because that is the real width (Printables 305674); leave room for the doubled strap's thickness. 2) Print strap hardware flat so the strap's pull runs along the [layer lines](../glossary.md#layer-lines), with four or more walls (Printables 228168; Printables 415716 suggests 4-5 [perimeters](../glossary.md#perimeters) and 100% [infill](../glossary.md#infill) for a strong buckle). 3) Prefer PETG for printed strap hardware; PLA slowly 'flows' (creeps) under constant load (maker notes, Printables 415716 and 602174). 4) A closed printed bar must be threaded onto the strap before the loop is sewn; if the strap must be added later, use a slotted 'no-thread' design (Printables 305674) or a screw-clamp repair buckle (Printables 1333399). 5) Keep the stitch pattern far enough from the printed part for the presser foot and needle to clear it; Fire Engineering's 1984 guidance asks for a stitched area at least 3 in long where hardware is attached, and Sailrite suggests an overlap about three times the webbing width for 1 in webbing. 6) Don't stitch over melted (heat-sealed) webbing ends: the hard burr abrades the thread (Practical Sailor); fold the end under or keep it clear of the stitches. 7) To sew webbing onto a printed part instead of round it, see Sew-on part (sewing holes, loops and slots on a rigid part).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Catalogue names: 'bonded nylon thread' and 'bonded polyester thread' in Tex 90 (V-92) and Tex 135 (V-138) (Sailrite: 'V-92 ... is the same as Tex 90'; 'Tex 135 (V-138)'), or lighter for bags only; parachute 'nylon thread V-T-295' sizes E, FF, 3-cord and 5-cord (FAA); 'polyester whipping twine' (Practical Sailor); heavy needles, size 18 and up for E (V-69) thread (FAA), size 20 used by Sailrite for Tex 90; 'sailmaker's needle' and 'sailmaker's palm' (Practical Sailor); nylon, polyester and polypropylene 'flat webbing' and 'tubular webbing' (Sailrite); 'basting tape' (Sailrite); 'hot knife'; 'seam ripper' (FAA); machines: portable walking-foot, compound-feed, heavy-duty harness and bar-tack machines (FAA lists examples; Sailrite tested on an Ultrafeed LSZ, brand). Brand names are given only as catalogue search terms.

## Rules of thumb

- Stitching needed (Sailrite): seam strength in lb per inch of stitching = stitches per inch x thread strength (lb) x 1.5; e.g. 8 SPI x 15 lb (Tex 90) x 1.5 = 180 lb/in, so a 4,050 lb loop needs 22.5 in of total stitching. Add extra stitching as a safety factor, because some samples broke below the calculated figure.
- Stitches per inch, side by side: 8-12 SPI is 'generally recommended for sewing webbing' (Sailrite, 2019, Tex 90 thread); 4-7 SPI for safety equipment, as more than seven reduces elasticity and cuts fibres and fewer than four snags (Fire Engineering 1984, size 6 thread); 7-11 SPI for parachute canopy lockstitch and zigzag work (FAA). The FAA warns that 'too many stitches per inch dramatically affects the strength of the seam by perforating the material'.
- Pattern strength, side by side: the FAA says 'A W–W pattern is stronger than a box X pattern'; Fire Engineering (1984) found the three-point double W 'slightly stronger' than the boxed cross but the boxed cross 'more than adequate' and better where sideways strength matters; Sailrite (2019) found 'no clear "winning" stitch pattern' and that the amount of stitching matters most; Practical Sailor found that for nylon webbing only the stitching within about 1 inch of the ends made a real difference, and that X, box or Z arguments are 'likely a carryover' from canvas and polyester; Ripstop by the Roll calls the box-X 'one of the strongest attachment methods you can sew on a conventional machine'.
- Add plain reinforcement rows: Sailrite's W, diamond and box-X samples needed separate three-pass straight-stitch rows above and below the pattern to reach a reasonable strength; Practical Sailor's best hand-sewn pattern for 1 in, 4,000 lb webbing was eight rows of seven 1/8 in stitches with rows 1/8 in apart (average break 3,650 lb), and twice as many stitches for a closed loop whose ends face opposite ways.
- Overlap length: about three times the webbing width for 1 in webbing, and nearly twice the width for 2-3 in webbing, even if the formula needs less (Sailrite); a stitched pattern at least 3 in long where hardware is attached, with 120-150 stitches in a 3-1/2 x 1-3/4 in seam (Fire Engineering 1984); a webbing eye at least 2 in long for a 5/16 in carabiner or shackle, longer for bigger hardware (Practical Sailor).
- Edges and ends: keep stitching at least 1/4 in from the edge and backstitch at least 1/2 in at the end of every line (Fire Engineering 1984).
- Home-machine bar tack (Sailrite): Sailrite's samples used two straight passes to lock the layers, then three zigzag passes at 4 mm width and 4 mm stitch length, ten bar tacks 1/2 in apart, which proved more than needed (eight would do). Ripstop by the Roll: limit plain straight stitching to '3-4 passes at a max' because more holes weaken the webbing. Garment bar tacks are much smaller, about 1/16-1/8 in wide and 1/4-3/8 in long (Wikipedia 'Bar tack').
- A sandwiched insert of webbing inside the joint (Practical Sailor used a 3/4 in overlap) lets the far stitches share the load and reached 100% of the webbing's rated breaking strength in hand-sewn tests (Practical Sailor).
- Thread: bonded nylon or polyester, at least Tex 90, polyester for outdoor use, contrasting colour so popped stitches show (Sailrite); E (V-69) thread needs at least a size 18 needle (FAA); larger needles make larger holes and blunt needles damage webbing (FAA).
- Webbing: the loop can never be stronger than the webbing; choose webbing with a higher minimum breaking strength than needed, and about twice the needed strength for outdoor use because sunlight weakens it (Sailrite). Retire webbing when about 30% of the fibres are worn at any point (Sailrite).
- Test your joint: sew samples with the same materials and layers before sewing the project (Ripstop by the Roll).

## Common failures

1) Joint unzips from the tail end: the end row takes most of the load in stretchy nylon, then the rest fails at once (Practical Sailor). Fix: dense stitching near the ends, extra rows, or a sandwiched insert. 2) Too little stitching for the load (Sailrite's formula) or random stitching (Fire Engineering 1984). 3) Too many stitches per inch or too many rows, which perforate and weaken the webbing (FAA; Ripstop by the Roll). 4) Wrong thread: cotton (FAA), thread thinner than Tex 90 (Sailrite), Dyneema or Kevlar twine on nylon webbing, which cuts it (Practical Sailor), or a thin bobbin thread that breaks first (Fire Engineering 1984). 5) Damaged or oversized needles weakening the webbing (FAA). 6) Loose or uneven thread tension giving raised, skipped or weak stitches (Fire Engineering 1984; FAA). 7) Stitching over melted ends, whose burr abrades the thread (Practical Sailor). 8) Eye spread wide by a big shackle or several attachments, peeling the throat stitches (Practical Sailor). 9) No reinforcement rows above and below a W, diamond or box-X pattern (Sailrite). 10) Sun-rotted or abraded webbing tearing beside sound stitching (Practical Sailor).

## Safety

Never home-sew climbing slings, harnesses, safety tethers, tow straps or lifting straps; a failed sewn joint fails suddenly ('the rest of the stitching failed in a millisecond'; Practical Sailor). Life-safety slings must meet EN 566 (UIAA/BMC), and the FAA restricts harness repairs to certificated master riggers and gives a real case where a harness re-sewn with cotton thread broke at the junction (FAA). Spread loops peel apart at much lower loads than straight pulls (Practical Sailor). Use contrasting thread so broken stitches show, inspect stitching often, and retire worn webbing (Sailrite; UIAA/BMC). Hot knives and flame-sealing webbing ends are burn and fume hazards ⚑ [uncertain: no opened source states this]. Keep fingers clear of the needle when feeding thick webbing through a machine ⚑ [uncertain].
