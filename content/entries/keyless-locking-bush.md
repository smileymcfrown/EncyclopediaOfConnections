A set of tapered steel rings that screws draw together so they wedge inward onto a plain shaft and outward into a hub, locking a pulley, gear or sprocket in place by friction without any key or keyway.

**In plain words:** A set of metal sleeves that you slide into the gap between a smooth shaft and the hole in a pulley or gear; tightening a ring of screws squeezes the sleeves so they grip both parts hard enough to turn together, with no key or flat needed. Loosen the screws and you can slide or turn the part to a new spot. **Proper term:** Keyless locking device (keyless bushing, locking assembly, shaft-hub locking device).

**Also called:** keyless bushing / keyless bush; locking assembly / friction locking assembly (terms used by RINGFEDER (brand) and SKF (brand)); shaft locking device; shaft-hub locking device; keyless locking device, KLD (Climax (brand) term); taper-lock bush / taper bush / taper-fit bush: the close relative covered in this entry, a split bush with a tapered outside that is pulled into a matching tapered hole in the pulley and is usually fitted with a key; 'Taper-Lock' is a Fenner (brand) name; QD bushing (quick-disconnect bushing): a flanged, tapered, keyed relative (Wikipedia 'Key (engineering)'; SKF); single-nut keyless bushing, e.g. Trantorque (brand, Fenner); B-LOC (brand, Fenner), Posi-Lok (brand, Zero-Max), RfN 7012 and similar (brand, RINGFEDER), FX series (brand, SKF), ETP hydraulic bushing (brand, Zero-Max); shrink disc: the outside-clamping version that squeezes a hollow hub onto a shaft; screw-clamp bushing; flush-mount, flanged and hub-mount screw-clamp bushings (McMaster-Carr catalogue names); Spannsatz (German, 'clamping set').

## How it works

Two slopes (tapers) slide over each other like wedges. Tightening the screws pulls the tapered rings together along the shaft, which forces the split inner ring to shrink onto the shaft and the split outer ring to swell against the hub bore (Machine Design; SKF). The very high sideways pressure (radial contact pressure) all round the joint creates enough friction to carry turning force (torque), end-thrust and some bending, like a press fit that can be undone with a spanner (Climax). Undo the screws (and on 'self-locking' shallow tapers, screw some into the jacking holes to push the rings apart) and the grip lets go, so the part can be moved, re-timed or removed (SKF).

## Parts

A small assembly that acts as one unit: a split inner ring (grips the shaft), a split outer ring (presses into the hub bore), one or two tapered pressure rings that slide between them, and a circle of high-strength screws (or one large nut on single-nut types); many have tapped 'jacking' holes for removal. The taper-lock bush relative is simpler: one split bush with a tapered outside, two or three grub screws, and usually a key.

## Everyday examples

- The belt pulley on an air-conditioning or ventilation fan or a pump, often held by a taper-lock bush (Industry inMotion)
- Conveyor pulleys and rollers, and the timing cams and turntables in packaging and bottling machines (Machine Design; Climax)
- The collet that holds the bit in a Dremel rotary tool or a router: the same wedge idea, a split tapered sleeve squeezed by a nut onto a smooth shank (Wikipedia 'Collet')
- Taper-lock belt pulleys sold with a bush, as in the Wikimedia Commons photo of a V-belt pulley with its Taperlock bush

## When to use it

Use it to fix a pulley, sprocket, gear, cam or arm to a plain round shaft when you want a strong, play-free grip without cutting keyways or flats, when you need to set the part's exact position or angle (timing) and be able to change it later, when loads reverse or shock (where keys pound loose), or when a keyway would weaken a small shaft. It is also a good way to reuse a shaft that already has a worn keyway (Climax; Machine Design). The taper-lock bush is the everyday industrial choice for belt and chain drives because one pulley size takes bushes for many shaft sizes and comes off easily (SKF; Industry inMotion).

## When not to use it

Avoid it when the hub is plastic, thin-walled or printed (the ring pressure will crack or creep it; bolt the part to a metal hub, or use a clamp hub or glue instead), when you want the joint to slip safely on overload (use a slip clutch, tolerance ring or ball-detent torque limiter), when the shafts are not in line (use a coupling), when cost and space matter more than strength (a set screw on a D-flat shaft, a clamping collar or a split clamp hub is cheaper and smaller), or for hex or splined shafts, which already lock by shape. For a permanent, cheap joint in mass production a press fit or glue may be simpler.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: fixes pulleys, sprockets, gears, cams, rollers and couplings to a plain shaft so they turn as one (Machine Design)
  - line parts up exactly: the part can be set anywhere along the shaft and at any angle (timing) before tightening, and self-centring types also centre it on the shaft (SKF; Climax)
- **Movement:** none: once tightened nothing moves; before tightening the hub can slide along and turn round the shaft for positioning
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 when tightened: the hub is locked to the shaft in every direction, as long as the load stays below the friction limit. While loose: 2 (slide along the shaft and turn round it), which is what allows stepless positioning and timing.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - self-aligning or self-centring: many series pull the hub concentric with the shaft as they tighten, giving very true running (SKF marks each FX series as self-centring or not; RINGFEDER does the same). Non-self-centring types need a separate pilot fit (centring guide) in the hub (SKF)
  - none of the other listed behaviours: it is a fixed joint. It will slip if overloaded, but it is not a torque limiter: SKF says it is 'Not recommended as a principle overload device', and Climax's ratings include no safety factor. Beware the catalogue word 'self-locking': it means a taper so shallow that the rings stay jammed when the screws are loosened and must be jacked apart (SKF FX61 and FX75; German Wikipedia 'Spannsatz'), not the 'won't back-drive' meaning used in this encyclopedia
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): wedge action turns screw force into radial pressure, and friction between the rings, shaft and hub carries the load; German Wikipedia calls it a force-locking shaft-hub connection
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): only in the keyed taper-lock bush relative, where a parallel key in the shaft keyway usually adds a shape lock (RACA Journal: 'a taper lock bush should always be fitted with a key'; Fenner instructions via Industry inMotion treat the key as optional)
- **Input and output:** Not a motion converter: rotation in -> the same rotation out (1:1) between shaft and hub, on one shaft (in line), with no slip and no backlash below the rated torque, so practically no power is lost ⚑ [uncertain: no efficiency figure published]. Back-drivable: yes; torque can pass either way, and it resists reversing torque well ('High resistance to alternating torque', SKF).
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Heavy for its size, and in every direction: an SKF FX10 for a 14 mm shaft carries 180 N·m of torque and 26 kN of end-thrust with eight M6 screws, and the single-nut SKF FX75 for an 8 mm shaft carries 14 N·m (SKF catalogue tables). It also takes bending (rated bending moment 22-35% of rated torque, Climax) and handles shock and reversing loads better than a key (Machine Design). Weak points: it slips if overloaded, oily or under-tightened, and its outward pressure can deform or split a thin or weak hub (Machine Design; Climax).
- **Play and precision:** No play: zero backlash once tightened (SKF; Climax; Machine Design). Self-centring types run very true; non-self-centring types depend on a pilot fit in the hub. Some series push the hub slightly along the shaft while tightening ('axial movement while tightening', SKF), and a taper-lock bush grips the shaft first and then draws the hub onto itself (Industry inMotion), so set the position allowing for this. Misalignment tolerance: none; it is a rigid mounting.
- **Taking it apart:** removable: loosen all screws a little at a time in sequence and the rings release; self-locking tapers are pushed apart by moving screws into tapped jacking holes (SKF). A taper-lock bush is released by moving a grub screw into its removal hole and tightening it, which pushes the bush out of the hub (RACA Journal)
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: SKF says that with correct fitting and removal and no damage 'the locking assembly can be re-used indefinitely'. Taper-lock bushes are broken by prising open the split or hammering them off (RACA Journal)
- **Sealing:** none: it is not a seal. Fenner's advice for taper-lock bushes is to fill the empty screw holes with grease to keep dirt out (Industry inMotion)
- **Adjustable:** yes: before tightening, the hub can be slid to any position along the shaft and turned to any angle ('infinite axial and angular timing', Climax); later you can loosen, reposition and re-tighten. Within limits the rated torque can be lowered by using a lower screw torque (Climax)
- **Access needed:** end of a tube or shaft: the bush slides onto the shaft from its end together with the hub, and all screws are worked from one face, so the back of the hub need not be reached
- **Materials:** Rings of high-carbon or alloy steel with grade 12.9 screws (Climax; SKF); stainless versions exist with lower torque ratings (SKF). Hubs: steel is best; SKF's friction table also covers cast iron and aluminium hubs, and Climax recommends hub steel with a yield strength of at least 45 ksi (310 N/mm²) for shrink discs, with a larger shrink disc for cast-iron hubs. Not suitable for plastic hubs, including 3D-printed ones, except as a separate metal hub that the plastic part is bolted to.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** SKF rates its FX bushings for about -40 to 150 °C with no loss of capacity, and up to 300 °C if all parts are at the same temperature, have similar thermal expansion and the fit tolerances are adjusted; Climax gives 0 to 400 °F (-18 to 204 °C) and warns that parts of different materials expand at different rates. Standard steel rings rust; stainless versions have lower torque ratings (SKF), and Climax offers nickel or thin dense chrome plating. Vibration can loosen screws, so re-check after the first run under load (Fenner via Industry inMotion). Grip depends on clean, correctly lubricated surfaces; oil or contamination makes it slip (Hobby-Machinist; SKF). On a printed hub, creep and heat (PLA's heat deflection temperature is 55 °C, Prusament) make the grip fade over time.

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: keyless bushings, locking assemblies, single-nut bushings, taper-lock bushes, and pulleys or sprockets pre-bored for taper-lock bushes
  - CNC: lathe-boring a metal hub to the H8-type tolerance and smooth finish the bush needs (SKF), or making a metal adaptor hub
  - 3D print (FDM): experimental only; e.g. Atjornov's untested two-part ABS HTD 5M belt pulley made to take a metal 1108 taper-lock bush, bolted together with M4 screws and meant for about 40 W (Printables). Printing the rings themselves is not realistic
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the rings are hardened steel bought parts, and a printed hub is generally too weak to take their pressure directly
- **Tools:** Hex keys and a torque wrench (every screw to the same catalogue torque); a C-spanner or ring spanner for single-nut types; solvent and rags to clean the surfaces. Taper-lock bush: hex key, a hammer with a block or sleeve to tap the bush home, and grease to fill the empty holes (Fenner instructions via Industry inMotion)
- **Difficulty:** moderate: the bush is bought ready-made and fitted with hand tools, but the hub needs an accurately bored, smooth, round hole (typically tolerance H8 and roughness Ra 3.2 µm or better, SKF) in metal thick enough to take the pressure, plus a torque wrench. Without a lathe, buy a pulley or sprocket already bored for the bush

### 3D printing tips

1) Treat the bush as a metal part you buy; print only the parts around it. 2) Don't press a steel locking assembly straight into a printed hub for real loads: an SKF FX10 for a 14 mm shaft pushes on the hub at 97 N/mm² (SKF table), while printed Prusament PLA yields at about 51-59 MPa and its layers pull apart at about 17 MPa (Prusament datasheet), so a printed hub would crack along the layer lines or creep loose. 3) Better: fit the bush in a metal hub or adaptor and bolt the printed pulley, gear or arm to it with several screws and wide washers to spread the load (Atjornov's pulley uses M4 screws with 20 mm washers under heads and nuts) ⚑ [uncertain: general advice, not a tested rule]. 4) If you experiment with a taper-lock bush in a printed pulley, keep the power low (Atjornov's untested ABS design targets about 40 W), use a tough material such as ABS, PETG or nylon rather than brittle PLA (Prusa: PLA 'breaks along layers or into shards upon impact'), make the wall round the bore thick and solid, and through-bolt the hub along its axis so the layers are clamped together ⚑ [uncertain: reasoning]. 5) Remember that a friction grip on plastic loosens as the plastic creeps or softens: Prusament PLA's heat deflection temperature is 55 °C, and Prusa says PLA softens above about 60 °C; re-check tightness after running. 6) For light all-printed designs the same wedge idea works as a printed split collet with a tapered outside squeezed by a nut, like the collet that holds a Dremel bit (Wikipedia 'Collet'), but expect only light torque ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) To fit an odd-sized shaft, Climax recommends a metal adaptor sleeve (a slit sleeve with a wall no thicker than 12.5% of the shaft diameter) rather than a printed shim.

## Rules of thumb

- Tighten the screws in pairs directly across from each other, in stages: first to one-quarter of the specified torque, then to one-half, then to full torque, then check again that none will turn further (Kevin Powers, 'Keyless devices get a grip on shafts', Machine Design, 2000).
- Every screw must reach the same catalogue torque, in a diametrically opposite sequence, or the device won't carry its rated torque (SKF FX catalogue, installation notes 2.1.3-2.1.4).
- Taper angle sets the grip: included cone angles range from about 12° (light to medium duty) down to 5° (heavy duty); the smaller the angle, the more torque it can carry (SKF FX catalogue, section 4.1).
- Friction is the whole trick: catalogue torque ratings assume a friction coefficient of about 0.12 (steel on steel with a thin oil film); dry steel gives about 0.14 and solvent-cleaned about 0.17 (SKF friction table). Never use lubricants containing molybdenum disulphide or silicone (SKF; Machine Design).
- Makers disagree on oiling, so follow the instructions for the exact product: Machine Design and Climax oil the ring surfaces lightly, SKF oils the internal cones but wants the ring-to-shaft and ring-to-hub faces dry, and taper-lock bushes must be completely free of oil and grease (RACA Journal; Fenner via Industry inMotion). Hobby machinists report single-nut bushings slip unless oily surfaces are degreased (Hobby-Machinist forum).
- Machine the shaft and bore to about h8/H8 (h11/H11 for some light series) with roughness Ra 3.2 µm or better (SKF FX catalogue p. 13-14); Climax asks for 63-125 µin RMS.
- The hub must be thick and strong enough not to deform under the outward pressure: Machine Design says consult the maker's hub-size data; Climax gives a formula for the minimum hub outside diameter from the ring's hub pressure and the hub material's yield strength, and SKF gives a table of hub-diameter factors by pressure, hub length and material.
- Published torque ratings include no safety factor, so expect it to slip above the rated torque; apply a service factor for shock (Climax; SKF service factors 1.0-3.0).
- Keyways weaken shafts: with keyed bushings the shaft diameter needs to be about 10% larger (SKF), and SKF's ASME-based shaft formula uses an allowable stress of 41 MPa for keyed but 55 MPa for unkeyed commercial steel.
- Many types can be fitted over an existing empty keyway without derating if the inner ring's slit is placed opposite the keyway and a screw sits over it (Climax).
- Taper-lock bushes have an 8° taper and grub screws in holes drilled half in the bush and half in the hub: the fixing screws thread into the hub and push on the blind half-hole in the bush, driving it deeper; for removal a grub screw moved into the removal hole reverses the force and pushes the bush out. Use only the correct holes, as some small bushes have an extra hole that is there for balance only (RACA Journal).
- Taper-lock fitting: tighten the screws gradually and alternately, tap the large end of the bush with a hammer and block, re-tighten, repeat once or twice, check the screws again after the drive has run under load for a short time, and fill empty holes with grease (Fenner instructions via Industry inMotion).
- Keyed, QD and Taper-Lock bushing systems are not designed for reversing bending loads (e.g. an overhung roll or conveyor pulley); keyless locking assemblies are rated for them (Climax).
- Small sizes exist for makers: SKF FX75 single-nut bushings start at a 3 mm shaft (2.5 N·m) and an 8 mm shaft in a 14 mm hub bore carries 14 N·m (SKF table); RINGFEDER RfN 7061 starts at 6 mm; McMaster-Carr's screw-clamp bushings start at 1/4 in.

## Common failures

1) Slipping: surfaces oily or contaminated (especially with molybdenum disulphide or silicone grease), screws under-torqued or tightened out of sequence, or the load above the rating (SKF; Machine Design; Hobby-Machinist). Fix: clean, lubricate only as the maker says, and torque every screw in stages across the circle. 2) Hub deformed or cracked because the hub wall is too thin or the material too weak for the ring pressure (Machine Design; Climax). Fix: check the maker's minimum hub diameter. 3) Hub wobbles or runs out of true: a non-self-centring type used without a pilot fit, or uneven tightening (SKF). 4) Part ends up in the wrong place: some series move the hub along the shaft while tightening (SKF; Industry inMotion). 5) Screws working loose under vibration if not re-checked after running (Fenner via Industry inMotion). 6) Won't come off: self-locking tapers need jacking screws, and taper-lock bushes need the removal hole; hammering or prising splits the bush, which RACA Journal says explains taper-lock bushes found 'broken in half'. 7) Taper-lock bush not seated because a parallel key with no top clearance holds it off the shaft (RACA Journal).

## Safety

It is part of rotating machinery, so guard it. It is not a safety slip clutch: if overloaded or contaminated it can slip suddenly, dropping or releasing whatever the shaft drives (Climax; SKF). Use the specified grade 12.9 screws and a torque wrench (SKF); over-tightening can strip threads or burst a thin hub, and under-tightening lets it slip. When releasing a heavy pulley or a self-locking taper, support the part, because it can let go suddenly once the rings separate ⚑ [uncertain]. Never prise a taper-lock bush open with a screwdriver in its split or hammer it off; it breaks (RACA Journal).

## Related

- Key and keyway (sunk key): the traditional shape-locked joint; simple and cheap, but it weakens the shaft, needs machining and can pound loose with backlash under reversing loads (SKF; Climax)
- Taper-lock bush and QD bushing: split tapered bushes pulled into a tapered hub bore, usually with a key; quick to fit and remove, but not rated for reversing bending loads (Climax); covered as close relatives in this entry
- Shaft-to-hub joints and rigid couplings (set screw, D-flat, clamp hub): cheaper and smaller for light loads, but lower torque and a set screw marks the shaft
- Shaft collar (clamping collar): a one-screw clamp ring used to stop parts sliding along a shaft rather than to carry torque
- Tolerance ring (wavy-band press fit): a springy steel band that holds by friction and deliberately slips at a set torque; much lower grip but tolerant of loose sizes
- Interference fit (press or shrink fit): the same even, play-free grip but needs a press or heat and very close tolerances, and parts can seldom be reused (SKF)
- Shrink disc: the outside version that clamps a hollow hub down onto a solid shaft (Climax; SKF FX190)
- Collet: a split tapered sleeve squeezed onto a tool shank; the same wedge principle used to hold bits rather than pulleys (Wikipedia)
- Self-locking wedge (taper key): a single wedge driven into a slot that stays put by friction
- Spline shaft and hex shaft: shapes that lock by interlocking rather than friction, allowing sliding along the shaft
