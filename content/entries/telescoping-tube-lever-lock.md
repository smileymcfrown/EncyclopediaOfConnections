A split clamp ring on the end of the outer tube with a lever that snaps shut over centre, squeezing the outer tube onto the inner one so the length is held at any position until you flip the lever open.

**In plain words:** A ring with a slot and a little flip-up lever at the end of the outer tube: flip the lever open and the inner tube slides; snap it shut and the ring squeezes tight so the tubes can't move. **Proper term:** Telescoping tube lever lock (flip lock; cam-lever clamp collar).

**Also called:** flip lock / flip-lock leg; lever lock / external lever lock; flick lock; FlickLock (Black Diamond (brand)); Speed Lock (Leki (brand)); DynaLock (MSR (brand)); snap lever (tripod leg lock); cam lock, cam-lever collar or cam clamp (for tubes); quick-release clamp (for posts and poles); leg lock lever (tripods, saw stands); seatpost quick-release clamp (the same idea on a bicycle seat post).

## How it works

The collar is a ring with a gap in it, so it can be squeezed smaller (the same way a bicycle seat tube has 'a vertical slit' so a ring can squeeze it 'without crumpling', Wikipedia 'Seatpost'). The lever pivots on a pin or screw across the two ears beside the gap. Its end is a cam (an off-centre curve), so closing the lever pulls the ears together and squeezes the collar, and the outer tube, onto the inner tube; friction then holds the length ('close the lever and it grips the tube firmly', Leki). The lever swings just past its tightest point (over-centre, past 'a top dead center pivot point', Easton patent US8006711B2), so the squeeze itself holds it shut. A small screw, nut or dial on the pivot sets how tight it closes, so it can be retightened as parts wear.

## Parts

A few parts acting as one unit: a clamp collar (a ring with a slot or split, fixed to the end of the outer tube), a lever with a cam shape at its pivot end, a pivot pin or screw through the collar's two ears, a tension screw, nut or dial that sets how tight it closes, and the two tubes (the outer tube's end is often slotted too).

## Everyday examples

- Trekking poles with flip-up levers (Black Diamond FlickLock, Leki Speed Lock (brands))
- Camera tripod and light-stand legs with flip locks
- A bicycle seat post held by a quick-release clamp
- Folding work stands and saw-stand legs, and sun-umbrella pole clamps (printed replacement levers for these are on Printables)

## When to use it

Use a lever lock when the length must be changed often and fast, by feel, with gloves or cold hands, and when you want to see at a glance whether it is locked (Ulanzi; Backpacker; Leki). Good for tripod and light-stand legs, trekking and camera poles, adjustable stands and robot or rig frames where you tune a length on the spot, and for fixing a post or pole in a holder at any height. It holds more reliably than a twist lock, and its tension can be set with a screw or dial.

## When not to use it

Avoid it where a lever sticking out would snag or break (packs, crowded mechanisms): a twist lock is slimmer (Ulanzi). Avoid it for exact, repeatable set lengths or for loads that must never slip: use a spring button lock or a pin through holes. Avoid it on thin-walled or carbon tubes unless the clamp force is controlled, because squeezing can crush them. For a setting that is almost never changed, a bolted clamp collar or set screw is simpler. For long-term constant clamping in hot places, a printed plastic collar may creep loose: use metal or check it often.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): the inner tube slides to change length
  - lock, and release by hand: snapping the lever shut locks the length anywhere along the slide; flipping it open frees it
- **Movement:** slide: in and out along the tube while the lever is open, over the length the overlap allows (round tubes can also turn, so rotate+slide when open)
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** Unlocked: 2 for round tubes (slide along and turn around), 1 for square or keyed tubes (slide only). Locked: 0, held by friction.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - over-centre (snaps past a point and stays): the cam lever passes its tightest point and the clamping force keeps it closed (Easton patent US8006711B2: 'a top dead center pivot point' gives 'a positive lock condition')
  - quick-release: one flick opens or closes it, with no tools, and it works with gloves on (Ulanzi; Backpacker)
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): pushing on the pole does not open the lever; only flipping it does
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): the over-centre lever and friction hold without any effort from the user
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the squeezed collar presses the tube walls together and friction holds the length
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Medium. Brand claims for trekking-pole lever locks: Leki Speed Lock Plus holds 'more than 75 kg' and Speed Lock 2 Plus 'more than 90 kg' per lock (Leki UK blog); Speed Lock 2 a minimum of 95 kg (Leki help centre). A vendor explainer gives 'up to 15+ kg' for general telescoping poles (poolpole.com, no test method). The friction resists both sliding and twisting; bending is carried by the overlap between tubes. An overload makes it slip rather than break. Too much clamping can crush a thin or carbon-fibre tube (Manfrotto parts blog: carbon fibre is vulnerable to squeezing forces).
- **Play and precision:** No play when closed, as long as the overlap is long enough (the ultralight printed tripod warns the inner tube must overlap by at least the lock housing length or 'it won't be rigid enough', danec). The length is not indexed, so it only returns to the same length if you use a printed scale or mark. It tolerates small changes in tube size because the tension can be adjusted (Easton patent: adjustable for 'pole members of different cross-section'), but the tubes must be round (or matching shapes) and a close fit.
- **Taking it apart:** tool-free quick release: flip the lever to unlock; the tubes can then slide or be pulled apart
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: it is opened and closed at every adjustment; clamp force drops as parts settle and wear, so the tension screw needs occasional retightening (Leki; Backpacker)
- **Sealing:** none: sand or grit in the lever joint can jam it (Ulanzi: 'a single grain of sand in a lever joint'), and dirt or polishing inside the collar makes it slip (Backpacker)
- **Adjustable:** yes, twice over: the length is stepless (any position along the slide), and the clamping force is set with the tension screw or dial (Leki; Benro; MSR: 'adjust the clamping force on the fly').
- **Access needed:** end of a tube or shaft: the collar fits over the open end of the outer tube; once built it is worked from the outside with one hand.
- **Materials:** Collar: moulded plastic or aluminium alloy (Leki Speed Lock 2 uses 'an enhanced alloy locking collar' for more holding force); lever: plastic or anodised aluminium; cam: stainless steel (MSR); pivot and tension screw: steel. Tubes: aluminium or carbon fibre (carbon needs gentler clamping). Printed versions: PETG or ABS collars and levers with steel bolts (Jori; Bebe, Printables).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Grit or sand in the lever joint can jam it (Ulanzi); dirt, polishing and wear make it slip (Backpacker); clean and dry after wet, sandy or salty use (Benro). Temperature changes make pole materials swell or shrink slightly, which 'can lead to your pole sections slipping or collapsing' unless the tension is readjusted (MSR). Vibration can loosen the tension screw over time: use thread-locker (Backpacker). Printed collars: PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV (Prusa); PETG suits below about 80 °C (Prusa) but still relaxes under constant squeeze (goodprints3d).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: complete lever locks, replacement levers and collars, bicycle seatpost quick-release clamps
  - 3D print (FDM): split collars, levers and replacement lever-lock parts, used with steel pivot bolts
  - CNC: machined aluminium collars and levers
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the collar and lever are printed as separate parts and joined with a pin or bolt; the lever usually needs supports (Jori, Printables 110310).
- **Tools:** None to use. To set the clamping force: a screwdriver, Allen key or nut driver on the tension screw (Benro: a 3 mm Allen key; Manfrotto 055: an 8 mm nut driver; Black Diamond FlickLock: a Phillips screwdriver, per Enwild), or no tool at all on designs with a finger dial (MSR DynaLock).
- **Difficulty:** moderate: a printed split collar with a bought bolt is easy, but a lever that snaps shut over centre with the right force, and plastic that keeps its grip, takes some tuning. Reusing a bicycle seatpost quick-release clamp or buying a replacement lever is the easy route.

### 3D printing tips

1) Use a metal bolt or pin as the pivot and tension screw: Jori's 22 mm post clamp taps M5 threads into the printed ball head and clamp and uses an M5 x 50 screw (Printables 110310); Bebe's clamp uses an M6 furniture connector bolt (Printables 860826); the ultralight tripod uses M3 hardware (danec, Printables 911528). Add a tension nut or screw so you can tune the snap-shut force. 2) Give the slot in the collar a generous rounded end: 'a narrow split with a sharp inside corner creates a crack starter' (goodprints3d). 3) Orient the collar so continuous extrusion paths go round the ring and carry the hoop tension, rather than relying on layer bonds (goodprints3d; Hubs: FDM parts are weaker across layers). 4) Spread bolt loads with wide ears and washers or metal sleeves, because a screw bearing on a small printed ledge sinks in and makes the clamp go loose (goodprints3d). 5) Use tough material and enough walls: PETG for levers (Bogen and Harbor Freight replacement levers, Printables), ABS (Bebe), and more shells or infill for leg locks 'since it needs to be somewhat strong' (Pete Prodoehl, Printables 40021); default slicer settings in PLA gave 'a very flexible lever' (gjpc, Printables 34970). 6) Plan for creep: plastic under a constant squeeze relaxes, so a PETG clamp can 'lose compression over hours or days' (goodprints3d); nylon recovers better; PLA softens above 60 °C (Prusa) and PETG suits use below 80 °C (Prusa). 7) Leave at least 0.3 mm clearance on moving parts such as the lever pivot (Prusa), and drill critical holes to size because FDM prints vertical holes undersize (Hubs). 8) Keep plenty of overlap: at least the length of the lock housing (danec). 9) For a printed collar clamping a separate printed or plastic outer tube, slot the outer tube's end too so it can close (Wikipedia 'Seatpost' describes the slit) ⚑ [uncertain].

## Rules of thumb

- Close the lever past its tightest point (top dead centre) so it locks positively and stays shut (Easton patent US8006711B2).
- A cam acting on both ears of the split ring clamps better than one acting on one side only, which 'occasionally proves to be inadequate for intensive use' (Rossignol patent US6676329B2).
- Adjust clamping force in small steps and test after each: Black Diamond suggests a quarter to half turn at a time (Enwild); for a Manfrotto 055 leg, 'never more than a third of a turn' (ManfrottoTripodParts); Leki: 'turn the tension screw a little tighter, then test again; if it will not close, loosen it slightly'.
- A correctly set lever needs 'a bit of force to close' (Enwild, on Black Diamond FlickLocks); if it suddenly gets hard to close, loosen it rather than forcing it (Leki).
- Benchmarks for a good trekking-pole lever lock: more than 75 kg (Speed Lock Plus) to more than 90 kg (Speed Lock 2 Plus) holding force per lock (Leki UK blog, brand claims).
- Mark and respect a minimum insertion (maximum extension) depth, as bicycle seatposts do (Wikipedia 'Seatpost'); FRC robotics teams reserve about one quarter to one fifth of each stage's length for overlap (Chief Delphi).
- Don't overtighten: it can bend or break the clamp or strip its threads (Wikipedia 'Seatpost'), and on carbon tubes 'wear out and damage the snap lever and maybe even crack the leg lock' (ManfrottoTripodParts).
- If a lever lock slips, tighten the adjustment screw (thread-lock it if it keeps loosening), then lightly sand the inside of the collar and the outside of the inner tube; don't oil it (Backpacker).

## Common failures

1) Slips because the tension has loosened or the tube is polished or gunky: tighten the tension screw a quarter turn at a time and test, thread-lock it, and lightly sand the collar and inner tube (Backpacker; Enwild; Leki). 2) Lever won't close or is very stiff: tension too high; back it off (Leki). 3) Broken or lost lever, often from knocks in transport: replace it, or print one (gjpc's tripod lever, Bogen and Harbor Freight replacement levers, Printables). 4) Jammed by sand in the lever joint (Ulanzi): rinse and clean. 5) Cracked collar or crushed tube from overtightening, especially on carbon fibre (ManfrottoTripodParts). 6) Snagging the lever on gear. 7) Printed collars creeping loose over hours or days, or cracking at a sharp slot corner (goodprints3d): use a rounded slot end, washers and a tougher material.

## Safety

Pinch points: fingers can be caught between the lever and collar as it snaps shut, and between the sliding tubes ⚑ [uncertain]. A lock set too loose can let a tripod or pole collapse under load: check it holds before mounting a camera or leaning on it (Benro: ensure leg locks are tightly engaged before mounting gear). Overtightening can crack the collar or crush a carbon tube (ManfrottoTripodParts).

## Related

- Telescoping tube twist lock (cam or collet twist lock): locks by twisting; slimmer and cheaper, but slower, harder with gloves and more prone to slipping or jamming
- Spring button lock (snap button): locks positively at set lengths through holes; no slipping, but no in-between lengths
- Eccentric cam lever (cam clamp, quick-release skewer): the same over-centre cam lever, used to clamp wheels and plates rather than tubes
- Toggle clamp: another over-centre clamp, using a linkage instead of a cam
- Clamp-on tube fittings (slip-on structural fittings): tube connectors that also clamp by squeezing, usually with a screw
- Shaft-to-hub joints (clamp hub): a split hub squeezed by a screw, the bolted version of the same squeeze
- Quick-release plate (dovetail clamp): a lever or knob clamp for quick camera mounting
- Indexable clamping lever (ratchet handle): a lever whose handle can be repositioned, used on screw clamps
