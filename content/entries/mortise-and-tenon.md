A tongue (tenon) cut on the end of one part is pushed into a matching hole (mortise) in the other part and locked in place, giving a strong, square joint for frames, chairs, tables and timber buildings.

**In plain words:** A tongue on the end of one piece that fits snugly into a matching hole in the other piece, then is locked in with glue, a wooden peg, wedges or a removable wedge. **Proper term:** Mortise and tenon joint (including wedged, drawbored and tusk tenons).

**Also called:** mortice and tenon (British spelling; Wikipedia 'Mortise and tenon'); M&T (woodworkers' shorthand); through tenon, stub tenon, blind tenon, haunched tenon (Wikipedia); pegged tenon, pinned tenon, treenail (trenail, trunnel) joint (Wikipedia); drawbored mortise and tenon, drawbore, draw-bore (Popular Woodworking 'Drawboring Resurrected'); wedged tenon, wedged through tenon, fox-wedged (foxtail wedged) tenon (Popular Woodworking; Wikimedia Commons 'MortiseFoxTail.svg'); wedged half-dovetail mortise (Wikipedia); tusk tenon, tusked tenon, keyed tenon, keyed through tenon, loose-wedge mortise and tenon (Woodsmith; Wood and Shop, Will Myers); loose tenon, floating tenon, slip tenon; Domino (brand) for Festool's loose-tenon joiner (Wikipedia 'Domino joiner'); hammer joint (a laser-cut fox-wedge tenon with a built-in wedge; Lasersheets).

## How it works

The tenon fits closely inside the mortise, so the joint can't slide sideways or twist, and the shoulders around the tenon bear on the face of the other part to keep the joint square and stiff against bending (Wikipedia 'Mortise and tenon'). On its own the tenon could pull straight out, so it is locked: glue bonds the tenon cheeks to the mortise walls; a drawbored peg goes through holes that are slightly offset, so driving the peg bends it and pulls the shoulders tight (Popular Woodworking 'Drawboring Resurrected'); wedges driven into saw cuts in a through tenon spread its end into a fan or dovetail shape inside a flared mortise so it can't pull back out (Popular Woodworking 'Wedged Mortise and Tenon'); and in a tusk tenon a tapered wedge driven through a slot in the protruding tenon bears on the outside face and pulls the shoulders tight, yet can be tapped out to take the furniture apart (Woodsmith).

## Parts

A few parts: the tenon (a tongue with shoulders, cut on the end of a rail), the mortise (a hole or slot in the other part, either through or blind), and a lock: glue; a wooden peg (pin) through both; one or two wedges driven into saw cuts in the tenon end; or, for a tusk tenon, a separate tapered wedge (tusk or key) through a slot in the protruding tenon (Wikipedia 'Mortise and tenon'; Popular Woodworking; Woodsmith).

## Everyday examples

- Chair and stool legs joined to their rails, the kind of joint that 'gets stressed a lot' (Matthias Wandel, woodgears.ca)
- Timber-framed barns and houses held together with wooden pegs through pegged tenons (Wikipedia 'Mortise and tenon')
- Trestle tables and workbenches that come apart by knocking out tusk-tenon wedges (Woodsmith)
- Stools and chairs whose round leg tenons go right through the seat and are split and wedged from above, as in Windsor chairs (Woodcraft; Popular Woodworking 'The Indispensable Mortise & Tenon')

## When to use it

Use it when a rail meets a post or stile at an angle, usually 90 degrees, and the joint must be strong and stiff for years: chairs, tables, frames, doors and timber buildings (Wikipedia; woodgears.ca). Choose a drawbored tenon when you can't clamp the work or want a joint that stays tight even if the glue fails (Popular Woodworking). Choose a wedged through tenon for a strong, decorative joint that can't pull out, such as chair seats and legs (Popular Woodworking; Woodcraft). Choose a tusk tenon when large furniture like a trestle table, workbench or bed must be taken apart for moving (Woodsmith). A laser-cut hammer joint gives fast, glue-free joints in plywood (Lasersheets).

## When not to use it

For quick or light jobs, dowels, biscuits or pocket screws are faster, though weaker (woodgears.ca). For flat-pack furniture that is often assembled, knock-down cam-lock or cross-dowel fittings are quicker than wedges. For joining boards edge to edge or at corners of boxes, use edge glue, finger or dovetail joints instead. For very soft woods like balsa or poplar, a wedged laser joint may break (Lasersheets). For 3D prints carrying heavy load across the [layer lines](../glossary.md#layer-lines), a bolted joint with [heat-set inserts](../glossary.md#heat-set-insert) is more reliable ⚑ [uncertain].

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: joins rails to legs and stiles in chairs, tables, doors and timber frames (Wikipedia)
  - line parts up exactly: the shoulders seat against the other part and keep frames square (Wikipedia)
  - lock, and release by hand: a tusk tenon's wedge is tapped out with a mallet to take a trestle table or workbench apart, then driven back to lock it (Woodsmith)
  - tighten and hold tension: a drawbored peg or a tusk wedge pulls the shoulders tight and keeps the tenon in tension as the wood shrinks (Popular Woodworking; Woodsmith)
- **Movement:** none: it is a rigid joint once locked
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 once glued, pegged or wedged. Before locking, the tenon can only slide straight in and out of the mortise (1 degree of freedom); the close fit and shoulders block every other movement.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): the offset holes of a drawbore make the peg pull the joint tight and keep it tight during [shrinkage](../glossary.md#warping--shrinkage); a tusk wedge pulls the shoulders against the post (Popular Woodworking 'Drawboring Resurrected'; Woodsmith)
  - [self-locking](../glossary.md#self-locking) (holds under load, won't back-drive): a shallow wedge (about 5 degrees on a tusk tenon) stays put under load; Woodsmith notes a vertical wedge is less likely to work loose. That this is friction self-locking is inferred, not stated by the source ⚑ [uncertain]
- **How it holds:**
  - shape (the parts interlock; [form-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): a flared wedged tenon or a peg or tusk wedge through the tenon mechanically stops it pulling out (Popular Woodworking; Woodsmith)
  - material bond (glue, solvent or melting): most furniture tenons are glued (Wikipedia; woodgears.ca tests)
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): a tight friction fit, and wedges or drawbore pegs that press the parts together (Wikipedia: 'glued or friction-fitted'; Popular Woodworking)
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Heavy for its size: the strongest of the common furniture joints in bending. In Matthias Wandel's tests (spruce rail on a post, load 20 cm from the joint) glued mortise and tenons broke at an average of 172 lb (about 780 N), about 25% more than a three-dowel joint (135 lb), and in maple at 270-300 lb; screwed joints bent open long before failing (woodgears.ca 'Wood joint strength testing'). In a later test mortise and tenons were about twice as strong as pocket-hole joints (woodgears.ca). Strong in [shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear) and bending; pull-out strength comes from the glue, peg or wedges, and a flared wedged through tenon is described as mechanically the strongest version because it spreads into a dovetail shape (Rockler / Center for Furniture Craftsmanship 'How to Make [Kerfs](../glossary.md#kerf) and Wedges for Through Tenons'). Weak points: short grain beyond a peg or wedge slot in the tenon, which can split out (Woodsmith), and a post split along its grain if wedges are driven the wrong way (Woodcraft).
- **Play and precision:** none once locked: the shoulders fix the angle and the lock removes play. Drawboring tolerates a loose-fitting tenon because the peg pulls it tight; only the shoulder fit needs care (Popular Woodworking 'Drawboring Resurrected'). In glued joints, the fit 'to the last few thousandth of an inch' made no detectable difference to strength as long as glue covered everything (woodgears.ca). 3D-printed versions need a designed gap, e.g. 0.5 mm for FDM (Hubs).
- **Taking it apart:** permanent for glued, wedged-through and glued drawbored tenons; removable for tusk (loose-wedge) tenons, where you tap out the wedge and pull the joint apart (Woodsmith), and for unglued pegged tenons whose peg can be driven out ⚑ [uncertain]
- **How often it can be reused:** once for glued and wedged tenons; many times for tusk tenons: Will Myers' Moravian workbench has been 'assembled and taken apart scores of times' over four years (Wood and Shop)
- **Sealing:** none: not designed to seal (ancient ship planks used pegged tenons, but the seal came from the planking fit, not the joint itself) ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Adjustable:** no for glued joints; a tusk tenon's wedge can be driven further to re-tighten a loose joint, though over-driving has split many tenons (Will Myers, Wood and Shop)
- **Access needed:** both sides for through, wedged and tusk tenons (the tenon end and wedges show on the far side and are driven from there); one side only (blind) for glued stub or loose tenons, which are assembled by pushing the parts together (Wikipedia; Woodsmith)
- **Materials:** Mainly solid wood, hardwoods and softwoods; pegs traditionally from dry, straight-grained white oak (Popular Woodworking 'Drawboring Resurrected'); wedges from straight-grained wood, often a contrasting species (Popular Woodworking 'Wedged Mortise and Tenon'). Also stone (Stonehenge's sarsens) and metal by blacksmiths (Wikipedia). Sheet versions in birch plywood and MDF of 6 mm or more; very soft woods like poplar or balsa may break, and bamboo splits along its grain (Lasersheets 'Hammer Joint'). 3D-printed in PLA (Printables 'Tenon Wedge Hammer Joint Test') or other plastics; Hubs prefers ABS to PLA for FDM interlocking joints because it is more [ductile](../glossary.md#brittle--ductile).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Wood shrinks and swells with humidity, mostly across the grain and only 0.1-0.2% along it (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 4), so joints can open a gap at the shoulder as wood dries; a drawbore peg keeps the tenon pulled tight during almost any shrinkage, but wet pegs shrink and loosen (Popular Woodworking 'Drawboring Resurrected'). Tusk wedges work loose over time and need re-driving (Will Myers, Wood and Shop). Outdoor joints need a waterproof glue or a mechanical lock such as pegs or wedges ⚑ [uncertain]. 3D-printed PLA tenons soften at about 60 °C and PETG at about 80 °C (CNC Kitchen).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - woodwork: the main method, by hand or with a mortiser, router or loose-tenon joiner (Wikipedia; Popular Woodworking)
  - laser cut: tab-like through tenons in sheet, e.g. the one-piece 'hammer joint' with a built-in wedge, for 3-12 mm sheet (Lasersheets; Hackaday)
  - CNC: routed mortises have rounded ends, so either round the tenon or square the mortise ends with a chisel (Popular Woodworking 'Wedged Mortise and Tenon' squares routed mortises with a chisel)
  - 3D print (FDM): printed tenon and wedge test joints and teaching models (Printables); round tenons suffer from layer stepping on FDM (Formlabs)
  - 3D print (resin): possible with the resin [clearance](../glossary.md#clearance) guidance (Formlabs), ⚑ [uncertain] for load-bearing use
  - off-the-shelf part: ready-made loose tenons (e.g. Domino (brand) tenons), dowels as pegs, drawbore pins
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the tenon, the mortised part and any wedge or peg are printed separately and assembled
- **Tools:** Hand tools: mortise gauge, chisels (including a mortise chisel), backsaw, brace and bit or drill, mallet (Popular Woodworking 'The Indispensable Mortise & Tenon'); for drawboring, drawbore pins (tapered steel pins, which can be made from a machinist's alignment or drift pin) and riven oak pegs (Popular Woodworking 'Drawboring Resurrected'). Machine methods: hollow-chisel mortiser, plunge router with a template, slot mortiser, or a loose-tenon joiner such as the Domino (brand) (Wikipedia 'Domino joiner'; Popular Woodworking 'Wedged Mortise and Tenon'). Laser cutter or CNC router for sheet versions (Lasersheets). Glue and clamps for glued joints.
- **Difficulty:** moderate: hand-cut joints need accurate marking and chiselling (Wikipedia calls the joint difficult to make because of the precise measuring and cutting), but drawboring and wedging forgive a less-than-perfect tenon fit (Popular Woodworking), and machines, loose-tenon joiners, laser-cut hammer joints and 3D-printed versions make it easy.

### 3D printing tips

1) Clearance: leave a gap around the tenon: Hubs suggests 0.5 mm for FDM interlocking joints (0.2 mm SLA and SLS); Prusa printers are accurate to about 0.2 mm and moving fits start at 0.3 mm; Formlabs suggests 0.2 mm for small and up to 0.4 mm for large resin or SLS assemblies. 2) Orientation: FDM parts are weakest between layers (PLA layer adhesion was only about 55% of flat strength in CNC Kitchen's test; Prusa: 'like wood, FFF prints are stronger in one direction'), so print the tenon lying down so its layers run along its length; a tenon printed standing upright tends to snap off at a layer line at the shoulder. 3) Prefer rectangular tenons: round tenons print with visible steps on FDM and need post-processing (Formlabs). 4) Round the edges and [fillet](../glossary.md#chamfer--fillet) the inside corner at the shoulder to ease assembly and reduce [stress concentration](../glossary.md#stress-concentration) (Hubs). 5) Wedged versions: add a slot in the tenon for a wedge, as in the laser 'hammer joint' copied in PLA (Printables 'Tenon Wedge Hammer Joint Test': tap the wedge flush with a soft hammer; 2 [perimeters](../glossary.md#perimeters), 15% gyroid [infill](../glossary.md#infill)); PLA is brittle, so a tougher plastic such as PETG may survive wedging better ⚑ [uncertain]. 6) For wood-to-print joints, remember wood swells with humidity while plastic doesn't, so leave room or use a printed wedge that can be re-driven ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) Printed PLA softens at about 60 °C (CNC Kitchen), so keep load-bearing printed tenons out of hot places.

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Loose (floating) tenons, e.g. Domino (brand) tenons with an oval cross-section, from 4 x 20 mm up to 14 x 140 mm, for Festool's loose-tenon joiner (Wikipedia 'Domino joiner'); wooden dowels or oak pegs for pinned tenons; drawbore pins (sold by antique tool dealers or made from a machinist's 'drift pin', 'alignment pin' or 'line-up tool'; Popular Woodworking); mortise chisels and hollow-chisel mortiser bits; 'wedge' blanks cut from straight-grained hardwood.

## Rules of thumb

- Tenon thickness: about one-third of the rail thickness; a haunch is one-third of the tenon length long and one-sixth of the tenon width deep (Wikipedia 'Mortise and tenon').
- Drawbore offset: 1/16 in. almost always works; 3/32 in. is better for furniture; up to almost 1/8 in. for big joints in tough woods like ash or elm; bigger offsets risk breaking the tenon or peg, especially in fragile woods like cherry (Christopher Schwarz, Popular Woodworking 'Drawboring Resurrected').
- Drawbore peg: 1/4 in. hole for cabinet work, 3/8 in. for workbenches, doors and windows; place the hole 3/8 in. from the mortise opening in furniture (1/2 in. in larger work); whittle the last 1/2 in. of the peg to a 1/8 in. tip; use dry, straight-grained white oak, as wet pegs shrink and let the joint loosen (Popular Woodworking 'Drawboring Resurrected').
- Wedged through tenon: a 3-degree [taper](../glossary.md#taper) on the mortise ends and wedges works well (can go up to 8 degrees; wider tapers are stronger); taper about three-quarters of the mortise depth; 1/4 in. strain-relief holes centred 1/4 in. from the tenon edge leave 1/8 in. strips that bend without splitting; wedges about 1 in. longer than the tenon (Popular Woodworking 'Wedged Mortise and Tenon').
- Wedge direction: orient the wedge slot perpendicular to the grain of the piece with the mortise, never parallel, or it will split; drill a 3/16 in. hole where the slot ends, 1/8-1/4 in. from the shoulder, and make round through-tenons 1/8-1/4 in. longer than the seat is thick (Ken Burton, Woodcraft 'Avoiding Splits with Wedged Tenons').
- Tusk tenon: bevel the outer faces of the wedge and wedge mortise at about 5 degrees; let the inside face of the wedge mortise extend slightly into the main mortise so the wedge pulls the shoulders tight; leave at least 1/2 in. of solid wood beyond the wedge mortise; a dovetailed-and-wedged tenon needs a steeper 10-12 degree wedge; forked-wedge notches at least 1/8 in. deep (Woodsmith). A properly designed tusk tenon leaves a slight gap just behind the wedge (Woodworker's Journal 'Tusk Tenons').
- Tusk tenon example that has lasted: wedge 3/8 in. thick, tapered about 1 in. in 12, with about 4 in. of wood beyond the wedge, in a workbench taken apart scores of times (Will Myers, Wood and Shop).
- Strength: glued mortise and tenons averaged 172 lb (spruce) and 270-300 lb (maple) with the load 20 cm from the joint, about 25% stronger than three dowels; orient tenons to cross as many growth rings as possible, since a tenon with rings parallel to it split the post early (Matthias Wandel, woodgears.ca).
- Wedged joints have no universal angles or lengths; test on templates with your own wood (Fine Woodworking 'Designing the Wedged Mortise and Tenon').

## Common failures

Glue failure over time, which lets an ordinary tenon loosen; drawboring or wedging prevents this (Popular Woodworking). Splitting the mortised piece by driving wedges parallel to its grain; turn the slot 90 degrees (Woodcraft). Tenon end blowing out behind a tusk wedge when there is too little wood beyond the wedge or the wedge is over-driven; leave at least 1/2 in. (Woodsmith; Wood and Shop). Tenon or peg breaking during drawboring when the offset is too big, especially in cherry (Popular Woodworking). Post shearing along its growth rings when the tenon runs parallel to them (woodgears.ca). Wedges that bottom out before they spread the tenon; test-fit and size them (Popular Woodworking). In laser-cut sheet, a wider kerf at the bottom of thick sheet can let the wedge shift sideways (Lasersheets). In 3D prints, tenons snap at a layer line if printed upright (see making tips).

## Safety

Sharp chisels and mallet work: keep hands behind the cutting edge ⚑ [uncertain]. Cutting small wedges on a table saw needs the blade guard removed, so take great care (Popular Woodworking 'Wedged Mortise and Tenon'). Driving wedges or pegs can split parts suddenly; use a wooden mallet, which is less likely to split wedges than a metal hammer (Woodcraft). Otherwise none known.

## Related

- Bridle joint (open mortise): the mortise is an open-ended slot, easier to cut but less enclosed (Wikipedia)
- Dowel and biscuit joints: round pins or oval wafers in holes, quicker but weaker than a tenon (woodgears.ca)
- Loose (floating) tenon, e.g. Domino (brand): a separate tenon glued into mortises in both parts (Wikipedia 'Domino joiner')
- Tab-and-slot joint: the sheet-material version of a through tenon, for laser and CNC cutting
- Japanese interlocking joints: complex tenons and splices locked by shape, wedges and pegs
- Knock-down cam-lock and cross-dowel connectors: hardware alternatives to the tusk tenon for furniture that comes apart
- Wedge dowel (click-in grooved dowel): a modern tool-free knock-down alternative
- Sliding dovetail and dovetail joint: other shape-locked wood joints that resist pulling apart
- Clevis joint (fork and pin): a tongue in a fork held by a pin, like a pegged open mortise but made to pivot
