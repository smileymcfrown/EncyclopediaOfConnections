A rope splice opens up the end of a rope and weaves its strands over and under the strands of the main rope, or, in hollow braided rope, pulls the end up inside the rope, to make a permanent loop, a join between two ropes or a neat end that keeps most or all of the rope's strength, where a knot typically loses 20-40% (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing').

**In plain words:** Weaving a rope's own strands back into itself, or sliding its end up inside its hollow middle, to make a permanent loop in the end, join two ropes end to end, or stop the end fraying, all without tying a knot. **Proper term:** Rope splice (splicing): tuck splices in laid (three-strand) rope, i.e. the eye splice, short splice, long splice, back splice and chain or ring splice; and bury splices in braided rope, i.e. the long-bury (finger-trap) splice and the locked Brummel splice.

**Also called:** eye splice, sailmaker's eye splice: a permanent loop in a rope end (ABOK #2725 per Animated Knots; Wikipedia's infobox gives ABOK #17, #2745, #2754) (Wikipedia 'Eye splice'; Animated Knots 'Eye Splice'); short splice: joins two rope ends, about twice as thick as the rope (ABOK #2634) (Animated Knots 'Short Splice'; Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'); long splice: joins two rope ends with almost no bulge, so the rope still runs through pulleys (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'; Samson 'Long Splice'); back splice, end splice, crown-and-back splice: tucks the strands back into the end to stop fraying (ABOK #2813) (Animated Knots 'Back Splice'; Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'); ring splice: attaches a rope end to a ring or sail clew (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing', citing Merry, The Splicing Handbook, p. 27); chain splice, rope-to-chain splice: a small Flemish eye (ABOK #2751) with extra tucks, joining rope to anchor chain (Animated Knots 'Chain Splice'; Samson); Flemish eye; cut splice; horseshoe splice (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing', 'Eye splice'); bury splice, long-bury splice, unlocked Brummel, finger-trap splice: the end pushed up inside hollow braided rope (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice'); locked Brummel splice, Brummel eye splice; McDonald Brummel (Animated Knots 'Brummel Eye Splice', 'Splicing Knots'); tuck-bury splice, end-for-end splice, double-braid (braid-on-braid) eye splice: rope makers' names for braided-rope splices (Samson 'How to splice rope'; Milne and McLaren 2006); soft shackle, rope shackle: a spliced loop of strong fibre rope closed by a stopper knot (Animated Knots 'Soft Shackle'; Wikipedia 'Shackle'); whoopie sling: an adjustable spliced sling, e.g. for hammock straps (Animated Knots 'Whoopie Sling'); rope grommet: an endless rope ring made by re-laying a strand (Wikipedia 'Grommet').

## How it works

In three-strand (laid) rope you untwist the end, bend it back to form the loop or lay it against the other rope, and tuck each loose strand over one strand and under the next of the main rope (standing part), against the twist, several times round; friction between the interwoven strands holds them and hands the load over gradually from the tail to the standing part (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'; Animated Knots 'Eye Splice'; Samson 3-Strand Eye Splice). In hollow single-braid rope (for example HMPE such as Dyneema, brand) the tapered end is pushed up the hollow centre; when the rope is pulled its braid gets longer and narrower like a finger trap and grips the buried tail along its whole length, and a Brummel lock (passing the rope through itself) or a line of stitches stops the tail sliding out when the rope is slack (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice', 'Brummel Eye Splice'; Wikipedia 'Eye splice'). Because the load is passed on along the length of the splice instead of through sharp bends as in a knot, a well-made splice breaks at the end of the splice at close to the rope's own strength, provided there are enough tucks or a long enough bury (Milne and McLaren 2006; Animated Knots 'Splicing Knots').

## Parts

Single piece: only the rope itself. Often finished or fitted with a few small extras: tape or whipping twine to hold the strands, stitching twine to lock a braided splice, and a thimble (a grooved metal or plastic liner) inside the eye. Tools: a fid or marlinspike, a knife and a lighter or hot knife.

## Everyday examples

- A loop spliced into the end of a boat's mooring or dock line, often round a metal or plastic thimble (Wikipedia 'Eye splice'; Samson)
- Hammock straps with adjustable whoopie slings (Animated Knots 'Whoopie Sling')
- A rope spliced straight onto a snap shackle or an anchor chain (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'; Animated Knots 'Chain Splice')
- Church bell ropes, which are traditionally spliced (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing', citing Beech, Splicing Bell Ropes Illustrated)

## When to use it

Use a splice when a loop or join must stay put for a long time, keep nearly all of the rope's strength, and be slim and neat: a permanent eye on a mooring line, tow rope or anchor rode (with a thimble if it fixes to a shackle or chain), a rope spliced to a chain or ring, joining two ropes (short splice) or a join that must run through a pulley (long splice) (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'; Animated Knots 'Short Splice', 'Chain Splice'; Samson). In slippery modern rope such as Dyneema, where ordinary knots slip and kink the fibres down to 'a mere 30%' of strength, a bury or Brummel splice is the right termination (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice', 'Splicing Knots'). Makers use them to hang printed pulleys and blocks from soft shackles, as lightweight soft shackles instead of metal ones, and as adjustable whoopie slings (Animated Knots 'Soft Shackle', 'Whoopie Sling'; Printables 552267). A splice is permanent and, unlike a bowline, has no 'tendency to shake undone when not loaded' (Wikipedia 'Eye splice').

## When not to use it

Never use a home-made splice for climbing, arborist work, rescue, lifting people, a swing or hammock that holds a person, or a load over people; buy rated, tested slings and terminations instead (Animated Knots 'Safety'; OSHA 1910.184). Don't splice when you need to undo and re-tie often: use a knot such as a bowline or figure-eight loop (Fixed loop knots) or a bend (Bends for joining two cords) (Animated Knots 'Splicing Knots'). A short splice is 'useless for any running rigging because the splice will be too fat to pass through any blocks' (Animated Knots 'Short Splice'). A back splice is bulky; use whipping or heat-sealing to stop fraying (Rope-end finishing) (Animated Knots 'Back Splice'). For steel wire rope, use a thimble with clips or a crimped sleeve (Wire-rope terminations). For a quick clamp-on grip on a cable, use a woven mesh grip (Woven self-tightening grip).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: makes a permanent loop (eye) in a rope end, attaches a rope to a ring, thimble, shackle or chain, or joins two ropes end to end (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'; Animated Knots 'Splicing Knots')
  - tighten and hold tension: only the adjustable spliced versions, such as the whoopie sling used 'to tension a hammock' (Animated Knots 'Whoopie Sling')
  - lock, and release by hand: only the soft shackle, a spliced rope loop closed by a knot that is opened and closed by hand like a metal shackle (Animated Knots 'Soft Shackle'; Wikipedia 'Shackle')
- **Movement:** none: the splice is a fixed joint; the rope on either side stays flexible. A whoopie sling's adjustable section slides along the rope until it is loaded (Animated Knots 'Whoopie Sling').
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 at the splice itself (the rope either side bends freely); 1 for the sliding part of an adjustable whoopie sling while it is unloaded.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - [self-locking](../glossary.md#self-locking) (holds under load, won't back-drive): a bury splice in hollow braid grips harder the harder it is pulled, because the outer braid narrows onto the buried tail, finger-trap style; 'the strain is progressively transferred from the standing end to the two lines of the eye' (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice'; see Woven self-tightening grip)
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): the whoopie sling's adjustable loop is pulled to length and holds once loaded (Animated Knots 'Whoopie Sling')
  - quick-release: only the soft shackle, whose knot is pushed back out through its loop when it is unloaded (Animated Knots 'Soft Shackle'; Wikipedia 'Shackle')
  - none: plain eye, short, long and back splices simply hold
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping ([force-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): the interwoven strands squeeze each other, and rope tension tightens the tucks; too few tucks and the splice 'can simply unravel' under load (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'; Milne and McLaren 2006)
  - jamming (grains or a braid lock up under load): in hollow braid the outer braid narrows and grips the buried tail along its length (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice')
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): a Brummel lock, the tucked strands of a chain splice round a chain link, and the soft shackle's knot trapped in its loop hold by shape when the rope is slack (Animated Knots 'Brummel Eye Splice', 'Chain Splice', 'Soft Shackle')
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Medium to heavy, in a straight pull only; weak where the eye bends over a sharp edge or a thin pin. Strength figures differ, so side by side: 'a knot typically reduces the strength by 20-40%' while 'a splice is capable of attaining a rope's full strength' (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'); one study found 'Splices average 25-40% of rope strength decay', while 'Literature and reference sources typically attribute only a 5% strength decay' (Wikipedia 'Eye splice', citing Diamond 2007); Samson says splicing 'in most cases ... maintains 100% of the specified rope strength', and its 12-strand Class II eye splice keeps '90% to 100% of average new rope strength' (Samson); Animated Knots expects '90 - 100%' for a long-bury splice in hollow braid. In tests on polyester rope, three-strand eye splices with 3, 4 or 8 tucks reached about full rope strength, 2-tuck splices slipped at 73%, and 4-tuck splices in 6 mm and 12 mm rope reached only 63% and 83%; braid-on-braid splices with a long enough overlap reached about 100%, but the shortest overlap slipped at 15-24% (Milne and McLaren, Sports Engineering 2006). For comparison, knots in the same tests kept 62-66% (bowline) and 76-84% (figure-eight loop). A soft shackle, with several rope parts sharing the load, typically breaks at about 175% of the line's strength (Animated Knots, citing Evans Starzinger; see Shackle and quick link for other tests). Never for holding people.
- **Play and precision:** not applicable. Note for matched pairs: a long bury shortens and thickens the rope by an amount there is 'no reliable formula for predicting', so make and load a test splice, measure it, then make the second one the same (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice').
- **Taking it apart:** semi-permanent: a splice 'is intended to be permanent' and 'Undoing a splice and re-making it takes much more time than doing the same with most knots' (Animated Knots 'Splicing Knots'); if removed it 'leaves a distortion of the rope' (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'). A worn chain splice is cut off and remade a few inches further up the rope (Animated Knots 'Chain Splice'). Exceptions: the soft shackle opens by hand (tool-free quick release) and the whoopie sling adjusts by hand.
- **How often it can be reused:** once: a splice is made to stay, and is cut off and remade when worn rather than undone (Animated Knots 'Chain Splice', 'Splicing Knots'). The finished soft shackle or whoopie sling is opened, closed or adjusted many times in normal use.
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** no for ordinary splices: the eye size and rope length are set when the splice is made. Yes for a whoopie sling or Grog's sliding splice, whose loop slides along the rope to change length and then holds (Animated Knots 'Whoopie Sling', 'Splicing Knots').
- **Access needed:** not applicable: it is made on a free end of the rope, usually before the rope is fitted; the locked Brummel is 'much easier to tie when both ends are available' (Animated Knots 'Brummel Eye Splice').
- **Materials:** Three-strand (laid) rope in natural fibre (manila, hemp, cotton) or synthetic fibre (nylon, polyester, polypropylene and other olefins): tuck splices such as the eye, short, long, back and chain splices (Wikipedia 'Eye splice'; Samson Class I instructions). Eight-strand plaited rope: tuck splices with the strands handled in pairs (Wikipedia 'Eye splice'). Hollow single braid, usually 12-strand, in HMPE (Dyneema, Spectra, Amsteel, brands), LCP (Vectran, brand) or aramid (Kevlar, Technora, brands): bury and Brummel splices, soft shackles and whoopie slings (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice', 'Whoopie Sling'; Samson Class II). Double braid (a braided cover over a braided core): rope makers' splices measured in fid lengths, and for high-performance cores only the core carries the load (Wikipedia 'Eye splice'; Samson). Modern high-modulus fibres 'are often slippery and cannot be secured with customarily trustworthy knots and splices', which is why they need long buries (Animated Knots 'Splicing Knots'). Sheathed (kernmantle) climbing rope and paracord-style cord are not normally spliced at home ⚑ [uncertain]. Thimbles are galvanised or stainless steel or nylon (Printables 934812).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Water: nylon 'absorbs water and is 10-15% weaker when wet' (Wikipedia 'Rope'). Sunlight and heat: polypropylene has 'limited resistance to ultraviolet light, friction, and heat', and some synthetics are damaged by UV (Wikipedia 'Rope'). HMPE (Dyneema) soft shackles are weakened by heat, 'also heat caused by friction', and can be 'cut by sharp edges, burned, or deteriorate in some environments' (Wikipedia 'Shackle'). Temperature: US rules allow fibre rope slings from -20 to +180 °F (about -29 to +82 °C) without lowering their working load (OSHA 1910.184(h)(2)). Movement and vibration while slack can draw an unlocked buried tail out of a braided splice (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice'). Chafe: chain splices see heavy load and chafing and should be inspected, though chain on a little-used anchor may rust before the splice wears (Animated Knots 'Chain Splice'). Natural-fibre rope rots more readily than synthetic rope (Wikipedia 'Rope').

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: rope, spliced by hand
  - 3D print (FDM): splicing fids, pull fids, thimbles and pulleys or blocks used with spliced rope (see make_it_yourself)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the splice is made in rope; only the splicing tools and the thimbles or blocks it fits are printed.
- **Tools:** Tape and a sharp knife; a lighter, hot knife or whipping twine to stop the strand ends fraying; a fid (a smooth tapered spike of wood, bone, aluminium or plastic: Swedish fid, tubular push fid, uni-fid or pulling fid) or a steel marlinspike to open the strands (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing', 'Fid'). In small, soft three-strand rope, masking tape wrapped on each strand end, a pencil or a ball-point pen can do instead (Animated Knots 'Eye Splice'). Braided-rope splices also use a marker, measuring tape, a wire pusher or splicing needle, and stitching twine and needle for the lock stitch (Wikipedia 'Eye splice'; Animated Knots 'Whoopie Sling'; Samson 12-Strand Class II Eye Splice); 'Often a hammer and winch are used as well for tougher splices' (Wikipedia 'Eye splice').
- **Difficulty:** moderate: a three-strand eye splice or a bury splice in hollow braid needs only a fid, patience and practice, but the locked Brummel 'tends to be confusing and repetition is required' (Animated Knots), and double-braid splices need careful measuring with the rope maker's instructions (Samson). In tests, three-strand splices made by inexperienced people were as strong as each other; the authors judged a professional's splice unlikely to be stronger 'so long as the splice geometry was correctly formed' (Milne and McLaren 2006).

### 3D printing tips

Splices aren't printed, but printing helps around them. 1) Splicing fids: one design (3, 4, 6 and 8 mm sizes, 'Ideal for Dyneema') prints in PLA, PETG or ABS but not glass- or carbon-filled filament, which makes it 'too rough'; it suggests 0.05 mm layers, 50% [infill](../glossary.md#infill), 3 [perimeters](../glossary.md#perimeters) and slow speed, printing upright (no supports, 'but not as strong') or lying flat with supports under the tip, sanding with 100-600 grit, and keeping 'the tip blunt to prevent it from catching on rope fibers' (Printables 999397). 2) A pull fid can be a printed handle with a loop of stainless steel wire wrapped tightly round it and taped (Printables 640920). 3) Printed thimbles: a parametric design (6-12 mm rope, STEP file to resize) recommends 'PETG or another though [tough] filament like ASA or TPU', notes that infill matters less than perimeters, and warns 'I haven't tested these thimbles for load bearing uses!' (Printables 934812); use metal thimbles for anything important ⚑ [uncertain]. 4) Printed pulleys and blocks are often hung from a soft shackle: one design for up to 6 mm rope uses a 3 mm Dyneema soft shackle and was tested to 100 kgf for 3 days in the sun, with a destructive test suggesting about 150 kg breaking force (Printables 552267). 5) Where a spliced eye bears on a printed part, give it a large, smooth, rounded surface or a thimble so it can't chafe or cut the rope, and size the eye so its two legs meet at no more than 60 degrees over the pin (the OSHA rule for lifting slings) ⚑ [uncertain for printed parts].

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Rope sold by construction (three-strand laid, eight-strand plaited, 12-strand single braid, double braid) and fibre; splicing fids (Swedish fid, tubular fid, uni-fid, pulling fid), marlinspike, wire splicing needles and pushers, whipping and stitching twine; thimbles (round and teardrop, steel or nylon); ready-made soft shackles, whoopie slings and spliced eyes from riggers and rope makers; free splicing instructions from rope makers such as Samson, New England Ropes and Yale Cordage (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing', 'Fid', 'Eye splice'; Samson 'How to splice rope'; Animated Knots 'Whoopie Sling').

## Rules of thumb

- Tucks in a three-strand eye splice, side by side: at least 3 in natural fibre and 5 in synthetics (Toss, The Complete Rigger's Apprentice p. 83, via Wikipedia 'Eye splice'); in slippery synthetics 'a minimum of five complete tucks is required', and seven for 'mooring, towlines, and other long term or critical applications' (Animated Knots 'Eye Splice', after ABOK #2725); Samson's eye splice for nylon, polyester and olefin rope is one round of tucks then '4 more complete tucks' (Samson 3-Strand Class I Eye Splice); the legal minimum for US lifting slings is 3 full tucks in manila and 4 in synthetic rope (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.184(h)(3)); in tests on polyester rope, 2-tuck splices slipped at 73% of the rope's breaking load while 3, 4 and 8 tucks all reached full strength (Milne and McLaren 2006).
- Tucks in a short splice: natural fibres 'hold well with three tucks each side', synthetics need at least five (Animated Knots 'Short Splice', ABOK #2634); OSHA's minimum for slings is 6 full tucks (3 each side) in manila and 8 (4 each side) in synthetic rope.
- How much to unlay: about 3 rope diameters per tuck, e.g. 7.5 in for five tucks in 1/2 in rope and 10.5 in for seven (Animated Knots 'Eye Splice'; Wikipedia 'Eye splice'); Samson counts back 16 picks for its eye splice; a seven-tuck chain splice needs at least 21 diameters (Animated Knots 'Chain Splice').
- Tails: on slings, don't trim strand tails flush; leave at least 6 rope diameters beyond the last full tuck (rope under 1 in) or [taper](../glossary.md#taper) them in with at least 2 extra tucks (OSHA 1910.184(h)(3)(iii)). If tails are too long it is 'far less trouble to make another tuck than to cut them', and melted ends add a little security; true tapering of single strands in slippery modern rope 'should probably never be attempted by amateur' splicers: instead cut out one strand, tuck once more with two, then once more with one (Animated Knots 'Eye Splice').
- Tuck against the lay (twist) of the rope and keep each strand's own twist; 'rope strength can be lost if the strands are twisted incorrectly' (Samson 3-Strand Class I Eye Splice; Animated Knots 'Eye Splice').
- Bury length in hollow braid, side by side: 72 rope diameters for slippery HMPE (Dyneema, Spectra) and about half that for polyester (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice', citing Brion Toss and Starzinger); at least 60 diameters, with DSM advising 60 times for coated and 100 times for uncoated Dyneema, e.g. 36 cm in 6 mm coated rope (Wikipedia 'Eye splice'); for a whoopie sling's first bury about 40 diameters in high-modulus fibre or 25 in polyester, nylon or polyolefin (Animated Knots 'Whoopie Sling', after Atlantic Braids).
- Braid splices: taper the tail, because a square end 'creates a weak point'; stitch it (a zigzag right through rope and tail, then back at 90 degrees) or lock it, because otherwise 'movement, shaking, and inquisitive fingers' can pull it out; whip the throat of the eye for the greatest security (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice'). Samson's lock stitch: at least 3 complete stitches on each side, each crossing 2 picks, starting no more than 1/4 fid length below the splice entry (Samson 12-Strand Class II Eye Splice).
- Fid lengths: rope makers' instructions measure in fid lengths, typically 21-22 rope diameters (a short fid is 1/3 and a long fid 2/3 of that) (Wikipedia 'Fid').
- Short or long splice: the short splice is about twice the rope's thickness and the stronger of the two; the long splice is only slightly thicker and still passes through pulleys but 'sacrifices some of the strength' (Wikipedia 'Rope splicing'). Samson's long splice unlays at least 36 picks, swaps strands over at least 25 picks and tucks each strand 4 times (Samson 3-Strand Class I Long Splice); Animated Knots calls it hard to control in slippery nylon.
- Back splice: about three tucks are enough because it carries no load; it makes the end about twice as thick and usually stops the rope passing through blocks, so a whipping is usually better (Animated Knots 'Back Splice'; Wikipedia 'Rope splicing').
- Lifting slings (US rules): at least 10 rope diameters of clear rope between eye splices, eyes big enough that the included angle at the splice is no more than 60 degrees over the load or support, 'Knots shall not be used in lieu of splices', and repaired or reconditioned fibre rope slings are prohibited (OSHA 1910.184(h)).
- Soft shackle: the stopper (diamond) knot must be pulled extremely tight, until it 'should feel like a wooden ball', or it may slip; trim the tails to about 1/4 in only if it is really tight, otherwise leave at least 1 in; Animated Knots has a calculator for rope length and mark positions from the rope diameter and loop size (Animated Knots 'Soft Shackle', after Allen Edwards).
- Size the rope with a design factor: breaking strength 5 to 12 times the working load, 12:1 for critical loads (Cordage Institute CI 1401-15, quoted by Animated Knots 'Safety').

## Common failures

Slipping out under load because there are too few tucks (2-tuck splices slipped at 73% of breaking load in tests) or too short a bury or overlap (the shortest braid-on-braid splices slipped at 15-24%, at very unpredictable loads) (Milne and McLaren 2006): use the tuck counts and bury lengths in rules_of_thumb, more for slippery rope. The buried tail working out of a braided splice while slack, from movement or fiddling: taper, stitch or lock it and whip the throat (Animated Knots 'Long Bury Splice'). A weak point at a square-cut tail: taper it (Animated Knots). Tapered strand ends in slippery rope getting 'dislodged' and making the splice untidy: drop whole strands instead (Animated Knots 'Eye Splice'). Strength lost because strands were twisted wrongly or tucked with the lay instead of against it (Samson). Chafe or cutting at the eye where it sits on a chain, shackle or sharp edge: fit a thimble (Wikipedia 'Eye splice'; Samson). A soft shackle's knot slipping because it wasn't pulled tight enough (Animated Knots 'Soft Shackle', after Allen Edwards). Heat from friction weakening HMPE (Wikipedia 'Shackle').

## Safety

Home-made splices aren't rated or tested: never use one for climbing, arborist work, lifting people or anything that would hurt someone if it let go. 'Wrongly handled, gripped, or tied, rope can kill, maim, or burn'; for live or critical loads rope makers' guidance is a design factor of 12:1 (Animated Knots 'Safety', quoting Cordage Institute CI 1401-15). In US workplaces, spliced fibre rope lifting slings must meet minimum tuck and tail rules plus the maker's recommendations, and repaired slings are banned (OSHA 1910.184(h)). Rope under load stores energy and snaps back if it breaks: never stand in a loop or bight that may suddenly tighten (Animated Knots 'Safety'); a soft shackle is 'less likely to cause injury if it strikes a crew member' than a metal one (Animated Knots 'Soft Shackle'). Burns from lighters and hot knives when sealing strand ends; hard, heat-sealed ends can be sharp enough to slice a hand (Budworth, quoted in Wikipedia 'Rope'). Sharp fids and marlinspikes can stab the hand holding the rope ⚑ [uncertain].

## Related

- Fixed loop knots (bowline, figure-eight loop, alpine butterfly): quicker loops that untie, but weaker (62-84% in the same tests) and the bowline can shake loose when slack
- Bends for joining two cords (sheet bend, double fisherman's): the knotted, removable way to join two ropes that a short splice does permanently
- Rope-end finishing (whipping, heat-sealing, back splice): the back splice is one of several ways to stop fraying
- Woven self-tightening grip (finger-trap grip): explains why a buried splice grips, the same braid-narrowing principle
- Shackle and quick link: the metal links a spliced eye usually connects to; the soft shackle is its rope version, with more test figures
- Wire-rope terminations (wire-rope clip, thimble, crimped sleeve): the equivalent loops for steel wire rope, including thimbles
- Binding knots and seizings: a seizing binds two parts of a rope together without interweaving strands
- Tensioning and adjustable hitches (trucker's hitch, taut-line hitch): knotted ways to tension a line instead of a whoopie sling
- Load-bearing stitch patterns for straps and webbing: the sewn equivalent for flat webbing
- Stopper knots and cord-end anchors: other ways to fix a cord end to a part
