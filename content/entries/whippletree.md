A whippletree is a free-floating bar pulled or pushed at a pivot near its middle, so it tips until the forces at its two ends balance, sharing one force equally (or in a set ratio) between two points, and several bars hung from each other share it between many points.

**In plain words:** A loose bar that is pulled (or pushed) at its middle and fixed to something at each end, so it tips like a see-saw until both ends pull equally; more bars can hang from its ends to share one pull between four, eight or more points. **Proper term:** Whippletree (whiffletree; load-equalising lever, equaliser bar).

**Also called:** whiffletree / whippletree / whippletree mechanism (Wikipedia); swingletree or singletree (the bar behind one horse) and doubletree (the bar that shares a load between two horses) (Wikipedia; Hansen Wheel); evener, two-horse evener, three-horse evener (farm hitching); leader bar (for the front pair of a four-horse team) and equalizer (Wikipedia); balance bar / bias bar (the pivoting bar between the two brake master cylinders in a race-car pedal box); parking-brake (handbrake) cable equaliser (cars); wiper blade frame, bridge and yokes (a conventional windscreen wiper blade is a whippletree set working in compression); flotation cell / whiffletree mirror cell / 9-point or 18-point cell (telescope mirror supports); whiffletree differential / seesaw differential (underactuated robot and prosthetic hands); whiffletree loading mechanism (aircraft structural testing); mechanical digital-to-analog converter (the IBM Selectric typewriter's whiffletree [linkage](../glossary.md#linkage)); equalising beam / equalising lever / compensating beam (a close relative in railway suspension, sharing weight between axles).

## How it works

The bar is held only at its middle pivot, so it is free to tip. It settles where the turning effects of its two end forces balance (the lever law): with the pivot in the middle both ends carry half, and with the pivot at one third of the length the short side carries two shares and the long side one, as in a three-horse evener (Rural Heritage). If one end meets more resistance or its cable is shorter, the bar simply tips until the shares are equal again, so it evens out differences in length or position; hanging a second layer of bars from each end splits each share again (1 to 2 to 4 to 8), and in a telescope mirror cell triangles on see-saws spread the weight over 18 points (Wikipedia; Fullerscopes). It keeps sharing only while the bar can still tip: once one end is stopped, for example when one finger of a robot hand touches an object, the other ends keep moving, which is what lets one motor close several fingers round an odd-shaped object (Frontiers).

## Parts

A few parts for one bar: the bar itself, a pivot pin, hook or clevis at (or near) its middle, and a pin, hook or cable end at each end. A stacked set is a small assembly that acts as one unit: for example 3 bars share one tendon pull between 4 fingers (Frontiers hand), and an 18-point telescope mirror cell uses 6 triangles balanced on 3 see-saw bars (Fullerscopes). Optional extras: stops that limit how far each bar can tip, locks that stop one output (OpenBionics), and screws or turnbuckles to set cable lengths.

## Everyday examples

- A conventional (framed) windscreen wiper blade: its bridge and yokes are a series of whippletrees that spread the arm's spring force along the rubber (Wikipedia)
- A car's parking-brake (handbrake) cable equaliser, which shares one cable's pull between the two rear brakes (MGA Guru)
- The evener and singletrees behind a team of horses pulling a plough or wagon (Wikipedia)
- Alexander Calder's hanging mobiles, which are whippletrees in tension (Wikipedia)

## When to use it

Use it when one force has to be shared evenly (or in a fixed ratio) between two or more points whose lengths or positions are not exactly equal: two cables that must pull equally (parking brake), one motor closing several fingers that touch an object at different moments (underactuated grippers and prosthetic hands), pressing a strip evenly onto a curved surface (wiper blade), supporting a thin mirror or plate on many points without bending it (telescope cells), loading a test structure as if the load were spread out (wing tests), or letting several animals or people pull one load fairly. It is simple, light, cheap and works in tension or compression; with an adjustable pivot it also sets a deliberate split, as in a brake balance bar.

## When not to use it

Avoid it when the outputs must move together exactly: use a rigid link, a linkage or gears. Avoid it when the outputs may need to move very different distances or keep turning: its travel is limited by the bar length, so use a rope-over-pulley differential or a gear differential instead (Frontiers compares these). It floats, so it will not hold a position or a grasp by itself: add locks or use a non-back-drivable drive. It needs free space to tip and translate (Frontiers), so in very tight spaces a pulley or gear differential may fit better. For pressing a wiper evenly, the modern alternative is a pre-curved spring-steel beam blade with no frame (Stoner Car Care).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - change speed or turning force: it splits one pull or push into equal (or deliberately unequal) shares at two or more points
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): each output can keep moving after another has stopped, so linked fingers, brake shoes or support pads each settle where they need to
  - change the kind of motion: several inputs can be added into one output movement, as in the IBM Selectric typewriter and linkage-based mechanical computers (Wikipedia)
  - tighten and hold tension: in a parking brake it shares one cable's pull equally between the two rear brakes (MGA Guru)
- **Movement:** rotate+slide: each bar tips (rotates) about its middle pivot through a limited angle while the pivot itself moves along the line of the pull; the travel is limited by the bar length and any stops (21 mm of tendon travel in the Frontiers hand, falling to 10 mm when three of the four fingers are blocked)
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 1 extra freedom per bar: besides following the input, each bar can tip, so its two ends can move by different amounts while their (weighted) average follows the input. A tree of 3 bars feeding 4 outputs therefore has 3 of these extra freedoms, which is why one motor can close four fingers that each stop at a different moment (Frontiers)
- **Special behaviour:**
  - self-aligning or self-centring: the bar tips by itself until the forces at its ends balance, so it automatically evens out unequal cable lengths, horse positions or surface curvature (load equalising)
  - motion conversion (e.g. rotary to linear): used the other way round, it adds several input movements into one output movement (mechanical adding, and the IBM Selectric's mechanical digital-to-analog converter) (Wikipedia; Hackaday)
  - none of the holding behaviours: it floats, so it does not lock or hold a position by itself unless locks are added (the OpenBionics hand adds a lock button for each finger)
- **How it holds:**
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): it passes a pull or push on and shares it between its ends
  - shape (the parts interlock; [form-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): its pins, hooks and clevises interlock with the links, cables or traces
- **Input and output:** One pull (or push) -> two pulls (or pushes) in roughly the same direction, or many when bars are stacked. Force split: each end carries the total force multiplied by the distance from the pivot to the OTHER end, divided by the bar length, so a centred pivot gives 50/50 and a pivot at one third gives 2:1 (Rural Heritage). Movement: for a centred bar the input moves by the average of the two end movements, so if one end is stopped the other end travels twice as far as the input. Efficiency: close to lossless apart from pivot friction; no measured figure was found ⚑ [uncertain]. Shaft arrangement: not shafts; the input and outputs are forces roughly parallel to the line of pull. Back-drivable: yes; it is a plain lever with nothing to lock it, so pulling on an output moves the input and the other outputs.
- **Power:** passive (no power needed); it simply passes on whatever pull or push it is given (from a horse, a hand lever, a brake pedal, a spring or a motor-driven tendon)
- **Load:** Light to heavy depending on size: small plastic bars in robot hands (the Frontiers whiffletree failed at 42.8 N per output), steel balance bars in brake pedal boxes, hardwood eveners 42-48 in long for horses (Hansen Wheel), and multi-level whiffletrees that load whole aircraft wings in structural tests (ASEE; SwRI). Strong along the line of pull; the bar itself is loaded in bending, highest at the centre pivot, so it needs to be deepest there ⚑ [uncertain]. Weak against sideways pushes and twisting, and it cannot carry any load once an end has run out of travel.
- **Play and precision:** It deliberately does not locate: the bar floats, so it has no fixed position. Play in the pins does not spoil the sharing, but friction does: a binding pivot or a cable that is trapped instead of floating stops it equalising (MGA Guru). The share stays close to the lever ratio only while the bar can still tip; once a bar hits a stop or runs out of travel it stops sharing (Frontiers). It tolerates large [misalignment](../glossary.md#misalignment) between its outputs by design, but only up to its travel (21 mm total, or 10 mm with three outputs blocked, in the Frontiers hand).
- **Taking it apart:** removable: each bar is hung on pins, hooks or clevis pins and can be unhooked; brake balance bars and equalisers bolt in
- **How often it can be reused:** effectively unlimited: nothing is deformed when it is unhooked and re-hung; the limit is wear of the pivot holes and pins
- **Sealing:** none: it is an open bar and pins; nothing about it seals
- **Adjustable:** yes: the split is set by where the pivot sits. A threaded race-car balance bar moves the pivot by turning it (about 4% change in front/rear brake bias per full turn, up to about 30% in total; Tilton via Street Muscle); farm eveners use different pivot or hitch holes (three-horse evener pivot at one third; Burton, Rural Heritage); the length of each cable or trace is set with screws, turnbuckles or chain links so the bars sit level before they start sharing
- **Access needed:** not applicable: it hangs in the load path and is pinned at three points, reached wherever the mechanism opens
- **Materials:** Hardwood with iron or steel fittings for horse eveners and singletrees (ash doubletree and hickory singletrees, Hansen Wheel; hickory or straight-grained oak, Burton). Steel for brake balance bars (with a spherical bearing at the pivot, Tilton), parking-brake equalisers and wiper-blade frames. Steel pipe cross-members and nylon cord in a university test rig (ASEE). Laser-cut acrylic (Plexiglas) or 3D-printed ABS plates for the OpenBionics hand. Telescope cells use metal triangles and bars with slippery plastic pads under the mirror (Fullerscopes).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Framed wiper blades collect ice and snow in their open frame, which spoils the contact (Stoner Car Care). Rusty or dirty pivots stop it equalising: MGA Guru's equaliser arm needs its bronze bushing to move without binding and its cable jacket to float freely. Vibration can loosen the locknuts of a threaded brake balance bar and shift the bias (Tilton via Street Muscle). Printed PLA bars soften above about 60 C and degrade in UV light, so keep them out of hot cars and sunlight; PETG copes to about 80 C (Prusa). Wooden eveners are finished with linseed oil against weather (Burton; Hansen Wheel).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - 3D print (FDM): small bars and stacked trees for grippers, prosthetic hands and models
  - laser cut: flat acrylic or plywood bars (the OpenBionics hand can be laser cut or 3D printed)
  - woodwork: traditional eveners and singletrees in hickory, oak or ash
  - sheet metal: pressed steel wiper-blade frames and equaliser brackets
  - CNC: machined steel or aluminium balance bars and structural-test whiffletrees
  - off-the-shelf part: parking-brake equalisers, brake balance bars, framed wiper blades, horse eveners
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the bars print flat in minutes but are assembled on separate pins (bolts, dowel pins or a length of filament); no [print-in-place](../glossary.md#print-in-place) whippletree model was found
- **Tools:** None for hook-and-pin versions (farm eveners); spanners or hex keys for bolted pivots, locknuts on a threaded brake balance bar (Street Muscle / Tilton), and turnbuckles or screws to set cable lengths (ASEE test rig used a turnbuckle; the Frontiers hand needs precise tendon tension calibration)
- **Difficulty:** easy: a single bar with three holes is simple to cut, print or drill; the tricky parts are giving each bar enough free travel, keeping the pivots free of friction, and setting cable lengths so all outputs start together

### 3D printing tips

1) Print each bar lying flat with its pivot holes vertical, so the bending load in the plane of the bar runs along the printed [perimeters](../glossary.md#perimeters) rather than across layers. 2) Holes print undersized: oversize them in the model or ream them out (EngineerDog suggests about 0.005 in over size), and use metal pins or a short brass tube as a bushing so the bar tips freely; any friction at the pivot stops it sharing (MGA Guru's advice for car equalisers: the arm should swing freely with finger force). 3) Put the three holes on one straight line and use the lever rule for their spacing: equal gaps for 50/50, a pivot one third along for a 2:1 split (Rural Heritage). 4) Give every bar enough room to tip: allow at least the largest difference in travel you expect between outputs (the Frontiers hand allows 21 mm), and stagger or shorten the lower bars in a stack so they cannot collide. 5) For tendon-driven grippers, add a screw or turnbuckle to each tendon so you can set the lengths; the Frontiers team notes that precise tendon tension calibration is needed. 6) If you want to hold a grasp or stop one finger, add a lock per output (OpenBionics uses locking buttons, or a servo-driven cam). 7) Material: PETG or nylon cope with heat and constant load better than PLA, which softens above about 60 C (Prusa); make the bar deepest around the centre hole, where bending is greatest ⚑ [uncertain].

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Clevis pins with R-clips or hitch pins, shoulder bolts and bronze bushings for pivots; rod ends and spherical plain bearings (a race-car brake balance bar pivots on a spherical bearing, Tilton); motorsport brake balance bars, pedal boxes and remote bias adjusters (e.g. Tilton, OBP (brands)); car parking-brake (handbrake) cable equalisers; framed (conventional) windscreen wiper blades; singletrees, doubletrees and three-horse eveners from harness and wagon suppliers (e.g. Hansen Wheel (brand)); turnbuckles and wire-rope thimbles for setting cable lengths; telescope flotation mirror cells (e.g. RC Optical Systems' 9- and 18-point cells (brand)).

## Rules of thumb

- Equal split: put the pivot exactly in the middle. For a 2:1 split, put the pivot one third of the way along from the end that must carry two shares: in a three-horse evener the single horse's end is twice as far from the hitch point as the two-horse end (Rural Heritage, 'Hitching Three: Adjustments'); the king-pin hole is bored 'at one-third the distance from the end' (Burton, Shop Projects Based on Community Problems, 'Evener and Singletree').
- In general, each end's share of the force = total force x (distance from the pivot to the other end) / (distance between the two ends); the lever balances when both turning effects are equal (Rural Heritage's 2:1 evener is the case with a 2:1 distance ratio).
- Stacking: each two-ended bar doubles the number of shares; 3 bars share one tendon between 4 fingers (Frontiers, Gao et al. 2021), and 6 triangles on 3 see-saw bars give an 18-point mirror cell (Fullerscopes).
- To imitate a spread-out (distributed) load with a whippletree, divide the load into parts and put each pivot at the centroid (resultant) of the loads it carries; the more levels, the closer the imitation (ASEE Advances in Engineering Education, 2009).
- Allow enough travel: the Frontiers hand's whiffletree gave 21 mm of tendon travel with all fingers free but only 10 mm with three fingers blocked, limited by the length of the upper bars (Gao et al. 2021).
- Race-car brake balance bar: each full turn of the threaded bar changes the brake bias by roughly 4%, the maximum adjustment is about 30% (beyond that change master-cylinder sizes), and the bar should sit parallel to the firewall when the pedal is loaded (Tilton Engineering via Street Muscle).
- More support points spread the load better: on RC Optical Systems' 16-inch mirror the calculated peak-to-valley error fell from 0.0179 wave with a 9-point cell to 0.0071 wave with an 18-point cell (RCOS).

## Common failures

1) A bar runs out of travel or hits its stop, so it stops sharing and one output takes all the load: give it more room or shorter output differences (Frontiers). 2) A binding pivot, bent pin or trapped cable stops it equalising: keep pivots free and let cable housings float (MGA Guru). 3) Unequal starting lengths mean one side must take up its slack before the other starts pulling: set lengths so the bars sit level (Frontiers' tendon calibration; parking-brake adjustment). 4) Pressure concentrated at the few claws of a framed wiper blade causes uneven wear and streaks (Stoner Car Care). 5) A threaded bias bar [creeps](../glossary.md#creep) out of adjustment when its locknuts are not tightened (Tilton). 6) Printed or wooden bars crack at the centre hole, where bending is greatest ⚑ [uncertain].

## Safety

Brake balance bars are safety-critical: a badly set bias can lock the wheels and spin the car under heavy braking (Tilton via Street Muscle). If one end's cable or link breaks, the bar swings to its stop and the remaining end may suddenly carry the whole load or none of it, so size every link for the full load ⚑ [uncertain]. Swinging bars and taut cables create pinch points; heavy structural-test whiffletrees can fall if a link fails.

## Related

- Gear differential (bevel or spur): shares [torque](../glossary.md#torque) between two outputs like a whippletree but allows unlimited continuous rotation, at the cost of more parts
- Rope-over-pulley (tendon) differential: a cable running round a floating pulley shares tension equally with more travel than a bar, but with rope friction (Frontiers compares both)
- Pulley system (block and tackle): also uses floating parts, but to multiply force rather than to share it
- Equalising beam (railway suspension): a pivoted beam that shares a vehicle's weight between two axles (Wikipedia)
- Bell [crank](../glossary.md#crank): a pivoted lever that turns a pull round a corner rather than splitting it
- Beam wiper blade: a pre-curved spring-steel strip that replaces the whippletree frame of a conventional wiper blade (Stoner Car Care)
- Fluidic differential: hydraulic or pneumatic sharing between fingers, without bars but with the risk of leaks (Frontiers)
