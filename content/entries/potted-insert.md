A local plug of hard epoxy (with or without a metal insert) set into the soft core of a sandwich or cored panel where a bolt or screw goes, so the fastener has something solid to grip and clamp against and the core around it stays sealed.

**In plain words:** A way to put a solid, screw-holding spot into a light panel that has a soft or hollow filling between two thin skins: you open the hole up, scrape the soft filling out around it, fill the space with epoxy (often around a metal threaded insert), and drill or screw into that solid plug, so the bolt can't crush the panel and water can't get into the filling. **Proper term:** Potted insert (sandwich-panel insert; hard point).

**Also called:** sandwich panel insert, honeycomb insert, panel insert (Shur-Lok 'Sandwich Panel Inserts'; Abbott Aerospace AA-SB-001 12.2.6); potted-in insert, potting an insert (Abbott Aerospace AA-SB-001 12.2.6); hard point, hardpoint (Kitplanes 'Tough Spot'; CMU Buggy 'Hard Point Manufacturing Documentation'; Model Aviation 'Installing a One-Piece Bolt-on Wing'); overdrill, fill and redrill; drill oversize, fill with epoxy and redrill (Practical Sailor 'A Solid Connection'; The Yacht Rigger); reefing and backfilling a cored deck (Cruising World, Steve D'Antonio); core-carve method (Practical Sailor); epoxy plug, epoxy ring (Practical Sailor; Cruising World); flox hard point (homebuilt aircraft; Kitplanes); through insert, blind insert, floating insert; spacer (Shur-Lok); hardware pad, high-density core insert (laid in during building; Cruising World).

## How it works

Cored panels are stiff and light because a thick, soft core holds two thin skins apart, but that core crushes when a bolt is tightened, and the thin skins tear or go egg-shaped under a screw (Kitplanes 'Tough Spot'). Potting replaces the core round the hole with a hard plug that is glued to both skins, so the bolt clamps against solid material and loads are passed into the skins over a wider area; Cruising World notes a 1/4 in bolt can press more than 500 lb on the core. In the factory version, the hole is drilled through one or both skins, the core is dug out round it, the insert is put in and potting compound is injected, locking the insert to the panel in all six directions (Abbott Aerospace 12.2.6). On boats the same plug also seals the core: water that gets past the sealant reaches only epoxy, not the balsa or foam (The Yacht Rigger; Practical Sailor).

## Parts

A few parts: the sandwich panel (two thin skins of fibreglass, carbon, plywood or aluminium with a core of honeycomb, foam, balsa or plywood), the potting compound (epoxy thickened with a filler such as colloidal silica, flox or chopped fibre, or a structural adhesive), and either a factory insert (a metal or plastic threaded bush with a flange and two small potting holes) or simply the fastener drilled through the cured plug. Often a backing plate, washer or cover patch of glass cloth too (Abbott Aerospace; Shur-Lok; Kitplanes; Practical Sailor).

## Everyday examples

- cleats, solar-panel brackets and antenna bases on a cored fibreglass boat deck (The Yacht Rigger; Cruising World)
- luggage bins, galleys and floor panels in airliners, made of honeycomb sandwich with potted inserts (Shur-Lok)
- steering, axle and harness mounts in a carbon-sandwich racing buggy chassis (CMU Buggy)
- wooden dowel hard points where nylon bolts hold a model aircraft's wing on (Model Aviation)

## When to use it

Whenever you need to bolt or screw something to a cored or sandwich panel: honeycomb, foam or balsa between thin skins. Use it for every through-bolted fitting on a cored boat deck, even lightly loaded ones, because any hole lets water reach the core (The Yacht Rigger). Lay solid hard points in during building if you know where the hardware goes (Cruising World). Use factory flanged inserts for repeated, accurate attachment points in honeycomb panels.

## When not to use it

Not needed in solid fibreglass, solid wood or solid plastic: drill, [countersink](../glossary.md#counterbore--countersink) and seal instead (The Yacht Rigger), or use a heat-set, press-fit or screw-in insert. Where drilling must be avoided or the back can't be reached, a Bonding fastener (glue-on stud) avoids the hole entirely (Practical Sailor 'Glue-on Fasteners'). For a soft-core panel where you only need to stop the bolt crushing it, a Compression limiter (metal sleeve) is a simpler alternative. For rotten or soaked core, repair or replace the core first (Practical Sailor).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: gives a bolt, screw or threaded insert a solid place to grip in a soft-cored panel
  - glue or bond: the plug and insert are held by epoxy or structural adhesive bonded to the skins and remaining core
  - seal it, or pass cables or fluid through: seals the core round a hole so water can't soak into it (Practical Sailor; The Yacht Rigger)
- **Movement:** none
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: the cured potting locks the insert to the panel 'in all six [degrees of freedom](../glossary.md#degrees-of-freedom)' (Abbott Aerospace 12.2.6).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it is a fixed reinforcement for a fastener; a 'floating' factory insert lets its nut move a little to take up screw [misalignment](../glossary.md#misalignment) (Shur-Lok)
- **How it holds:**
  - [material bond](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond) (glue, solvent or melting): the potting compound is glued to the skins and core
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the cured plug is wider than the hole in the skin, and an insert's flange sits against the skin, so it cannot pull straight out (Abbott Aerospace 12.2.6) ⚑ [uncertain: the 'wider than the hole' locking is inferred from the figures]
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Medium to heavy, depending on plug size and panel. Strongest in compression (bolt clamp) and in-plane [shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear), because the plug spreads load into the skins; pull-out (tension) strength depends on the core's shear strength just outside the potting, which is usually the weak point (Abbott Aerospace 12.2.6 method). Bigger plugs carry more, because load capacity rises with circumference (Kitplanes). One team's test: a 1 in factory insert with a 5/16 in through-bolt in a 1/2 in carbon sandwich panel held 30 kN (6,744 lbf) static (CMU Buggy; a single club test, not a design value). An epoxy plug with no backing plate can crack with age and be pushed out of position (Practical Sailor).
- **Play and precision:** No play once cured. Position [accuracy](../glossary.md#repeatability--accuracy) depends on drilling: the final hole is drilled through the centre of the cured plug, or the factory insert is held flush while it [cures](../glossary.md#cure). In thin carbon skins, a flush insert flange must have no [chamfer](../glossary.md#chamfer--fillet) or it [undercuts](../glossary.md#undercut) the skin under side load (Abbott Aerospace 12.2.6).
- **Taking it apart:** permanent for the potting itself (the plug is part of the panel); the bolt or screw that goes into it stays removable
- **How often it can be reused:** once for the potting; the fastener in it can be undone many times. A plug drilled for a self-tapping screw wears with repeated use; glass strips mixed in help for screw holes (Practical Sailor) ⚑ [uncertain: no cycle figure]
- **Sealing:** watertight: the epoxy plug isolates the core so water that gets past the bedding sealant can only reach epoxy, and the hole is then chamfered and bedded with sealant (Cruising World; The Yacht Rigger). An unsealed hole lets water wick sideways through foam or plywood core well beyond the hole (The Yacht Rigger).
- **Adjustable:** no: the position is fixed once the plug cures; drill the [pilot hole](../glossary.md#pilot-hole) with the part jigged in place first (Kitplanes).
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind) for the boat 'core-carve' method and for blind factory inserts, worked from the top with the underside taped; both sides for the through-the-thickness version (tape the bottom, bolt through) (Practical Sailor; The Yacht Rigger; Shur-Lok).
- **Materials:** Suits any sandwich or cored panel: fibreglass or carbon skins over balsa, PVC or polyurethane foam, Nomex or aluminium honeycomb, or plywood core (boat decks and cabin tops, aircraft and solar-car floors, buggy and RC panels) (Cruising World; Abbott Aerospace; CMU Buggy). Potting: epoxy thickened with colloidal silica or a high-density filler (Cruising World), flox (cotton fibre) and epoxy (Kitplanes), toughened epoxy such as G-Flex (Practical Sailor), or a toughened structural adhesive (CMU Buggy). Avoid hollow glass-bubble (microballoon) fillers, which still compress under a bolt (Kitplanes reader comment) ⚑ [uncertain: reader comment]. Polyester and vinylester bond poorly to already-cured skins (Practical Sailor). Not needed in solid laminate or solid wood, where you can drill and bolt directly (The Yacht Rigger). Foam-board models: a dowel or plywood hard point epoxied through the foam does the same job (Model Aviation).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Water: the main reason for potting; once water gets into a cored deck, balsa rots and freeze-thaw cycles can turn foam core to mush and cause delamination (Practical Sailor). Moisture during the job: epoxy has limited [tolerance](../glossary.md#tolerance) for damp core; dry the core first (Practical Sailor). Ageing: rigid epoxy plugs can become [brittle](../glossary.md#brittle--ductile) and crack over time; toughened epoxies crack less (Practical Sailor). Heat: as for epoxy generally ⚑ [uncertain: no figure on an opened page]. Cold: cure is much slower; one guide says allow a full 24 hours at normal room temperature and longer in the cold (The Yacht Rigger).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part (factory sandwich-panel inserts, epoxy and fillers)
  - woodwork or general workshop drilling (hand drill, hole saw, core-removal hook)
  - laying in solid wood, plywood or high-density foam hard points while building the panel
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the plug is cast in epoxy inside the panel; printed parts can only be the jig or a spool-shaped insert
- **Tools:** Drill and bits (pilot, oversize and final size) or a hole saw (Kitplanes: about 1 in is typical); a core-removal tool such as a bent nail, hooked Allen key or bent roofing nail in a drill, or a Dremel-type cutter (Cruising World; Practical Sailor; Kitplanes); masking tape for the underside; epoxy, filler, mixing pots and a syringe for filling (Cruising World); countersink to chamfer the hole for sealant (Cruising World; The Yacht Rigger); gloves and eye protection.
- **Difficulty:** moderate: only hand tools, but you must dig out the core without damaging the opposite skin, fill without trapping air, and wait for a full cure before drilling and tightening.

### 3D printing tips

A 3D print is itself a kind of cored panel (thin walls round sparse [infill](../glossary.md#infill)), but in a print it is simpler to design the hole [boss](../glossary.md#boss) solid (extra [perimeters](../glossary.md#perimeters) or a solid modifier region) or use a heat-set or press-fit insert than to pot one afterwards ⚑ [uncertain: general slicer practice, no source opened for this entry]. Printing is useful for jigs that hold a factory insert flush and square while the potting cures, or for a printed spool-shaped insert for a foam-board model; keep printed inserts away from heat, as a large epoxy fill can get hot ⚑ [uncertain: no tested example found]. ### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

- sandwich panel inserts: threaded or non-threaded, through or blind, locking or non-locking, and floating versions (Shur-Lok (brand) 'Sandwich Panel Inserts')
- panel spacers (one-piece, or plug and sleeve) for floor panels, often with epoxy under the flange (Shur-Lok)
- potting compounds and structural adhesives (e.g. Loctite EA E-60HP (brand), used by CMU Buggy)
- epoxy fillers: colloidal silica (WEST SYSTEM 406 (brand)), high-density filler (WEST SYSTEM 404 (brand)), cotton flox (Cruising World; Kitplanes)
- toughened epoxy (WEST SYSTEM G-Flex (brand)) (Practical Sailor; Kitplanes reader comment)

- nutplates riveted to the hard point (homebuilt aircraft; Kitplanes)

## Rules of thumb

- Boat deck, top skin only: redrill the top skin about half again as big as the fastener hole (1/4 in -> 3/8 in), reef out the core about 1/2 in round the hole, tape the underside, fill with epoxy thickened 'to the consistency of mayonnaise', then redrill slightly oversize (5/16 in for a 1/4 in fastener) and chamfer for sealant (Cruising World, Steve D'Antonio).
- Boat deck, through both skins: drill an oversize hole through the whole deck, e.g. 5/8 or 3/4 in for a 5/16 in bolt, tape the bottom, inject thickened epoxy, cure 24 hours, then drill the bolt hole through the centre (The Yacht Rigger). The two sources' oversize ratios differ (1.5x vs 2-2.4x); both aim to seal the core and stop crushing.
- Use slow hardener: the core insulates the hole, so fast epoxy in warm weather or sun can boil and leave a foamy plug; Practical Sailor found fast 105/205 OK only below about 70 °F and out of the sun (Practical Sailor).
- For screw holes, push in strips of 6 oz glass cloth (about 3/4 x 2 in, 2-4 strips in small holes) after injecting half the epoxy, to stop cracking; don't use mat, roving or biax (Practical Sailor).
- Back the plug: an unbacked epoxy plug can become brittle, crack and be pushed out, so use a backing plate (Practical Sailor).
- Aircraft hard point: a 1 in hole saw through the inner skin is typical; larger circumference carries more load; cover with glass patches about three times the hole diameter, laid on the wet flox (Kitplanes 'Tough Spot').
- Factory insert: drill the skin to the insert's diameter, clear the core well beyond it, fill completely, push the insert in with a twist, inject through one potting hole until it comes out of the other, cure 24 hours (CMU Buggy).
- Design: the foot of the bracket bolted to a potted insert must be at least as wide as the potting, and the insert flange must stay flush and parallel to the skin (Abbott Aerospace 12.2.6, from ECSS-E-HB-32-22A).

## Common failures

- Crushed core and loose fitting: bolt tightened straight onto foam or balsa without a plug; pot the hole so the bolt bears on epoxy (Cruising World; The Yacht Rigger).
- Soft, rotten deck spreading from the hole: hole sealed only with bedding compound, which eventually fails; seal the core with an epoxy plug (The Yacht Rigger).
- Foamy, bubbly or weak plug: fast hardener exotherming in heat or sun; use slow hardener or a toughened low-exotherm epoxy (Practical Sailor).
- Voids and air pockets: fill slowly with a syringe or brush, and inject until it comes out of the second potting hole (Cruising World; CMU Buggy).
- Damaged opposite skin: digging or hole-sawing too deep; stop at the core (Kitplanes).
- Plug cracking or pushing out over time: no backing plate; add one, or add glass strips to the plug (Practical Sailor).
- Fastener tightened before full cure, still bearing on soft material (The Yacht Rigger).
- Insert flange chamfered or proud in a thin carbon skin, which undercuts the skin under side load (Abbott Aerospace).

## Safety

Epoxy skin allergy: some people become sensitised, with symptoms 'from skin irritation to full-on shock' (Practical Sailor); wear gloves and eye protection, and never clean epoxy off skin with solvent (WEST SYSTEM 'Health effects from overexposure to epoxy'; 'General Epoxy Safety Guidelines'). Overheating: fast epoxy in an insulated hole or a large fill can exotherm and boil; a hot pot of epoxy can ignite nearby materials and give off fumes, so mix small batches (Practical Sailor; Epoxyworks 'Health and safety'; The Yacht Rigger). Dust: wear eye protection when reefing out core (Cruising World) and don't breathe sanding dust from partly cured epoxy (WEST SYSTEM). Water in a cored deck rots balsa and can lead to soft, unsafe decks (Practical Sailor). Hard points that hold people (harness, lifeline or rigging fittings) must be designed and tested for the load; CMU Buggy tested theirs before using them for driver harnesses.

## Related

- Heat-set threaded insert, Press-fit and expanding inserts, Screw-in threaded insert: all need solid material round them to grip; a potted insert makes that solid material inside a soft core
- Blind threaded fixings (rivet nut, well nut): squeeze against a thin sheet from one side; a rivet nut would crush a soft core
- Bonding fastener (glue-on stud): glued to the surface with no hole at all, so the core is never opened
- Compression limiter (crush tube, bolt sleeve): a metal sleeve that takes the bolt's clamp load; an epoxy plug does the same job and also seals
- Fillet-and-tape joint: glassing a bulkhead or frame into a panel; a potted hard point is often the mounting spot inside such a structure
- Two-part epoxy: the potting compound; filler choice and slow hardener matter here
- Hollow-wall anchors (toggle, molly): the equivalent for plasterboard, which also has thin skins but no glue-in plug
- Mid-print embedding ([captive nuts](../glossary.md#captive-nut), magnets and bearings): the 3D-printing way to place a threaded part inside a part while it is being made
