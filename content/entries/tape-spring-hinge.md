A strip with a curved cross-section, like a tape-measure blade, clamped between two parts so that it folds by buckling into a kink and then unfolds itself and locks straight, giving a hinge with no pin, no friction and no latch.

**In plain words:** A short piece of curved metal strip, like the blade of a tape measure, used as a hinge: it folds with a sharp kink when pushed, then springs back straight and stiff on its own and stays locked straight. **Proper term:** Tape-spring hinge.

**Also called:** tape spring; measuring-tape hinge or carpenter's-tape hinge; tape-spring rolling hinge (TSR hinge, a tape-spring hinge guided by rolling wheels and wires); self-deploying, self-locking elastic hinge; composite tape-spring hinge (glass, Kevlar or carbon fibre versions); bistable tape spring (a version that stays either rolled up or straight); snap bracelet or slap bracelet (the bistable steel-strip toy that uses the same effect); tape measure antenna (radio antenna elements cut from a tape-measure blade); Carpentier joint (European aerospace name for tape-spring hinges) ⚑ [uncertain].

## How it works

The curve across the strip makes it behave like a shallow gutter, which is very stiff against bending while it is straight; this is why an extended tape-measure blade stays rigid (Wikipedia, Tape measure). Bend it hard enough and the middle of the strip flattens and suddenly snaps through, gathering all the bending into a short kink that is flat across and curved along its length with a radius about equal to the tape's original cross-curve radius; once kinked, only a small, nearly constant moment is needed to keep folding (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997). The bent tape stores elastic energy, so when released it unfolds the hinge by itself, and as it comes straight the cross-curve pops back and the hinge locks straight with no latch (Watt and Pellegrino 2002).

## Parts

A few parts: one or more short strips of curved tape (steel tape-measure blade, beryllium copper, or glass, Kevlar or carbon-fibre composite) clamped at each end to the two parts being joined. Space hinges often use two tapes with their curves facing opposite ways, sometimes with stacked tapes, and the TSR hinge adds acetal rolling wheels held together by tensioned wires to guide the fold (Watt and Pellegrino 2002).

## Everyday examples

- A tape measure: the curved steel blade stays rigid when pulled out, a design patented by Hiram Farrand in 1922 (Wikipedia, Tape measure)
- Snap (slap) bracelets, made of layered bistable steel bands (Wikipedia, Slap bracelet)
- Bicycle trouser clips made from curved spring strip (Naked Scientists)
- Tape-measure radio antennas for amateur satellite reception, and tape-spring antennas on CubeSats (Printables 540996; cubesatshop.com ISIS antenna)

## When to use it

Use a tape-spring hinge when something should fold flat for storage and then open by itself and lock straight with no latch, pin or motor: fold-out antennas, booms, panels, flaps, legs, camera or sensor poles, toys and kinetic art. It is light, cheap, has zero friction and backlash, needs no lubrication, and has no moving parts to jam after long storage (Watt and Pellegrino 2002); because it deploys on its own stored strain energy it needs no motor (Communications Engineering 2024).

## When not to use it

Avoid it where the folded part must be held in a controlled position or carry load while folded (it is very floppy when folded; Watt and Pellegrino 2002); use a detent hinge, friction hinge or knuckle hinge instead. Avoid it where a heavy load could buckle it when it should stay straight; add a locking pin or over-centre lock. Avoid it where a sudden snap would be dangerous or damaging, or where the hinge must move slowly and smoothly under control; use a damped or self-closing spring hinge.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): folds up to about 180 degrees by bending, with no pin or rubbing surfaces
  - store energy: the folded tape stores spring energy that deploys the hinge by itself (Watt and Pellegrino 2002)
  - lock, and release by hand: it snaps and locks straight on its own, and a firm push past its peak moment buckles it to fold again
  - join and hold: once straight it holds the two parts in line and carries a small bending load
- **Movement:** flex: folds by forming a local kink; tape-spring hinges have been used to deploy panels through about 180 degrees (Watt and Pellegrino 2002)
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 1 intended (folding about the kink). When folded the hinge is very floppy in other directions too (very low stiffness in the folded configuration, Watt and Pellegrino 2002); when straight it is stiff in all directions
- **Special behaviour:**
  - over-centre (snaps past a point and stays): once near straight the tape snaps back into its curved section and locks straight without any latch; folding it again needs a push past the peak (buckling) moment (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997)
  - self-aligning or self-centring: elastic latching gives a highly repeatable, accurate deployed position (Watt and Pellegrino 2002)
  - fail-safe or breakaway: an overload buckles the tape into a fold instead of breaking the parts, as long as the tape stays elastic ⚑ [uncertain]
- **How it holds:**
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): the curved section's own stiffness holds the hinge straight, and the stored bending energy drives it there
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed). Space versions are held folded by a restraint and then released, e.g. the ISIS CubeSat antenna uses a thermal knife that burns through a wire (cubesatshop.com); power is only needed for that release device, and once released the tape deploys on its own
- **Load:** light: it locks strongly for its weight but deploys gently; a 135 mm long TSR hinge with one pair of 25.4 mm wide steel tapes needed only 0.1 to 0.3 N m to deploy but locked against 13 N m (Watt and Pellegrino 2002). Strong along the tape and stiff while straight; it resists bending that puts the tape's edges in tension (opposite-sense bending) better than bending the other way (equal-sense bending), which is softer and buckles sooner into a twisted fold (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997); once folded it carries almost no load
- **Play and precision:** Play: none; tape-spring hinges have zero friction and backlash. Repeatability: elastic latching gives highly repeatable and accurate positioning (Watt and Pellegrino 2002). Misalignment: tolerates little while folded because it is so floppy, which makes deployment paths uncertain; on latching the panel can rebound and re-buckle the tapes (twice in an undamped test) before finally locking (Watt and Pellegrino 2002)
- **Taking it apart:** removable: the tapes are clamped or bolted to the parts (e.g. aluminium clamp blocks in the TSR hinge, Watt and Pellegrino 2002) and can be unscrewed
- **How often it can be reused:** many times, as long as the fold never overstresses the tape; a tape that overshoots and yields is permanently damaged (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997); no cycle count found ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** no after assembly; the deployment and locking moments are set by the tape size, the number of stacked tapes (moments rise roughly in proportion) and the spacing between the tapes (Watt and Pellegrino 2002)
- **Access needed:** not applicable: the tape is clamped to both parts before use
- **Materials:** Steel tape-measure blade: the TSR hinge used 'Contractor Grade' steel tape 25.4 mm wide, 0.1 mm thick with a 15 mm cross-curve radius (Watt and Pellegrino 2002). Heat-treated beryllium copper (CuBe) sheet 0.1 mm thick (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997). Fibre composites (glass, Kevlar, carbon) for space hinges and booms (Roccor video; Kwok and Pellegrino). Plastics such as LDPE can form tape springs but relax during storage and deploy slowly (Kwok and Pellegrino). 3D-printed plastics are suited to the clamps and brackets rather than the spring itself ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Polymer and composite tape springs relax during storage, so the energy available to deploy drops; deployment slows with longer storage and higher temperature, and in severe cases they stop deploying on their own (Kwok and Pellegrino). Temperature also matters for composite booms: one segment of the Mars Express MARSIS fibreglass-and-Kevlar radar boom deployed but did not lock until the spacecraft was turned to warm it in sunlight (ESA 2005) ⚑ [uncertain whether its hinges were tape springs]. Steel tape blades can rust where the coating is cut ⚑ [uncertain]. Vibration could unlatch a lightly locked hinge ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: a strip cut from a steel tape-measure blade (Watt and Pellegrino 2002; Naked Scientists)
  - 3D print (FDM): clamps, brackets and couplers that hold tape-measure strips, as in printed tape-measure antenna couplers (Becky Stern, Printables)
  - sheet metal: beryllium copper or spring-steel strip formed to a curved section (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997); forming the curve at home is untested ⚑ [uncertain]
  - CNC: machined clamp blocks (aluminium blocks in the TSR hinge, Watt and Pellegrino 2002)
  - composite layup: glass, Kevlar or carbon-fibre tape springs for space use (Roccor video) [advanced]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the springy strip is normally a metal tape-measure blade clamped into separate brackets; a printed plastic strip would relax and lose its snap over time (Kwok and Pellegrino, on polymer tape springs); not tested for printed plastics ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Tools:** Tin snips or old scissors to cut the tape and tough tape to cover the sharp cut edges (Naked Scientists); a screwdriver for clamp screws ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Difficulty:** easy to moderate: cut a strip from an old steel tape measure and clamp its ends to printed, wooden or metal brackets; the tricky parts are covering the razor-sharp cut edges (Naked Scientists), leaving enough free length so the fold does not damage the tape near the clamps (Watt and Pellegrino 2002), and softening the snap on latching

### 3D printing tips

Print the clamps and brackets, not the spring: use a steel tape-measure blade for the tape itself and print blocks that grip each end. Shape the clamping faces to match the tape's cross-curve so the curve is not flattened where it is held ⚑ [uncertain], and leave enough free tape between the clamps for the kink to form without reaching them; Watt and Pellegrino sized their hinge so the tapes would not be damaged during folding, with 88 mm of free tape length for 25.4 mm wide tapes and a smallest workable hinge of 106 x 44 x 21 mm. Clamp two tapes back to back with their curves facing opposite ways so the hinge behaves the same in both fold directions and does not overshoot when it latches (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997). Expect a sharp snap on latching: in tests the undamped hinge gave peaks of about 150 g, reduced to about 25 g by sticking sound-damping tape (3M 434) to both sides of each tape (Watt and Pellegrino 2002); a strip of damping or foam tape is an easy maker version ⚑ [uncertain]. Becky Stern's printed tape-measure antenna couplers were printed at 20% infill (Printables 540996). Avoid printing the spring itself from PLA or other plastics that creep; plastic tape springs lose force during long storage, faster when warm (Kwok and Pellegrino); PLA was not tested ⚑ [uncertain].

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Steel tape measure ('carpenter's tape', 25 mm or 1 in blade; the TSR hinge used 'Contractor Grade' tape); beryllium copper strip (e.g. alloy C17200) ⚑ [uncertain]; composite tape-spring hinges from space suppliers (e.g. 'Roccor (brand)'); CubeSat deployable antenna systems with tape-spring elements (e.g. 'ISISPACE (brand)'); sound-damping tape (e.g. '3M 434 (brand)') to soften latching (Watt and Pellegrino 2002).

## Rules of thumb

- To keep the fold elastic (no permanent kink), the tape's cross-curve radius R divided by its thickness t must be at least E / (yield stress x (1 + Poisson's ratio)); for heat-treated beryllium copper this means R/t of at least 85.6 (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997). For comparison, the steel tape-measure blade used by Watt and Pellegrino (R = 15 mm, t = 0.1 mm) has R/t = 150
- The kink's lengthwise radius is about equal to the tape's cross-curve radius R, so the fold is roughly a cylinder of radius R (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997)
- Steady folding moment of one tape: (1 + v) x D x a when bent so the edges stretch (opposite sense) and (1 - v) x D x a the other way (equal sense), where D = E t^3 / 12(1 - v^2), v is Poisson's ratio and a is the angle the cross-section subtends in radians; these moments do not depend on tape length (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997)
- Steady deployment moment of a hinge with one pair of tapes: M = E t^3 a / (6(1 - v^2)); real hinges give a larger, uneven moment because the tapes touch (Watt and Pellegrino 2002)
- Mount tapes in pairs with their curves on opposite sides: this creates an energy well that traps the moving part's energy at lock-out so it does not overshoot (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997)
- Stacking two or three tapes raises both the deployment and locking moments roughly in proportion (Watt and Pellegrino 2002)
- Damping tape on both sides of each tape cut latching shock about six-fold (150 g to 25 g), better than crushable foam (60 g) (Watt and Pellegrino 2002)
- To make a bistable snap bracelet from a tape measure, bend a 15 to 20 cm length around a radius of about 6 to 10 mm until it just starts to deform (Naked Scientists)

## Common failures

Permanent kink or weakened snap when the tape is folded too tightly or overshoots, because it yields (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997); choose a large enough R/t and add end stops. Rebounding and re-buckling instead of locking on the first try; mount tapes in opposed pairs and add damping tape (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997; Watt and Pellegrino 2002). Tapes damaged near the clamps when the free length is too short (Watt and Pellegrino 2002). Loss of deployment force after long storage in plastic or composite tapes (Kwok and Pellegrino). Buckling sideways into a twisted fold when bent the 'wrong' (equal-sense) way (Seffen and Pellegrino 1997).

## Safety

Cut tape-measure steel has very sharp edges; cover them with tough tape (Naked Scientists), and note that slap bracelets were recalled because the steel band could pierce its cover and cut (Wikipedia, Slap bracelet). A folded tape spring stores energy and snaps open suddenly; latching shocks reached about 150 g on an undamped test hinge (Watt and Pellegrino 2002), so keep faces and fingers clear of larger ones.

## Related

- Bistable compliant mechanism: a one-piece part with two resting shapes; a bistable tape spring (snap bracelet) is one kind
- Constant-force spring: another curved steel strip, coiled on a drum, that gives a nearly constant pull, used in spring-return reels
- Self-closing hinge (spring hinge): a pin hinge with a separate spring; stronger and controllable, but it has friction and parts that can jam
- Detent hinge (click hinge): holds several set positions rather than folding flat and snapping straight
- Living hinge and flexure hinge: bend instead of pivoting, but do not lock straight on their own
- Rolling-contact joint: Watt and Pellegrino's TSR hinge combines tape springs with a wire-wrapped rolling hinge to control the fold
- Origami mechanisms and deployable structures: fold patterns that also let flat panels deploy
- Over-centre (toggle) lock: a linkage that snaps past centre to lock a hinge straight, with separate parts
