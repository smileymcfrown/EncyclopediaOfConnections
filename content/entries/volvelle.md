Two or more paper or card discs share a single pivot through their centres, so turning one disc against another moves pointers, windows and printed scales into new line-ups, giving a paper calculator, a reference dial, a code wheel or a changing picture.

**In plain words:** Paper or card circles stacked on one centre pin, so that turning one circle lines up its windows, pointer or pictures with the circle underneath. **Proper term:** Volvelle (wheel chart; rotating disc or wheel mechanism in paper engineering).

**Also called:** wheel chart (Wikipedia 'Volvelle'; Puget Sound Collins Library); paper wheel, rotating paper 'wheel chart' (Museum Wales); volvella: the medieval name, used for a movable device for working out the position of the sun and moon in the zodiac (Wikipedia 'Volvelle'); Apian wheel, after Petrus Apianus (Peter Apian), the 16th-century maker of many volvelle designs (Museum Wales; Marsh's Library); rotating disc (Mark Hiner's ten pop-up mechanisms, Center for Book Arts record); wheel: an illustrated disc held between two pages and seen through die-cut holes (popuplady.com glossary); rotary mechanism (Warwick WMG paper-engineering lesson 3); turning wheel, revolving wheel, rotator (Newberry Library workshop; Sabuda how-to-book list); dissolving wheel, dissolving disc, metamorphosis or transformation (first meaning): two slotted discs that pass over and under each other to change one picture into another (popuplady.com glossary); interlocking Victorian turning circle (Sabuda's summary of Joan Irvine's 'How to Make Super-pops'); turn-the-wheel card, reveal wheel card (card-making terms; Craft with Sarah); code wheel, cipher wheel, secret code wheel (Wikipedia 'Volvelle' on game 'feelies' and code wheels; Printables cipher-wheel models); slide chart (the wider family of hand-held paper calculators that volvelles belong to; Wikipedia 'Volvelle'); planisphere (a rotating star chart, a close relative; Wikipedia 'Volvelle' see-also).

## How it works

Each disc carries information arranged round a circle: numbers, names, pictures or scales. Because all the discs turn about the same centre, a mark at a given radius on one disc always passes over the same ring on the disc below, so turning the top disc lets a window or pointer pick out one item at a time (popuplady.com: 'In the case of discs, often there are die-cut holes to allow the information on the base page to show through'). Early volvelles worked out the positions of the sun and moon, holy days and tides, and 20th-century wheel charts did everything from 'inventory control to color calibration' (Wikipedia 'Volvelle'). In a hidden wheel, the disc sits between two pages, an edge of it sticks out through a slot so the reader can turn it, and pictures on it appear through windows in the front page (popuplady.com 'Wheel'; Warwick lesson 3). In a dissolving wheel, two round pictures on one centre are both cut into pie-shaped segments, so that as one is turned its segments slide over and under the other's, and the top picture 'metamorphoses' into the bottom one (popuplady.com 'Metamorphosis' 1).

## Parts

A few parts: a base sheet or largest disc; one or more smaller discs or pointers stacked on top (largest at the bottom, smallest on top); die-cut windows in the upper layers; and a centre pivot. The pivot can be a knotted string or thread, a split-pin paper fastener (brass fastener, brad), a rivet, a metal grommet or eyelet, or a paper rivet made of split card tabs pushed through a hole and glued (Museum Wales; Puget Sound; popuplady.com; Warwick lesson 3). A hidden wheel adds a front page with windows and spacers so the wheel can turn between the two layers (Warwick lesson 3; Craft with Sarah).

## Everyday examples

- Wheel charts and 'spinners' given away as promotional calculators, colour or unit converters, and verb-conjugation or first-aid wheels (Wikipedia 'Volvelle', quoting Jessica Helfand)
- Code wheels and cipher wheels, such as the volvelles packed with the Infocom games Sorcerer and A Mind Forever Voyaging to stop copying (Wikipedia 'Volvelle')
- Turning-wheel greetings cards and children's books where a picture changes in a window as you turn a disc at the page edge (Craft with Sarah; popuplady.com 'Wheel')
- Planispheres (rotating star charts) and the 'Apian wheels' in old astronomy books (Wikipedia 'Volvelle'; Museum Wales)

## When to use it

Use a volvelle when you want a cheap, flat, hand-turned dial: to look things up (a calculator, converter, calendar or quiz wheel), to set and read a value (a mood meter or 'Full-O-Meter' with an arrow on a split pin; WSU), to encode and decode messages (code wheels), or to make a picture change or move in a card or book. It is flat enough to bind into a book or post in an envelope, and it needs only card, a hole punch and a split pin or a piece of thread. Hide the wheel between two layers and show it through windows when you want a surprise reveal (Warwick lesson 3; Craft with Sarah). Use the dissolving wheel when you want one round picture to turn into another (popuplady.com).

## When not to use it

Avoid it for anything that carries load or must survive rough handling: card tears round the centre hole and the discs go floppy ⚑ [uncertain]. For a dial that must hold its setting firmly, use a clicking [detent](../glossary.md#detent) or a friction hinge. For a precise or load-bearing pivot in wood, plastic or metal, use a plain pivot with a pin or shoulder bolt, or a turntable (lazy Susan) bearing. For movement that happens by itself as a page opens, use a parallel-fold or V-fold pop-up or an automatic pull-strip instead. Avoid split pins in anything for babies and toddlers (see safety notes); use a glued paper rivet or a set grommet instead ⚑ [uncertain].

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): one disc turns freely about the shared centre pivot
  - click into set positions: in a loose sense only. The windows, pointers and printed scales give the reader a set of positions to line up with, but a plain paper volvelle has no click; it stays wherever friction at the pivot holds it ⚑ [uncertain whether any paper designs add a real click]
- **Movement:** rotate: unlimited turning, 360° and on round, for a volvelle or a hidden wheel turned from the page edge. A pointer or a disc limited by a slot (for example a wheel turned through a slot in the card) may only have a part turn ⚑ [uncertain for typical limits]. The dissolving wheel turns about one segment's width between its two pictures (popuplady.com describes the top picture rotating 'over then under' the bottom one).
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 1: each disc turns about the shared centre. Stacked discs each have their own 1 relative to the base, so a three-disc volvelle has 2 independent settings. A loose pivot also lets the discs tilt and slide a little, which is play rather than intended movement.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - indexing (clicks into set positions): only loosely; the printed scales and windows define the readings, but a paper volvelle doesn't click into them ⚑ [uncertain]
  - none: otherwise it simply turns and holds by light friction at the pivot
- **How it holds:**
  - shape (the parts interlock; [form-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): the pin, string knot, grommet or paper rivet passes through the centre holes and its head and spread legs (or glued tabs) stop the discs coming off
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): light clamping at the pivot keeps a disc where it was set; a split pin's opened legs or a snug knot 'as close to the base as possible' sets how stiff it is (Puget Sound Collins Library)
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): it is a pivot for turning, not a structural joint
- **Input and output:** Hand rotation of one disc -> rotation of that disc relative to the others, ratio 1:1, read off as a new line-up of windows, pointers or scales. Discs can be geared to the reading only by the printed scales: a circular slide rule or E6-B flight computer, for example, uses logarithmic scales so that turning one disc multiplies or divides (Wikipedia 'Volvelle' see-also) ⚑ [uncertain for detail]. Not back-drivable in any useful sense: there is no load.
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** light: carries only its own weight and the reader's finger. The centre hole is the weak point: under rough handling the card round it can tear or wear oval ⚑ [uncertain]. WSU's instructions say 'Flimsy copy paper will not work well' for a hand-turned pointer and suggest heavier stock.
- **Play and precision:** Noticeable play: a punched hole round a thin pin or thread lets the discs shift sideways and tilt, so a reading is only as precise as the printed divisions are wide. Puget Sound advises a snug knot ('You want a snug fit between the knot and the paper') and measuring the spacing of windows and the information below them accurately. Off-centre holes make windows drift off their ring as the disc turns, so mark and punch the centres carefully ⚑ [uncertain]. It tolerates curling and slight [misalignment](../glossary.md#misalignment) well because nothing is driven.
- **Taking it apart:** removable for a split-pin fastener (fold the legs back and pull it out) or a string pivot (untie it); permanent for a glued paper rivet, a set metal grommet or rivet, which must be cut out or torn to take the discs apart
- **How often it can be reused:** Taking apart: a few times with a split pin or string; once with a glued paper rivet or a set grommet. Turning: many times while the card stays stiff and the centre hole isn't worn oval ⚑ [uncertain: no cycle counts found]. Museum Wales and Puget Sound report 16th-century volvelles in books that still work, though some are 'in a very fragile state' (Museum Wales).
- **Sealing:** none: it is open paper
- **Adjustable:** yes: the setting is the whole point, as the discs are turned to any reading. The [stiffness](../glossary.md#stiffness) can also be adjusted: tighten or loosen a string knot, or squeeze or ease the split-pin legs ⚑ [uncertain for split pins].
- **Access needed:** both sides: a split pin is pushed through from the front and its legs are opened at the back; a string is threaded from the back and knotted at the front; a paper rivet is pushed through and glued on the other side (Museum Wales; Puget Sound; Warwick lesson 3; WSU). In a hidden-wheel card, the pivot is fixed before the second layer is glued over it (Craft with Sarah).
- **Materials:** Suits paper, card and cardstock (heavier stock is better for anything handled a lot; WSU), thin plastic sheet, craft foam for a stiff, easy-to-grip wheel (Craft with Sarah), and 3D-printed plastic discs (Printables cipher wheels). Early volvelles were printed on paper, sometimes on reused pages of discarded text (Puget Sound). Pivots: brass or other soft-metal split-pin paper fasteners (Wikipedia 'Brass fastener'), cotton or waxed thread, metal grommets or rivets (popuplady.com; Wikipedia 'Pop-up book'), or paper tabs. Doesn't suit very floppy paper (copy paper) or anything that must take real force; for wood, acrylic or metal dials use a proper pivot such as a shoulder bolt or rivet.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Paper and card absorb moisture, warp and curl in damp air, which can make discs bind or catch on windows ⚑ [uncertain: no source opened]. Sunlight fades printed scales ⚑ [uncertain]. 16th-century volvelles survive in library collections, some still working and some 'in a very fragile state' (Museum Wales), so a paper volvelle kept dry can last centuries. Brass paper fasteners don't rust ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - hand cut paper and card (scissors, craft knife, compasses or circle cutter, hole punch)
  - die cut or laser cut: commercial wheel charts use die-cut windows ('the generous use of the die-cut', Jessica Helfand quoted in Wikipedia 'Volvelle'); popuplady notes paper may now be cut with a laser
  - cutting machine (Cricut or Silhouette, brands) 'print then cut' for wheels and windows (Craft with Sarah)
  - 3D print (FDM): two flat discs with raised letters and a centre post and cap (Printables cipher wheels)
  - laser cut: card, thin plywood or acrylic discs ⚑ [uncertain: no laser-specific volvelle source opened]
  - off-the-shelf part: split-pin paper fasteners, eyelets and grommets
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: printed volvelles are printed as separate flat discs and joined with a centre post and press-on cap (Printables model 1882238) or by fitting the inner disc into a recess in the base (Printables model 226087). A [print-in-place](../glossary.md#print-in-place) version with a captive centre post is possible in principle ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Tools:** To make: scissors or a craft knife and cutting mat, a pair of compasses or a circle cutter for round discs (Puget Sound recommends a compass), a single-hole punch for the centre hole (WSU Full-O-Meter instructions), and glue for paper rivets and hidden-wheel cards. For the pivot: a split-pin paper fastener, a needle and strong thread such as waxed bookbinding thread (Puget Sound), or an eyelet or grommet setter ⚑ [uncertain for setter]. A cutting machine (Cricut, Silhouette; brands) can cut the windows and discs (Craft with Sarah). To use: none.
- **Difficulty:** easy: cut circles, punch a centre hole and push a split pin through; a child's activity in museums and schools (Museum Wales; Warwick; Newberry Library workshop). A dissolving wheel is harder because the segments must be cut accurately and woven together ⚑ [uncertain for difficulty rating].

### 3D printing tips

1) Print each disc flat; letters and scales can be raised so they read clearly, and one cipher-wheel design prints 'face down for best results' with a 0.25 mm nozzle and 0.15 mm layers for sharp symbols, using supports only in the recess for the slider (Printables 'Caesar Shift Cipher Wheel', model 226087). 2) Another design prints 'flat with no supports: two flat discs with raised letters, and a small cap' on a centre post, 110 mm outer and 75 mm inner wheel (Printables model 1882238). 3) Leave a running [clearance](../glossary.md#clearance) between the centre post and the hole so the disc turns but doesn't wobble; about 0.2-0.3 mm on the radius is a common FDM starting point ⚑ [uncertain: no source opened for this item]. 4) Monospaced fonts space themselves evenly round a circular text path (model 226087). 5) Raised ridges or lettering give fingers something to grip (model 226087's 'slider version with raised lettering for easier rotation'). 6) To add real clicks, put a small bump on one disc and matching dimples on the other at each position (see ball detent and printed detent entries) ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) Multi-colour printing or a filament swap at a set layer makes the scale readable without painting ⚑ [uncertain].

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Split-pin paper fasteners, also sold as brass fasteners, paper brads, round-head brads or butterfly clips (Wikipedia 'Brass fastener'; WSU 'split pins or round top brad fasteners'); scrapbooking brads; metal eyelets and grommets with a setting tool; waxed bookbinding thread and a needle (Puget Sound); foam squares or foam tape as spacers for a wheel hidden in a card (Craft with Sarah); craft foam sheet for a stiff wheel (Craft with Sarah); a single-hole punch (WSU) or 1/8 in hole punch.

## Rules of thumb

- Stack in order of size: 'The largest piece would serve as the base, and then all the corresponding circles would all be stacked up on top of each other in order, until the smallest was on the top. Then the whole thing would be secured through the middle with string' (Museum Wales); 'You can use a split pin to secure the circles instead of string!' (Museum Wales).
- Plan the windows against the information under them: decide where the cut-outs go on the top circle and measure 'accurate spaces on both the bottom and top circles' (Puget Sound Collins Library, which gives two circles of 6 in and 4 in circumference as an example).
- Thread pivot: knot one end, thread through from the back and tie off 'as close to the base as possible' for 'a snug fit between the knot and the paper' (Puget Sound).
- Paper rivet with no metal (Warwick lesson 3, rotary mechanism): make a centre hole 'about 1cm wide' in the card; on the wheel mark the same small circle, draw two lines across it to form two tabs, cut and fold them, push the tabs through the hole to the front, and glue the picture onto the tabs, 'making sure that it is free to turn around'.
- Hidden wheel turned from the edge (Warwick lesson 3): draw the wheel 'just smaller than the size of half the card', place it 'to the edge of the card' and cut a slot where you can turn the circle; for a variation the moving part sits inside 'using windows or slots to see the moving image'.
- Hidden wheel in a folded card (Craft with Sarah): fix the wheel with a brad through the card front, open the legs inside, then glue the front to the back with foam squares, one row along the top and two rows along the bottom, and 'Make sure there are no foam squares that will end up touching the wheel'; cut the wheel from craft foam, or several layers of heavy cardstock, for stiffness and grip.
- Use stiff stock for parts that are handled: 'Flimsy copy paper will not work well' for a hand-turned pointer on a split pin (WSU Extension 'Full-O-Meter' instructions).
- Wheel between pages: 'secured between two adjoining pages by a paper disc or metal grommet', with die-cut holes in the top page (popuplady.com glossary 'Wheel').
- Split pin fitting: the pin goes through punched holes and the legs are separated and bent over; 'When used for only a few sheets of paper, holes can be made using the sharp end of the fastener'. The legs are usually of slightly different lengths 'to allow easy separation' (Wikipedia 'Brass fastener').

## Common failures

1) The centre hole tears or wears oval and the disc wobbles: use heavier card, reinforce the hole with a paper patch or a grommet, and don't make the hole bigger than the pin needs ⚑ [uncertain]. 2) Too stiff to turn or too loose to stay set: adjust the knot or the split-pin legs; Puget Sound advises a snug knot. 3) Windows don't line up with the information under them: lay out windows and data on the same centre and radius before cutting, and measure the spacing on both discs (Puget Sound). 4) In a glued paper rivet or hidden wheel, glue gets on the turning part and fixes it: Warwick warns to make sure the picture 'is free to turn around', and Craft with Sarah warns to keep foam spacers clear of the wheel. 5) Flimsy paper buckles and catches; use heavier stock (WSU). 6) Off-centre pivot hole makes the disc swing out of its window as it turns ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) Dissolving-wheel segments catch on each other or show gaps if they are cut inaccurately; Mark Hiner's and Duncan Birmingham's books treat dissolving scenes as a separate, more advanced model with its own 'technical considerations' (Center for Book Arts record; Superplexus) ⚑ [uncertain for the failure itself].

## Safety

Split-pin paper fasteners have sharp leg tips (a split pin's sharp end can pierce a few sheets; Wikipedia 'Brass fastener'), so fold the legs flat and, for children, cover them with tape or glue a card patch over them [needs confirming]. Split pins, grommets and small discs are small parts that a young child could swallow or choke on: in the United States, toys and other articles intended for children under 3 years (36 months) that present a choking, aspiration or ingestion hazard because of small parts are banned, and 16 CFR part 1501 gives the test (eCFR). Use a glued paper rivet or a set grommet for very young children, and keep loose pins away from them [needs confirming]. Craft knives and die-cutting tools: cut on a mat, away from your hand.

## Related

- Split-pin paper fastener (brass fastener, paper brad): the commonest volvelle pivot, with its own entry; a volvelle is the mechanism, the split pin is the part
- Eyelets and grommets: the metal ring pivot used in the Astronomicum Caesareum and in commercial wheels (Wikipedia 'Pop-up book'; popuplady.com)
- Pull-tab and slider mechanism: moves pictures by pulling a strip instead of turning a disc; the slatted (Venetian-blind) transformation is the straight-line cousin of the dissolving wheel (popuplady.com 'Metamorphosis' 2)
- Parallel-fold and V-fold pop-ups: rise up automatically as the page opens; a volvelle has to be turned by hand
- Plain pivot and rolling bearing: the rigid-material version of a centre pivot, for loads
- Turntable (lazy Susan) bearing: a large flat bearing for a turning platform that carries weight
- Ball detent and printed detents: add real clicks if a dial must stop at set positions
- Slide chart and slide rule: the straight sliding version of the same 'line up two scales' idea (Wikipedia 'Volvelle' see-also)
- Score-and-fold hinge and glued tab joint: the other basic paper joints used to build volvelle cards and books
