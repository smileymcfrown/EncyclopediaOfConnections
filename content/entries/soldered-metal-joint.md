Soft soldering joins metal parts by heating them until a tin-based alloy that melts below about 450 °C flows into the narrow gap between them and wets both surfaces, giving a watertight, gas-tight and electrically conductive joint that is good for copper pipes and sheet metal but too weak to carry real mechanical loads on its own.

**In plain words:** Joining metal parts by melting a soft, low-melting metal (solder) into a tight gap between them with a soldering iron or a small gas torch, so that it flows in, cools and holds the parts together while sealing the gap, as on copper water pipes, stained glass and tinplate. **Proper term:** Soldered metal joint (soft soldering; sweat-soldered capillary joint in copper pipe).

**Also called:** soft soldering, soft solder, tin-lead or lead-free solder, plumber's solder (Wikipedia 'Solder'); sweating, sweat joint, sweated joint: US plumbing names for soldering pipe joints; outside the US 'sweating' means tinning two flat surfaces with solder and then reheating them together (Wikipedia 'Soldering'); capillary joint, capillary fitting, solder fitting, solder-joint pressure fitting (ASME B16.18 cast, B16.22 wrought) (Wikipedia 'Soldering'; CDA Copper Tube Handbook); end-feed fitting (no solder inside, solder fed in from the end) and solder-ring fitting, also called a Yorkshire fitting (a ring of solder already inside the fitting) (Wikipedia 'Soldering'); wiped joint: an old plumbing joint where pasty solder is wiped and moulded round the pipe joint (Wikipedia 'Solder'); tinning (coating a surface with solder first) (Wikipedia 'Flux (metallurgy)'; NBS Circular 492); soldering copper (a heavy copper-headed iron heated in a flame, used on sheet metal) (Wikipedia 'Soldering'); electric pipe-soldering tool or resistance soldering tongs, e.g. Antex Pipemaster (brand) (Wikipedia 'Soldering'; CDA Copper Tube Handbook); low-melt solder (e.g. 70 °C solder for white-metal model kits); fusible alloys such as Wood's metal, Field's metal and Rose metal (Wikipedia 'Fusible alloy'; GardenRails forum); came glasswork and the copper-foil (Tiffany) method in stained glass (Wikipedia 'Came glasswork'); not the same as silver soldering or hard soldering, which are kinds of brazing (see Brazed joint).

## How it works

The parts are cleaned to bare metal, coated with flux and heated until they are hot enough to melt the solder; the molten solder then 'wets' the surfaces, forming a thin alloy layer with them, and is pulled into the narrow gap by capillary action, the same effect that draws water up a thin tube (Wikipedia 'Soldering'). The flux dissolves oxide, stops the hot metal oxidising again and helps the solder spread (CDA Copper Tube Handbook). Because the parts themselves never melt, the joint can be made with a small iron or torch, but the solder is much weaker than the metal, so the strength comes from a large overlap area: lap and sleeve joints are several times stronger than butt joints (NBS Circular 492). In a copper pipe joint the pipe slides into a fitting cup with a gap of about 0.1 mm, the fitting is heated, and solder touched to the edge of the gap is sucked all the way round and in (CDA Copper Tube Handbook).

## Parts

Single piece once made: the two metal parts plus a thin film of solder between them. Making it needs a few things: a heat source (soldering iron or gun, soldering copper, gas torch, or electric pipe-soldering tongs), solder (wire, bar or the ring inside a solder-ring fitting), flux, abrasive cloth or wire brushes for cleaning, and something heat-proof to work on. Copper pipe joints also use a capillary fitting (coupler, elbow, tee, reducer or end cap) (Wikipedia 'Soldering'; CDA Copper Tube Handbook).

## Everyday examples

- copper water and central-heating pipes with soldered elbows and tees (Wikipedia 'Soldering'; Commons photo Fitting1537.JPG)
- stained-glass windows and Tiffany-style lamps, where lead came or copper foil is soldered at every joint (Wikipedia 'Came glasswork')
- sheet-metal objects such as food cans, roof flashing, rain gutters and car radiators (Wikipedia 'Soldering')
- brass musical instruments, whose bodies are often soldered, and brass, nickel-silver or white-metal model locomotive kits (Wikipedia 'Soldering'; GardenRails forum)

## When to use it

For permanent, sealed joints in copper and brass pipe and tube (water and heating pipes, model steam pipework, small tanks), for sheet-metal boxes, trays, gutters and repairs in tinplate, copper, brass and zinc, for stained glass, and for building brass and white-metal models, where the low heat avoids warping thin parts or melting nearby details. It is cheap, needs only an iron or small torch, and conducts electricity as well as sealing. Soldered pipe joints are quicker and cheaper than compression or flare fittings when many joints are needed, and last a long time if the flux is cleaned off (Wikipedia 'Piping and plumbing fitting').

## When not to use it

For load-bearing or structural joints (brackets, frames, shafts, hooks): soft solder is 'unsuitable for mechanical load-bearing applications' (Wikipedia 'Soldering'); use brazing, welding, bolts or rivets, or add a folded seam or screws and let the solder only seal (NBS Circular 492). For parts running above about 120 °C or carrying steam: braze instead (CDA). For wet pipes that can't be drained, or places where a flame is dangerous: use push-fit, press or compression fittings or flared joints (CDA). For hidden joints in walls that later need to come apart: use unions or accessible mechanical fittings. For stainless steel or aluminium, soldering is possible only with special fluxes and solders, so riveting, brazing or welding is often easier. For plastics use plastic welding; for wires see Soldered wire joint. Never use lead solder on drinking-water pipes.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: light-duty permanent joints in copper, brass, tinplate, lead came and similar metals
  - seal it, or pass cables or fluid through: watertight and gas-tight joints in copper water and heating pipes, tanks, cans, gutters and roof flashing (Wikipedia 'Soldering')
  - glue or bond: it works like a metal 'glue' that bonds to both surfaces, and also conducts electricity
- **Movement:** none
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: the parts are fixed together once the solder sets.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it is a plain permanent joint. It can be undone on purpose by reheating it, and very low-melting solders are used as heat-triggered weak links in fusible plugs and sprinklers (Wikipedia 'Fusible alloy'; see Burn-wire and fusible-link release)
- **How it holds:** material bond (glue, solvent or melting): the molten solder wets and alloys with the surface of each part and sets as a metal bond (Wikipedia 'Soldering'); for strength it is often combined with shape, e.g. a folded seam, a sleeve or a bolt (NBS Circular 492)
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed) once made; making it needs a soldering iron (mains or battery) or a gas torch
- **Load:** Light to medium. Soft solder itself 'does not make a strong joint and is unsuitable for mechanical load-bearing applications' (Wikipedia 'Soldering'). Joint shape decides the strength: a butt joint only carries the tensile strength of the solder, while lap, scarf and sleeve joints carry several times that, and a well-made soldered sleeve joint in copper tube can hold until the tube itself fails (NBS Circular 492). It is weakest when peeled or bent and under long-term load at raised temperature, where solder creeps; joints in copper tube 'should not be considered dependable' above about 121 °C with tin-antimony solder (NBS Circular 492). In lap-shear tests on 1 mm steel, copper and brass sheet, lead-free solders gave about the same joint strength as lead solders (Brožek 2013).
- **Play and precision:** No play. Because the parts don't melt and the heat is low, there is little distortion compared with brazing or welding, so close-fitting parts keep their shape. Parts must be held still until the solder has fully set: non-eutectic solders pass through a pasty stage, and movement then cracks the joint (Wikipedia 'Soldering', 'Solder'). Capillary joints need a gap of about 0.1-0.15 mm all round (CDA), so the tube must be supported centred in its fitting.
- **Taking it apart:** semi-permanent (can be undone but may damage parts): reheat until the solder melts and pull the parts apart; copper pipe joints are described as 'reasonably permanent but reversible' (Wikipedia 'Soldering'), but reheating a pipe joint near others can loosen them too
- **How often it can be reused:** a few times: a joint can be melted apart, cleaned and re-soldered, but repeated heating oxidises and erodes the parts
- **Sealing:** airtight or pressure: a good solder joint is 'electrically conductive, water- and gas-tight' (Wikipedia 'Soldering'); copper water and heating pipes are sealed this way, with rated working pressures given in the Copper Tube Handbook (CDA, Table 14.4a)
- **Adjustable:** no: the parts are fixed once the solder sets; they can only be repositioned by reheating the joint
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind) for many sheet-metal lap seams and stained-glass joints, where the iron and solder are applied from one face; pipe joints need access all round the joint to heat it evenly and feed solder round the whole gap (CDA Copper Tube Handbook)
- **Materials:** Easy: copper, zinc, brass, silver and gold; next easiest: iron, mild steel and nickel; difficult because of tough oxide films: stainless steel and some aluminium alloys; titanium, magnesium, cast iron, some high-carbon steels, ceramics and graphite only after plating with a metal that solder wets (Wikipedia 'Soldering'). Galvanised (zinc-coated) steel solders with a zinc/ammonium chloride flux, which must be cleaned off quickly because it attacks zinc (NBS Circular 492), and antimony-bearing solders should be avoided on zinc or galvanised metal because the joint goes brittle (Wikipedia 'Solder'). Stainless steel needs a very aggressive acid flux (NBS Circular 492). Aluminium needs special zinc or tin-zinc solders and fluxes (Wikipedia 'Soldering'; NBS Circular 492). Also used on tinplate, lead came, copper foil on glass and white-metal (low-melting) castings. It cannot join plastics or bare glass, and won't stick to painted, oily or plated-over surfaces until they are cleaned.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Heat: lead-tin solder weakens and creeps as it gets hot and should not be used above about 177 °C, less under constant load (NBS Circular 492); copper systems running continuously above 121 °C should be brazed (CDA). Vibration: a loose-fitting joint with a thick solder layer can crack under stress or vibration (CDA). Corrosion: flux residue left inside or outside a joint keeps corroding and can eat right through the tube wall (CDA); soldered aluminium joints suffer 'rapid galvanic corrosion' in moist or marine air unless protected, e.g. by a moisture-proof lacquer or paint over the joint (NBS Circular 492). Cold: tin on its own can suffer 'tin pest' at low temperatures; lead and antimony in the solder prevent it (Wikipedia 'Solder'). Low-melt bismuth solders can fail at only about 95 °C if they mix with lead-tin solder (Wikipedia 'Solder').

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - sheet metal: tinplate, brass and copper sheet, gutters, flashing, boxes and models
  - off-the-shelf part: copper pipe and capillary fittings (end-feed and solder-ring), solder, flux and soldering irons or torches
  - woodwork: not for the joint itself, but stained-glass panels are assembled on a wooden board with stops (Wikipedia 'Came glasswork' lists a plywood board among the materials)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: soldering joins metal; 3D printing can only make jigs and holders
- **Tools:** Soldering iron or soldering gun for small parts, sheet metal, stained glass and models (a 25 W iron is too small for large connectors, roof flashing or heavy lead came, Wikipedia 'Soldering'); a propane or propylene ('MAPP') torch, or an electric pipe-soldering tool for 8-22 mm pipe, for copper pipe (Wikipedia 'Soldering'; CDA); tube cutter, deburring tool or file, abrasive cloth or pads and fitting brushes (CDA); flux and a small brush (never apply flux with fingers, CDA); solder; a heat-proof mat or shield behind pipe work; damp cloth or heat-sink paste to protect nearby parts (Wikipedia 'Soldering'); a dry rag to wipe the joint; safety glasses, fume extraction or good ventilation, and a fire extinguisher for torch work.
- **Difficulty:** moderate: the tools are cheap and small parts are easy with an iron, but every joint needs clean metal, the right flux and enough heat, and copper pipe joints take practice ('Skill and knowledge are required to produce a satisfactorily soldered joint', CDA Copper Tube Handbook)

not applicable to the joint itself (plastic parts are joined by Melted plastic weld or heat-set inserts instead). ## Rules of thumb

- Gap: the capillary space between a copper tube and its fitting is about 0.004 in (0.1 mm), and capillary action works best between 0.004 and 0.006 in (0.10-0.15 mm); too much clearance 'can lead to solder metal cracking under conditions of stress or vibration' (CDA Copper Tube Handbook pp. 37-40, https://copper.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/copper_tube_handbook.pdf). In older NBS tests, sleeve-joint strength fell as the solder film got thicker than about 0.002 in (0.05 mm) (NBS Circular 492, https://archive.org/details/circularofbureau492mebs).
- Shape the joint for strength: butt joints only hold as much as the solder's own tensile strength, lap and scarf joints several times more; where the solder is mainly a seal or an electrical joint, get the mechanical strength first 'by bolting, interlocking, twisting together of wires, or by other suitable joining methods' (NBS Circular 492, https://archive.org/details/circularofbureau492mebs).
- Temperature limit: lead-tin joints 'should not be used at temperatures higher than about 350 °F (177 °C)', and less under prolonged loading (NBS Circular 492); for continuous service above 250 °F (121 °C), or where the highest strength is needed, braze instead (CDA Copper Tube Handbook p. 33-34, https://copper.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/copper_tube_handbook.pdf).
- Melting points: 63/37 tin-lead melts at 183 °C (eutectic, no pasty stage), 60/40 at 183-190 °C, 50/50 at 183-215 °C; common lead-free solders melt around 220 °C and are harder to use (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldering). Soldering is defined as using a filler that melts below 840 °F (450 °C); in practice most soldering is done at about 350-600 °F (177-316 °C) (CDA Copper Tube Handbook p. 35).
- Drinking water must use lead-free solder and approved flux: in the US solders with more than 0.2% lead have been banned for potable water since the 1986 Safe Drinking Water Act amendment (CDA p. 34; Wikipedia 'Solder' dates the US ban to 1974, the year of the original Act, so the sources differ); in the UK the Water Supply (Water Fittings) Regulations 1999 prohibit lead solder on pipes supplying water for 'cooking, drinking and bathing' (APHC, https://aphc.co.uk/news/safe-pipework-lead-and-the-law/).
- Copper pipe sequence: measure and cut square, ream the burr, clean to bright metal, flux thinly 'as soon as possible after cleaning', assemble and support, heat the tube then the fitting cup, feed solder opposite the flame, cool naturally (shock-cooling with water may stress the joint), wipe off flux and test (CDA Copper Tube Handbook pp. 35-40). Flux needed is about 2 ounces per pound of solder (CDA p. 40).
- Heat the fitting, not the solder: solder flows towards the heat, and if the copper starts to discolour it is overheating and the solder will stop flowing (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soldering). Don't point the flame into the face of the fitting cup or you burn the flux (CDA p. 39).
- Pipe repairs: the pipe must be dry, and 'Any pressure which is formed by heating of the tubing must have an outlet' (Wikipedia 'Soldering'); open valves part-way before heating them and heat mainly the tube (CDA p. 40).
- Iron size: a 25 W iron won't heat large connectors, copper roof flashing or big lead came, while 100 W may be too much for circuit boards; temperature-controlled irons hold their temperature better (Wikipedia 'Soldering'). Don't run the iron hotter than needed: solder fume levels 'can triple between 250-400 °C' (HSE INDG249, https://www.hse.gov.uk/pubns/indg249.pdf).
- White-metal model kits: use low-melt solder (about 70 °C) with a temperature-controlled iron set to about 150-170 °C, and a higher-melting 100 °C solder for the main build before adding details (GardenRails forum, https://www.gardenrails.org/viewtopic.php?p=138605). Bismuth-containing solders can form a phase that melts at only 95 °C with lead and tin, so joints can come apart or crack (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solder).

## Common failures

(1) Dirty or oxidised metal: solder balls up and won't flow; clean to bright metal and flux straight away (CDA; Wikipedia 'Soldering'). (2) Overheating: burnt flux, discoloured copper, and solder that won't enter the joint (CDA; Wikipedia 'Soldering'). (3) Too little heat: a cold, grainy joint sitting on the surface (Soldered wire joint entry covers cold joints). (4) Voids where flux was trapped because the flame was in the wrong place, so the solder sealed the mouth of the cup first; aim the flame at the back of the fitting cup and feed solder opposite it (Wikipedia 'Soldering'). (5) Water in the pipe stops it getting hot enough (Wikipedia 'Soldering'; CDA). (6) The parts moved while the solder was pasty, cracking the joint (Wikipedia 'Solder'). (7) Loose fit or excess solder film: weaker joint that cracks under vibration (CDA; NBS Circular 492). (8) Flux corrosion pinholes from too much aggressive flux left in the pipe; use only a thin coat and flush the system (CDA). (9) Burrs left inside a cut tube causing erosion-corrosion from turbulent flow; ream every cut end (CDA). (10) Brittle joints from antimony solder on galvanised metal, or bismuth solder mixed with lead-tin (Wikipedia 'Solder'). (11) A soft-soldered part asked to carry load creeps or peels apart over time; redesign as a lap, folded or bolted joint, or braze it (NBS Circular 492).

## Safety

FLUX FUMES: rosin-based flux fume 'is a top cause of occupational asthma'; use fume extraction, keep your head out of the plume, don't overheat the iron, and consider rosin-free fluxes (HSE solderer page; HSE INDG248, INDG249). Acid plumbing and sheet-metal fluxes contain hydrochloric acid, zinc chloride or ammonium chloride, which are harmful: use gloves, goggles and ventilation (Wikipedia 'Flux (metallurgy)'), and 'Do not apply with fingers' because flux carried to the eyes, mouth or cuts can harm you (CDA). Molten flux sticks to skin and can burn worse than a drop of solder (Wikipedia 'Flux (metallurgy)'). LEAD: lead solder and lead came are poisonous if swallowed; never eat, drink or smoke while working, wash your hands with soap and water afterwards (alcohol hand sanitiser does not remove lead), and keep children away; stained-glass work also exposes you to acid-flux fumes (Illinois Department of Public Health 'Lead Safety for Hobbyists'). Use lead-free solder wherever children may touch the work, outdoors, and always on drinking-water pipes, where lead solder is illegal (Wikipedia 'Soldering'; APHC; WaterSafe). Avoid cadmium-bearing solders: heating cadmium gives cadmium oxide fume, which damages lungs and kidneys (HSE EIS31). TORCH FIRES AND BURNS: an open flame in a building is a fire hazard (Wikipedia 'Piping and plumbing fitting'); put a heat mat behind pipes, don't heat pipes touching timber or other combustibles close enough to ignite them by conduction, keep an extinguisher ready, and keep a fire watch for at least half an hour afterwards to catch smouldering fires (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.252, the US hot-work rule for welding, cutting and brazing). Never heat a closed pipe or vessel: trapped water turns to steam and must have an outlet (Wikipedia 'Soldering'). Soldering irons reach several hundred degrees: use a stand and unplug it when finished.

## Related

- Brazed joint (brazing, silver soldering, hard soldering): the same capillary idea with a filler that melts above 450 °C at red heat; much stronger and good to higher temperatures, but needs a hotter torch
- Welded metal joint (fusion welding): melts the parts themselves; strongest, but much more heat, distortion and equipment
- Soldered wire joint (soldered splice): the same soft soldering used for electrical wires and components
- Solder sleeve (solder-seal splice): a heat-shrink tube with a solder ring for wires
- Compression fitting and Push-to-connect fitting: flame-free mechanical ways to join copper pipe, easy to undo; preferred for wet pipes or where a torch is unsafe
- Flare fitting: flame-free, metal-to-metal copper tube joint, sometimes required where torches are not allowed (CDA)
- Parallel pipe thread with a sealing washer (pipe union, dielectric union): screwed joints often found on the other end of a soldered fitting
- Burn-wire and fusible-link release: uses a deliberately weak low-melting solder joint as a heat-triggered release
- Two-part epoxy and Epoxy putty: cold-bonding alternatives for metal repairs that can't be heated, but not for drinking-water pressure pipes
- Blind rivet and Clinching: cold mechanical joints for sheet metal that carry more load than solder
