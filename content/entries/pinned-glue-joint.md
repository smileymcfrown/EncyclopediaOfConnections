Drill (or poke) a matching hole into each of the two faces to be joined, push a short pin into both, and glue it all together, so the joint is held by the glue and also by a hidden pin that stops it sliding or snapping off.

**In plain words:** A short bit of wire, rod, toothpick or skewer hidden inside a glued joint, poking into a hole in each part like a little bone, so a small glued-on arm, head or wall doesn't snap or slide off when it is handled. **Proper term:** Pinned glue joint (pinning; pinned butt joint).

**Also called:** pinning (miniature and model-kit term; Wikipedia 'Pinning (modelling)'); pinned joint / wire pin and glue; brass-rod pin, paper-clip pin, garden-wire pin; toothpick pin, toothpick joint (foam board; foldnfly 'Toothpick joints'); skewer pin, BBQ or bamboo skewer through foam (Flite Test); neck pin or toothpick neck (polymer clay figures); base pin (pinning a miniature to its base); dowelling a broken figure (repair wording) ⚑ [uncertain]; pinning a hinge (model-aircraft term for a toothpick or straight pin pushed through a flat hinge; see Slot-in model hinge).

## How it works

A plain glued butt joint between two small faces is held only by a thin layer of glue, which is [brittle](../glossary.md#brittle--ductile) (especially superglue on metal) and breaks with a knock or a sideways bend. A pin that goes into both parts carries that sideways ([shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear)) and bending load itself, so the glue only has to stop the pin pulling out; the long glued surface along the pin is much bigger than the joint face (Bolter and Chainsword: 'the strength of the bond is in the surface area'). Tabletop Gaming calls the wire 'a support beam of sorts'. One round pin still lets a part turn on it, so two pins, or one pin plus a shaped joint face, stop it spinning (Bolter and Chainsword). In soft foam a toothpick or skewer pushed through several layers does the same job, and in raw polymer clay a wire or toothpick is pushed in before baking so the part sits on it.

## Parts

A few parts: the two parts being joined, one or two pins (brass rod, paper-clip steel, garden wire, carbon rod, a round toothpick or a bamboo skewer) and the glue or filler (superglue, epoxy, epoxy putty, hot glue or, for raw clay, the clay itself). Temporary pins (dressmaker's pins, T-pins) are only used to hold parts while glue sets.

## Everyday examples

- a metal or resin tabletop miniature whose arm, weapon or wings are pinned on, and the miniature pinned to its base
- a polymer clay figure whose head sits on a toothpick or wire through the neck
- a foam-board model plane or school model held together with toothpicks and hot glue, or with skewers poking through the fuselage for the rubber-band wing hold-down
- a broken ornament or figurine repaired with a hidden pin

## When to use it

When a small glued joint has little contact area and will be knocked or handled: heavy metal miniatures used for gaming (Wikipedia), conversions where parts were not designed to fit together (Wikipedia), big or heavy resin parts (Starship Modeler), attaching a figure firmly to its base (Bolter and Chainsword), polymer clay heads, hearts and limbs that would otherwise just sit on top (a toothpick or wire pushed in before baking; The-Leaky-Cauldron.org and Tiny Pandora clay tutorials), and foam-board or foam-plane parts where glue alone tears the soft foam (foldnfly; Flite Test). Also as a quick way to hold foam-board walls square while glue sets, using pins (Studio Matrx: 'glue and hold with clamps/pins').

## When not to use it

Display models that are rarely handled often don't need it (Wikipedia). Not for parts too thin to hold a hole without breaking through. Not for wooden furniture and boxes: use Dowel and biscuit joints. Not when the part must come apart again: use a magnet joint or an unglued locating pin. Not for loads that matter (shelves, anything that could fall on someone): use proper fasteners. Not for steel pins that must be pressed in hard: see Drive-in fixing pins.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - glue or bond: it is a way of making a small glued joint much stronger
  - join and hold: the pin holds the parts together against knocks and sideways loads
  - line parts up exactly: matching holes put a part back in the same place, and pins pushed into a cork or base hold a miniature while it is painted (Bolter and Chainsword)
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): only the unglued toothpick version in foam board, which foldnfly says can make 'joints that can freely pivot or swivel'
- **Movement:** none (glued); a single unglued toothpick or skewer pin in foam can act as a pivot (rotate)
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 once glued. A single round pin before gluing leaves 2 (turning around the pin and sliding along it); a second pin, or a shaped joint face, removes the turning (Bolter and Chainsword)
- **Special behaviour:**
  - self-aligning or self-centring: once the holes are matched, the pin puts the part back in exactly the same place every time you dry-fit it
  - none of the others: it has no lock, release, click or spring action
- **How it holds:**
  - [material bond](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond) (glue, solvent or melting): superglue, epoxy or epoxy putty glues the pin into both holes and the faces together
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the pin sitting in both holes stops the parts sliding sideways or snapping off even if the face glue lets go
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): toothpicks and skewers pushed tightly into foam, and temporary dressmaker's pins or T-pins, hold by friction only
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** light: for miniatures, models, figures, props and foam models handled by hand, not for anything structural. Strong against sideways knocks and bending across the joint (the pin carries them) and against twisting when two pins are used; weakest in straight pull-out, which only the glue along the pin resists. In soft foam the weak point is the foam around the pin, which crushes and tears (Flite Test comments). No published strength figures were found ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Play and precision:** small: the hole is drilled 0.25 to 0.5 mm larger than the pin to leave room for glue and for small [misalignment](../glossary.md#misalignment) (Bolter and Chainsword), and the glue or epoxy putty fills the slop; Starship Modeler forum members deliberately drill one hole oversize so the two holes need not line up exactly. Locating [accuracy](../glossary.md#repeatability--accuracy) depends on how well the second hole is marked
- **Taking it apart:** permanent once glued (the pin can only be got out by cutting or drilling it out); temporary pins in foam board and dry-fitted pins in a painting cork are removable
- **How often it can be reused:** once for glued pins; many times for temporary pins and unglued push-in pins in foam (but foam holes wear bigger each time; Flite Test comments)
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** no once glued; the pin can be bent slightly to fine-tune the pose before the second side is glued (Spikey Bits; Bolter and Chainsword)
- **Access needed:** both sides (the two faces being joined must be reachable to drill before assembly); one side only when the joint is glued first and then drilled through from the most reachable side and pinned (Starship Modeler forum)
- **Materials:** Pins: brass rod (won't rust, easy to cut and file; Bolter and Chainsword), steel paper clips (harder on cutters, can rust over time; Bolter and Chainsword), garden wire (Tabletop Gaming), carbon-fibre rod (Starship Modeler), round toothpicks, bamboo BBQ skewers. Parts it suits: metal (pewter) and plastic miniatures, resin kits and resin 3D prints, FDM prints, baked or raw polymer clay, air-dry clay, foam board, Depron and EPO foam planes, balsa. Glue: superglue (cyanoacrylate) for metal, plastic and resin; slow epoxy or epoxy putty for larger or loose-fitting parts; hot glue or foam-safe glue in foam, because ordinary superglue can melt foam (foldnfly). Not suited to: wood furniture (use dowel joints), parts too thin to hold a hole, or anything that must come apart again
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Limits are those of the glue: superglue is brittle and 'not recommended on parts that will flex or twist under load' (Motion RC), so pinned superglue joints still crack if bent; hot glue in foam softens in heat (see Hot-melt adhesive). Steel paper-clip pins can rust after a long time (Bolter and Chainsword); brass does not. In polymer clay, wood (toothpicks, skewers) holds moisture that can expand in the oven and crack the piece, so Sculpey's blog advises drying wooden parts in the oven first; metal does not have this problem (Sculpey blog). Foam holes wear larger with use and after hard landings (Flite Test comments).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: brass rod, piano wire or carbon rod as pin stock; pin vice and micro drill bits
  - 3D print (resin): pinning large or heavy resin-printed parts and broken resin figures (Starship Modeler forum covers resin kit parts)
  - 3D print (FDM): drill into printed parts, or design pin holes into the cut faces (see Split-part joints for large prints)
  - woodwork: balsa model parts and hinge pinning with toothpicks (Airfield Models)
  - hand work in foam board, EPO and Depron foam and polymer clay (no machine needed)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the pin is a separate part pushed or glued in after printing; designed-in holes can be printed so that only the pin is added ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Tools:** a pin vice (hand drill) with small drill bits (1.0 mm is the common hobby size; Tabletop Gaming), side cutters to clip wire, a file to roughen the pin, superglue or slow epoxy, epoxy putty for gaps, paint or Blu Tack to mark the second hole; for foam just a toothpick or skewer, a knife and hot glue or foam-safe glue; safety glasses when clipping wire (CCOHS)
- **Difficulty:** easy to moderate: easy in foam (push a toothpick in) and in raw clay; moderate in metal, resin and small plastic parts, where the hard part is marking and drilling the second hole in line with the first (Tabletop Gaming; Starship Modeler: 'The marking & drilling of holes is about the toughest part')

### 3D printing tips

1) For prints you design yourself, put the pin holes into the CAD model on both faces so they line up exactly; this is the 'pegs and holes' or 'loose pin' method covered in Split-part joints for large prints (item 62). 2) Small printed holes usually come out undersize, so print them slightly small and open them to size with a drill bit in a pin vice afterwards ⚑ [uncertain]. 3) For already-printed or broken parts, drill with a pin vice, not a power drill: the part can't be held safely under a power drill (CCOHS) and a fast drill wanders and breaks through thin walls (Airfield Models, about balsa). 4) Resin prints are brittle: use a pin of 1 mm or less in small parts, drill slowly, and back the bit out often to clear the swarf (Bolter and Chainsword recommends ground, not rolled, bits for small holes). 5) Hollow resin prints have thin walls, so pin where the wall is solid or fill the cavity with epoxy putty first, the way Bolter and Chainsword fills bases with Milliput before drilling. 6) Make holes 0.25 to 0.5 mm larger than the pin and glue with superglue for a snug fit or slow epoxy or epoxy putty for a loose one (Bolter and Chainsword; Starship Modeler). 7) Roughen the pin with a file for extra grip (Bolter and Chainsword).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Brass rod (common hobby sizes 0.020 in / 0.51 mm and 0.032 in / 0.81 mm; Bolter and Chainsword), 'pinning rod' or 'pinning wire' sold for miniatures, piano wire, carbon-fibre rod, round toothpicks, bamboo BBQ skewers, dressmaker's pins and T-pins (temporary), pin vice (pin vise) hand drill with [collets](../glossary.md#collet), ground HSS or cobalt micro drill bits (0.7 mm and 1.0 mm are the most used; Bolter and Chainsword), side cutters, superglue (cyanoacrylate), slow-setting two-part epoxy, epoxy putty (e.g. Milliput or 'green stuff', brands), Blu Tack (brand) for marking holes, silicone fuel tubing to pad T-pins (Model Airplane News)

## Rules of thumb

- Drill the hole 0.25 to 0.5 mm larger than the pin to leave room for glue; e.g. 0.8 mm brass rod in a 1.0 mm hole (Bolter and Chainsword forum)
- 1.0 mm is the standard bit that most hobby drills come with and suits garden wire pins in metal miniatures (Tabletop Gaming)
- Each hole should be at least half the length of the pin deep, i.e. the pin is fully buried (Starship Modeler forum); about 1/4 in (6 mm) deep is fine for typical resin model parts (Starship Modeler forum)
- Up to a point, longer pins are stronger; about 20 mm of pin for a trooper on its base, trimmed to 5 to 10 mm showing below the feet when fitted (Bolter and Chainsword forum)
- Use two pins (or two thin pins in one slightly larger hole) to stop a part with one small contact point loosening or spinning (Bolter and Chainsword forum)
- Dry-fit and cut the pin to length before any glue goes on; cut it too long rather than too short (Tabletop Gaming)
- For a pinned hinge in balsa: drill 3/32 in (2.4 mm) through surface and hinge, push in a round toothpick, trim flush, add a drop of thin CA (Airfield Models)
- In foam, spread the load where a pin goes through a wall that is pulled on: glue a popsicle stick or an old gift card with a hole in it over the skewer hole, or back it with packing tape (Flite Test Old Fogey comments); a thin plywood washer does the same ⚑ [uncertain]

## Common failures

1) Holes don't line up, so the part sits crooked: mark the second hole by touching a blob of paint on the pin end (Tabletop Gaming), a ring of thick paint around the first hole (Spikey Bits), Blu Tack (Wikipedia) or a sharpened pin tip (Starship Modeler), or drill one hole oversize and fill it with epoxy putty (Starship Modeler). 2) Superglue grabs before the pin is fully in: dry-fit first and push it home quickly (Tabletop Gaming). 3) Drill breaks or wanders: use ground (not rolled) bits and a pin vice, and drill straight (Bolter and Chainsword; Spikey Bits). 4) Drilling right through a thin part: mark the depth and stop early (Spikey Bits). 5) Glue squeezed out of the hole stops the parts closing flush: lightly [countersink](../glossary.md#counterbore--countersink) the hole edge (Bolter and Chainsword). 6) Pin pulls out of a smooth hole: roughen the pin with a file (Bolter and Chainsword). 7) Part spins on a single pin: add a second pin (Bolter and Chainsword). 8) Skewer or toothpick holes in foam tear out: spread the load with tape, card or a popsicle stick (Flite Test comments). 9) Toothpick in raw clay leaves a weak, crumbly joint or cracks: bake the body first, then add the head on the baked pin and smooth it in (Ann's Clay How To page), or use wire instead.

## Safety

Drill small parts with a hand pin vice, not by holding the part under a power drill: CCOHS says 'Never drill with one hand while holding the material with the other' and to clamp small stock so it can't spin. Wear safety glasses when clipping wire, because cut ends fly (CCOHS: eye protection 'whenever there is a potential hazard from flying particles, pieces of wire'), and don't cut hardened wire (e.g. piano wire) with ordinary cutters (CCOHS). Superglue bonds skin. Cut skewer and toothpick ends flush or cap them so no sharp point sticks out. Temporary dressmaker's pins and T-pins are sharp: count them out and back in. Keep cut wire offcuts away from children and pets.

## Related

- Dowel and biscuit joints: wooden pegs or biscuits in sized holes in woodwork; pinning is the same idea scaled down for models, using wire and glue
- Locating pins and hole-and-slot: precise pins for exact, repeatable location in machines, usually without glue
- Split-part joints for large prints: pegs, dowels and puzzle joints designed into the cut faces of a 3D print
- Drive-in fixing pins (roll pin, spring pin, dowel pin): steel pins held by a press or spring fit, not glue
- Epoxy putty: the filler used to fill gaps and oversize holes around pins
- Cyanoacrylate (superglue): the usual glue for pinning metal and resin miniatures
- Wire and foil armature: a full wire skeleton inside a clay figure, rather than a short pin across one joint
- Magnet joint: lets a pinned-on part come off again (swappable weapons on miniatures)
- Slot-in model hinge: 'pinning a hinge' with a toothpick through the hinge leaf is the same trick
