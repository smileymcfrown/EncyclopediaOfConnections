A screw, bolt or nut with an unusual drive (a pin in the socket, odd lobes, two holes, an odd number of sides), a slot that only grips one way, or a head that snaps off when tight, so that ordinary tools can't undo it and casual tampering with covers, battery boxes, signs and public fittings is stopped or slowed.

**In plain words:** Screws, bolts and nuts with odd-shaped heads that a normal screwdriver or spanner won't turn, or that can only be screwed in and never back out, or whose head snaps off once tight, so passers-by and children can't easily open a cover, battery door, sign or public fitting. **Proper term:** Tamper-resistant fastener (security screw; security fastener; anti-tamper fastener).

## How it works

There are three tricks. 1) An unusual drive shape that common tools don't fit: a pin in the middle of a hex or star socket stops a normal bit going in, two small holes need a two-pin bit, and an odd number of sides (pentagon) can't be gripped by spanners, which need parallel faces, or by six- and twelve-point sockets (Wikipedia 'List of screw drives'). 2) A one-way slot: its sides are ramps, so a flat screwdriver grips when turning in but slides up the ramps and slips out (cams out) when turning to undo (Locksmith Ledger; Wikipedia). 3) A breakaway head: an ordinary hex is joined to a smooth domed or cone-shaped head by a deliberately weak neck, and when the tightening torque reaches the design point the hex shears off, leaving nothing to grip (Wikipedia; PTS). Types 1 rely on few people owning the right bit, so they only deter casual tampering; types 2 and 3 are meant to be permanent (Wikipedia; Fastener Engineering).

## Parts

Single piece plus a matching tool: a screw, bolt or nut with a special drive, and the matching driver bit or socket (for pin-in drives the bit has a hole down its centre) (Wikipedia 'List of screw drives'; Locksmith Ledger). A shear nut is one piece made of a smooth cone, a thin weak neck and an ordinary hex collar that breaks off (PTS; Albany County Fasteners). One-way screws and shear nuts need only ordinary tools to fit (Insight Security; PTS).

## Everyday examples

- One-way slotted screws on public-toilet fittings and vehicle number plates (Wikipedia; Commons photo of a one-way screw 'as found on a bathroom stall')
- Breakaway-head bolts on street signs, prison door locks and car ignition switches (Wikipedia)
- Pentalobe screws on iPhones and MacBooks, and 'Gamebit' or tri-wing screws on Nintendo consoles and game cartridges (Wikipedia)
- Pentagon-headed bolts on fire hydrants, water-meter covers and gas valves, and one locking nut with a special key on each car wheel (Wikipedia 'List of screw drives'; Wikipedia 'Lug nut')

## When to use it

Use one where an ordinary screw would do the job but you want to stop casual or accidental opening: covers over mains or hot parts in places the public can reach (Wikipedia 'Tamperproofing'), signs, bus shelters, playground equipment, shed hasps, outdoor lights and cameras (Insight Security), and equipment panels that only maintenance staff should open (Insight Security). Pick a removable special drive (pin Torx, pin hex, two-hole) where authorised people must get in again, and a permanent one (one-way screw, shear nut, breakaway bolt) for fixings that should never come off, such as number plates and signs (PTS; Wikipedia). In maker projects they suit a printed enclosure for a public installation or a shared workshop machine, and a battery box in something children handle, though there an ordinary captive screw is often enough (see rules_of_thumb).

## When not to use it

Not as real security: bits are easy to buy, a printed bit can turn low-torque screws (Printables 33382), and Locksmith Ledger removed one-way, spanner and pin-hex screws with only a flat screwdriver, a small hammer and pliers. For valuables use a key lock (Pin-tumbler lock) or an alarmed or monitored enclosure; Locksmith Ledger's example is a door keypad that switches the lock power itself, so anyone who unscrews it and pulls its wires opens the door: 'a couple of fancy screws can only do so much'. Not where the item must be opened in an emergency or serviced often by people who won't have the bit; use a quarter-turn cam latch or captive thumbscrew instead. Don't use permanent types (one-way, shear nut, breakaway) on anything that may need repair, battery changes or recycling; the Oral-B TP3 example shows a maker leaving a way to open a product at end of life (Wikipedia). For a child-resistant battery box, an ordinary or coin-slot screw that stays captive in the lid is usually enough, and is easier for the owner (Bureau Veritas; QIMA). In thin or soft printed plastic without inserts, any screw will strip, security or not (CPSC letter).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: holds a cover, panel, battery door, sign or fitting like an ordinary screw (Wikipedia 'Tamperproofing')
  - lock, and release by hand: works like a simple lock whose 'key' is the matching driver bit; only people with the bit can open it, and the permanent types (one-way, breakaway, shear nut) can't be undone without cutting or drilling (Fastener Engineering; Wikipedia)
- **Movement:** none: once tightened nothing moves.
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: it clamps the parts together like any screw joint.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - one-way (moves freely one way, locks the other): a one-way screw can be driven in with a flat screwdriver but the driver slips out when turned to undo it (Wikipedia; Locksmith Ledger). The screw itself turns both ways; it is the drive that only grips one way
  - torque-limiting (slips or clicks at a set force/torque): a breakaway head or shear nut snaps off at roughly a set tightening torque, and non-security breakaway nuts (e.g. some toilet-seat bolts) use this as a crude torque limiter; it is less precise than a torque wrench (Wikipedia 'List of screw drives')
  - none for pin-in, multi-lobe, two-hole and odd-sided drives: they hold like ordinary screws and work only because the matching tool is uncommon (Wikipedia)
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the tightened screw squeezes the parts together, exactly like an ordinary screw joint
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the thread flanks lock screw and nut or insert together; the special head only controls who can turn it
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed).
- **Load:** Light to heavy, the same as an ordinary screw, bolt or nut of the same size and material, because the special shape is only in the drive ⚑ [uncertain: no opened source compares strengths directly]. Strong in tension and in shear once tightened. The weak points are the drive (a hollow security bit or a shallow one-way slot can only pass limited torque, so these screws can't always be tightened as hard as normal ones ⚑ [uncertain]) and, for shear nuts, the fixed shear torque: too low a shear torque leaves the joint under-clamped, too high can over-stress the bolt or the part before the cap breaks off (PTS).
- **Play and precision:** not applicable: it clamps like an ordinary screw and doesn't locate parts.
- **Taking it apart:** Depends on type. removable (with the matching bit): pin-in Torx, pin hex, 5-lobe, two-hole, tri-wing, pentalobe and pentagon drives come out normally with the right tool (Insight Security). permanent: one-way screws, breakaway-head bolts and shear nuts are 'relevant only when the need for removal is unlikely' and come out only by drilling, cutting a new slot, gripping with locking pliers or using a screw extractor (Wikipedia; Albany County Fasteners; PTS).
- **How often it can be reused:** many times for pin-in and other special drives used with the correct bit (Insight Security); once for shear nuts and breakaway heads (the hex end is a throw-away part; Albany County Fasteners); once for one-way screws if you want tamper evidence, because removal gouges the ramps, so Locksmith Ledger advises fitting a new one-way screw every time one is removed.
- **Sealing:** none: the fastener itself doesn't seal; seal the cover with a gasket if needed.
- **Adjustable:** no: one-way, breakaway and shear-nut fixings can't be re-tightened or adjusted after fitting (Wikipedia; PTS); pin-in and other special drives can be re-tightened with the matching bit.
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind) when a security screw goes into a tapped hole, threaded insert or captive nut; both sides for a shear nut on a bolt or stud, because you hold the bolt with one spanner while turning the nut with another (Albany County Fasteners).
- **Materials:** Mostly stainless steel, A2 (304) or A4 (316); PTS recommends A4 'outdoors or in exposed locations' for shear nuts, since corrosion of the thin neck changes its shear behaviour (PTS). Plated steel and plastic versions exist (Wikipedia mentions a plastic breakaway nut on toilet seats). Security screws come in the same thread forms as ordinary ones: machine screws for nuts and inserts, and self-tapping and wood-screw forms ⚑ [uncertain: catalogue ranges not checked]. They suit any material an ordinary screw suits; in 3D prints use them with threaded inserts so the plastic threads don't wear or strip (see making_tips_3d_printing).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Outdoors and near the sea use A4 (316) stainless: PTS says A4 is the safer choice for shear nuts 'outdoors or in exposed locations', because corrosion of the thin neck could change when it shears (PTS). The small recesses of security drives can fill with dirt or paint and need cleaning before a bit will seat ⚑ [uncertain: no source opened]. Vibration loosens security screws like any screw; use a threadlocker or locking hardware where needed (see related). Printed covers: plastic battery compartments get warm in use, and CPSC staff asked for heat conditioning in tests (CPSC letter); PLA parts slowly 'flow' (creep) under constant load, which can loosen a clamped cover over time (maker note, Printables 415716).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: security screws, nuts and bits are bought
  - 3D print (FDM) and 3D print (resin): the cover, screw bosses, recesses and insert holes designed around them
  - CNC, sheet metal, woodwork: enclosures, sign plates and public fittings fixed with them
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the fasteners are metal parts that are bought; only the parts they hold are printed.
- **Tools:** The matching security bit or driver: Torx TR or hex bit with a centre hole, 5-lobe bit, spanner (two-pin) bit, tri-wing, tri-point, pentalobe, line-head or pentagon bit (Wikipedia; Insight Security); keyed proprietary drives need the maker's own driver (Wikipedia). One-way screws: an ordinary flat screwdriver to fit, a one-way removal tool to undo (Insight Security). Shear nuts and breakaway bolts: an ordinary socket or spanner to fit, plus a second spanner to hold the bolt (Albany County Fasteners; PTS); removal needs a drill, grinder or cutter (PTS). Security bit sets containing most common types are widely sold (Wikipedia; Commons 'Set of security screw driver bits').
- **Difficulty:** easy: buy the screws and a matching bit and fit them like ordinary screws. Making a genuinely secure drive yourself is hard, because a 3D-printed bit can copy most drives well enough to turn low-torque screws (Printables 33382).

### 3D printing tips

1) Use metal threads: a cover that authorised people open repeatedly will wear or strip screw threads cut straight into plastic; the US product-safety regulator (CPSC) criticised the old toy standard because 'users could easily lose or strip the screws securing the battery compartment' (CPSC letter to ASTM, 2023). Fit heat-set or press-in brass inserts and use machine-thread security screws (see Heat-set threaded insert). 2) Make the screw captive in the lid, so it stays attached when the cover is off: current toy rules require battery-door fasteners to 'remain attached to the toy or battery compartment cover before and after abuse tests' (QIMA; Bureau Veritas), and EU machinery rules say the same for fixed guards (CECE guidance on Directive 2006/42/EC). A captive washer on the screw shank is one common way (Wikipedia 'Captive fastener'). 3) Recess the head: exposed pin-hex button heads were unscrewed with ordinary pliers in Locksmith Ledger's test, so sink the head in a counterbore only slightly wider than the bit, or use a countersunk head, so there is nothing to grip ⚑ [uncertain: the recess advice is our inference from that test]. 4) Don't rely on a printed custom drive for security: a maker printed a PLA Gamebit driver that was strong enough to undo and refit two security screws (Printables 33382), so anyone with a printer can copy an odd shape; on the other hand a printed 'tamper-proof' knob that needs a screwdriver is a good way to stop accidental adjustment (Printables 1044952). 5) A freely spinning cover is another printable way to stop something being gripped and turned, like the spinning outer ring on some locking wheel nuts (Wikipedia 'Lug nut'): one printed outdoor-tap lockout spins freely over the handle and needs a printed key (Printables 1754160). 6) For child-resistant battery boxes, a security drive is usually unnecessary: an ordinary screw opened with a screwdriver or coin meets the 'common household tool' requirement (Bureau Veritas), and a special drive is allowed in toys only if the tool comes with the toy (QIMA). 7) Battery heat: CPSC staff asked for plastic battery compartments to be tested after conditioning 'at a temperature simulating battery operation' (CPSC letter), so prefer PETG, ABS or ASA to PLA for battery boxes that get warm ⚑ [uncertain: no source gives a PLA limit for this use].

## Rules of thumb

- Match the type to the threat. Pin-in, multi-lobe, two-hole and other special drives only stop people who don't own the bit; bits for most security types are widely sold, so they are 'secure only against casual adversaries' (Wikipedia 'List of screw drives'); they 'impede opportunistic breaches more so than deliberate intentions' (Fastener Engineering). For anything worth a determined attack, use a real lock (Locksmith Ledger).
- Use the permanent types (one-way screws, breakaway heads, shear nuts) only where the fixing should never come off again (Wikipedia; Albany County Fasteners: 'only use them where you never intend on removing them again').
- Shear nuts: hand-tighten, then hold the bolt with one spanner and turn the nut with another until the hex snaps off; put a washer under the nut on soft material (Albany County Fasteners). Choose a nut whose shear torque suits the bolt size and the clamping the joint needs, not just one with the right thread (PTS).
- Prefer drives that common tools can't improvise: a tri-angle (TA) recess 'can readily be driven with hex keys', whereas TP3 can't be driven by a flat blade or readily by hex keys (Wikipedia). Pentagon heads defeat spanners and sockets but can still be turned with pliers 'if enough force is applied' (Wikipedia).
- Sources disagree on pin-in Torx: Wikipedia's 'Torx' article says the centre pin 'allows a smaller flat head screw driver to be used successfully between two adjacent lobes', while 'List of screw drives' says the pin 'can also prevent attempts at turning the screw with a small flat-bladed screwdriver'. Treat pin-in drives as low security either way.
- Treat tamper-resistant screws as tamper-evident too: one-way, spanner and pin-hex screws removed with ordinary tools leave gouges or plier marks, so inspect them regularly and fit a new one-way screw each time one is removed (Locksmith Ledger).
- Battery boxes for button or coin cells (USA): compartments 'must be secured such that they require the use of a tool or at least two independent and simultaneous hand movements to open' (CPSC FAQ on 16 CFR 1263 / ANSI/UL 4200A-2023). Toys under 14 meet the toy standard instead (CPSC FAQ).
- Toys (ASTM F963-23, mandatory in the USA for toys made from 20 April 2024): batteries that fit in the small-parts cylinder must not be reachable without a 'common household tool', defined as a 'screwdriver (straight-blade or Phillips-type), pliers, coin, or other object commonly found in most households'; battery-door fasteners must stay attached; and a specialty fastener is allowed only if the right tool and instructions come with the toy (Bureau Veritas bulletin 24B-015; QIMA).
- Machine guards (EU): fixed guards 'must be fixed by systems that can be opened or removed only with tools', their fixings 'must remain attached', and quick-release fixings such as wing nuts 'must not be used'; ordinary bolts or screws that need keys or wrenches are acceptable (Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC Annex I 1.4.2.1 and the Commission guide §218, quoted by CECE). So a tool-only, captive fixing is the requirement, not a security drive.
- Keep the matching bits wherever authorised people will need them; losing the only bit locks you out as surely as a lost key (Insight Security).

## Common failures

1) Defeated with ordinary tools: one-way screws undone by pushing hard while turning anticlockwise (or tapping the screwdriver handle), spanner screws tapped round with a screwdriver and hammer, pin-hex button heads turned with pliers (Locksmith Ledger); pentagon heads gripped with pliers (Wikipedia). Avoid by recessing heads, using cone-shaped nuts that pliers can't hold (Fastener Engineering; Albany County Fasteners) and inspecting for marks. 2) Pin snapped off, after which a normal bit fits (Insight Security). 3) Wrong bit size: tri-angle bit sizes 'marked on the tool' are often wrong and differ between makers, so the bit slips and rounds the recess (Wikipedia); try the next size and press firmly. 4) Breakaway or shear head breaking too early (joint under-clamped) or needing so much torque that it damages the part (Wikipedia; PTS); choose the right size and grade and use a washer on soft materials (Albany County Fasteners). 5) One-way screw put in the wrong place: it can't simply be backed out; it must be drilled and extracted, or slotted with a rotary tool, or gripped with locking pliers (Wikipedia). 6) Lost bit, so the owner is locked out (Insight Security). 7) Battery-door screws lost or threads stripped in plastic (CPSC letter): make them captive and use inserts. 8) The same one-way screws reused after unauthorised removal, so the tamper marks no longer mean anything (Locksmith Ledger).

## Safety

Button and coin cells are a serious swallowing hazard for children; in the USA products with replaceable button or coin cells must have compartments that need a tool or two simultaneous hand movements to open (CPSC FAQ, 16 CFR 1263), and toys must meet ASTM F963-23 (QIMA; Bureau Veritas). A lost or stripped battery-door screw leaves batteries exposed, which is why CPSC pushed for captive screws (CPSC letter to ASTM, 2023). Tamper-resistant screws are used on electrical fittings in public buildings 'to reduce tampering or vandalism that may cause a danger to others' (Wikipedia 'Tamperproofing'), but they are not a substitute for a properly designed and approved enclosure: this entry gives no mains wiring advice, and mains work must follow local law. Machine guards must be fixed so they open only with tools and keep their fixings attached (CECE guidance on Directive 2006/42/EC). Removing permanent fasteners means drilling, grinding or cutting a slot with a rotary tool (Wikipedia; Insight Security): wear eye protection and keep hands clear of the disc ⚑ [uncertain: general workshop practice, not from an opened source]. Don't use permanent fixings on anything that may need emergency access.

## Related

- Ordinary screw joint (bolt and nut, machine screw): the same joint with a common drive; a security screw simply replaces it
- Pin-tumbler lock and other tumbler locks: a real key lock, for when deterrence isn't enough
- Cam latch (quarter-turn cam lock): a keyed or tool-operated latch for panels that are opened often; quicker to open than screws
- Hidden latches (magnetic-key latch, puzzle lock): hide the fastening instead of making it hard to turn
- Tamper-evident breakaway bridge (frangible link): shows that something was opened rather than resisting opening; tamper-resistant screws give some evidence too (gouges)
- Mechanical interlock: mentions one-way and security screws as a way to make interlocks hard to defeat
- Shear-pin coupling (breakaway link): also has a deliberately weak section, but it breaks in service to protect a machine, not once during fitting
- Blind rivet (pop rivet): another permanent, one-side fixing that can only be drilled out
- Heat-set threaded insert: gives a printed part metal threads, so security machine screws can be removed and refitted without stripping
- Threadlocking hardware (nylon-insert nut, lock washer, split pin) and Anaerobic adhesive (threadlocker): stop screws loosening by vibration, not by people
- Tool-free fasteners (thumbscrew, wing nut, captive panel screw): the opposite aim; captive panel screws show how to keep a screw attached to a lid
- Battery holder and battery contacts: where child-resistant covers are needed
