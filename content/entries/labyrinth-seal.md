Two parts shaped so the gap between them doubles back on itself several times, so dust, grit and splashes run out of energy or drain away before they reach the inside, with no rubbing contact and nothing to wear out.

**In plain words:** An overlapping, zig-zag gap between two parts, such as a lid whose skirt hangs down over a raised rim, or nested rings round a spinning shaft, that makes dust and splashes follow a long winding path so they can't get in, even though the parts never touch. **Proper term:** Labyrinth seal (non-contact seal).

**Also called:** labyrinth seal, non-contact seal, gap seal, mini maze seal (GMN Bearing USA); bearing isolator (a labyrinth device fitted at a bearing housing in place of a lip seal; Machinery [Lubrication](../glossary.md#lubrication)); rotor and stator labyrinth; teeth on rotor (TOR) or teeth on stator (TOS) (Wikipedia 'Labyrinth seal'; Machinery Lubrication); lip-and-groove joint or tongue-and-groove joint (the static version between two halves of a plastic housing; RJC Mold); baffle seal, baffle vent or labyrinth vent (a winding air path that lets air through but slows water and dust) ⚑ [uncertain]; louvres (angled slats that let air through but keep out rain, as on a Stevenson screen; Wikipedia 'Stevenson screen'); overlapping lid skirt, drip lip or rain lip ⚑ [uncertain].

## How it works

Instead of pressing rubber against a surface, the two parts are shaped so the narrow gap between them turns back and forth several times, and each turn makes dust and droplets lose their momentum (GMN Bearing USA; Machinery Lubrication). On a spinning shaft the path runs inward, so spinning (centrifugal force) throws dirt and liquid back outward, away from the bearing (Wikipedia 'Labyrinth seal'; GMN). On a still lid or hub, the openings face downward so gravity drains any water back out, like two C-shaped channels nested one inside the other (Jobst Brandt). Because the parts never touch there is no friction and no wear, but the path is always open, so it only slows leakage: water standing against it, a water jet into the gap or a pressure difference will push through (Wikipedia; GMN).

## Parts

Two parts, no extra pieces: ribs, grooves, skirts or nested rings shaped into a stationary part (the stator, or a box) and a moving part (the rotor on a shaft, or a lid) (Machinery Lubrication; GMN Bearing USA). Optional: grease packed into the gaps, and a drain hole at the lowest point.

## Everyday examples

- The hubs of old Sturmey-Archer 3-speed bicycles and some [freewheels](../glossary.md#freewheel) and pedals, which stay clean and dry inside for decades with only labyrinth seals (Jobst Brandt)
- The white slatted box (Stevenson screen) that holds a weather station's thermometer, keeping out rain and sun but letting air through (Wikipedia 'Stevenson screen')
- The winding air channels moulded into the top of an inkjet ink tank, which let air in while slowing evaporation (Wikimedia Commons photo)
- Steam turbine and gas turbine shafts, and bearing isolators on pumps and electric motors (Wikipedia; Wikimedia Commons; Machinery Lubrication)

## When to use it

Use a labyrinth when you want to keep dust, grit, rain and splashes out (or grease in) without any rubber that wears, drags or ages: around spinning shafts in fans, wheels, turntables, hubs and robot joints, where a rubbing seal would add friction or limit speed; on printed lids and boxes that are opened often or must breathe; as a first barrier to shield a gasket from direct spray; and in outdoor sensor shelters that need airflow. It is free to add to a printed part and never needs replacing.

## When not to use it

Don't use a labyrinth alone for anything that will be submerged, hosed at close range, sealed against pressure or vacuum, or that holds a standing liquid (GMN); use an [O-ring](../glossary.md#o-ring) or gasket instead, perhaps behind a labyrinth. For an oil bath on a shaft, a contact lip seal or an O-ring is needed ⚑ [uncertain]. It is also a poor choice where the parts can't be kept from rubbing (loose or badly printed shafts), or where a vertical opening faces up into the rain.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - seal it, or pass cables or fluid through: keeps dust, grit, rain and splashes out of a box, lid or bearing, and grease or oil in, without touching
  - line parts up exactly: a lip-and-groove version on a box also aligns the two halves (RJC Mold 'Lip and Groove Design')
- **Movement:** none for a box or lid joint; rotate (unlimited, at any speed) for a shaft labyrinth, since the parts never touch; GMN's only speed limit is the seal lifting off the shaft at very high speed (GMN Bearing USA).
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 for a closed box. 1 on a shaft (it turns freely), but the labyrinth does not guide the shaft: bearings must hold it true, and it only tolerates end float and wobble smaller than its gap (about 0.5 mm [axial](../glossary.md#axial--radial) and radial in GMN's plastic gap seals).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it does none of the listed clever things; its special feature is that it seals without contact, so it has no friction, no power loss, no wear and effectively unlimited life (GMN Bearing USA; Machinery Lubrication)
- **How it holds:**
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): it does not hold anything together; screws, clips, a thread or bearings hold the parts, and the labyrinth only shapes the gap between them
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** none: it carries no load and must not be used to locate or support the parts; a shaft labyrinth needs its own bearings.
- **Play and precision:** The gap must stay open all the way round: GMN's plastic gap seals use about 0.5 mm axial and radial [clearance](../glossary.md#clearance), and machined bearing isolators can run with gaps as small as 0.003 in (76 micrometres) (GMN; Machinery Lubrication). Any wobble (run-out) or sag bigger than the gap makes the parts rub ⚑ [uncertain]. A gap that is too small acts like a capillary tube and can wick water in (Jobst Brandt). A lip-and-groove on a box also helps line the halves up (RJC Mold).
- **Taking it apart:** removable: it is simply the shape of the two parts, so it comes apart whenever they do; press-fitted shaft labyrinths come off with the shaft (GMN).
- **How often it can be reused:** effectively unlimited: the parts never touch, so nothing wears (GMN 'unlimited life'; Machinery Lubrication). A grease-packed labyrinth needs repacking now and then because rotation slowly moves grease out (GMN).
- **Sealing:** dust-resistant: keeps out dust, grit and splashing water, and drains rain if its openings face down (Jobst Brandt; GMN), but it is not watertight under water, against water jets aimed into the gap, or against any pressure difference (GMN; Wikipedia). Moulded ABS instrument boxes with a tongue-and-groove joint are sold with an IP54 rating (dust protected and splash-proof) (Bud Industries PI series listing; Wikipedia 'IP code') (whether those boxes rely on the joint alone is not stated) ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Adjustable:** no: the gap is fixed by the shape of the parts.
- **Access needed:** not applicable for a box joint; end of a tube or shaft for a shaft labyrinth, which is slid or pressed on from the shaft end (GMN; split designs can be fitted round a shaft without removing it, Printables 'Split Labyrinth Seal').
- **Materials:** Any rigid material: GMN makes its gap seals from acetal (POM) plastic, which resists oils, greases, many solvents and water but not strong acids; others are bronze, stainless steel or plastic (GMN; Machinery Lubrication). 'Plain old plastic will suffice for a labyrinth seal because there is no contact' (Jobst Brandt). For printing: PLA indoors; PETG or ASA outdoors or near heat (CNC Kitchen heat tests); ABS or another higher-temperature material near hot machinery (Printables 'Split Labyrinth Seal').
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** There is no rubber to crack from ozone or sunlight, so the limits are those of the plastic or metal: printed PLA softens from about 60 °C, PETG about 80 °C and ASA about 110 °C (CNC Kitchen). A gap that is too fine wicks water in by capillary action in heavy rain (Jobst Brandt). Temperature changes make an enclosure breathe, pulling in moist air and pooled water as it cools (Stockwell), so condensation can still form inside. Grease in a labyrinth is slowly flung out by rotation (GMN). Fine dust eventually works through unless the path is long or grease-filled ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - 3D print (FDM): ribs, skirts and nested rings print as part of lids, hubs and housings, usually without supports if printed upside down (Printables 'Helical rain/radiation shield', 'Compact Stevenson Screen')
  - 3D print (resin): finer gaps and smoother walls ⚑ [uncertain]
  - CNC (lathe): the classic way for metal shaft labyrinths and bearing isolators (Machinery Lubrication)
  - injection moulding: lip-and-groove joints on plastic enclosures (RJC Mold)
  - off-the-shelf part: plastic gap seals and labyrinth seals for bearings (GMN Bearing USA), bearing isolators (Machinery Lubrication)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the labyrinth is two separate printed parts (box and lid, or rotor and stator) that are assembled, but each part carries its own ribs or grooves, so no extra seal, spring or rubber is needed.
- **Tools:** none for the labyrinth itself, only whatever holds the parts together; a grease gun or a finger of grease if it is grease-packed
- **Difficulty:** easy: it is just ribs, skirts or grooves printed into the lid, box or hub, with no added parts; the design skill is in drainage, gap size and keeping the parts from rubbing

### 3D printing tips

1) Leave a real gap between the interleaved walls: about 0.5 mm like GMN's plastic gap seals is a sensible starting point, and bigger is safer for FDM, because a too-narrow gap acts as a capillary that sucks water in (Jobst Brandt) and printed rings are rarely perfectly round, so they may rub (exact FDM value not verified) ⚑ [uncertain]. 2) Make every opening face downward and give water a way out: a lid skirt that hangs outside and below the rim, drain slots or a drainage channel at the lowest point (Jobst Brandt: labyrinths 'use gravity to purge water'; Printables 'Splash-Proof Switch and Socket Box' uses a drainage channel). 3) Use several turns: two nested C-shaped channels are the minimum; more overlaps slow dust and spray further (Jobst Brandt; Wikipedia 'many grooves'). 4) Print lids and hats upside down so downward skirts, ribs and louvres need no supports (Printables 'Helical rain/radiation shield', 'Compact Stevenson Screen'). 5) On a shaft, make the path lead inward toward the bearing so spinning throws dirt back out, and fit it with the larger labyrinth diameter facing the dirt (GMN). 6) For dusty places, pack the labyrinth with grease (GMN; Printables 'Split Labyrinth Seal' packs its cavity with wheel-bearing grease). 7) Where water must be kept out properly, use the labyrinth as the first barrier in front of a gasket or O-ring: a flange that shields the gasket from direct spray greatly improves wash-down performance (Stockwell 'Enclosure Gaskets'; RJC Mold). 8) Give cable entries a drip shield or drip loop so water running down the wire can't follow it in (Printables 'Helical rain/radiation shield' has a wire pass-through with a drip shield). 9) Let the box breathe through the labyrinth or a vent: a hot box cooled by rain sucks air and water in (Stockwell). 10) Outdoors, print in PETG or ASA rather than PLA, which softens from about 60 °C in the sun (CNC Kitchen).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Search for 'non-contact labyrinth seal', 'labyrinth gap seal' or 'gap seal' for bearings (e.g. GMN S and SA series in POM plastic, brand), 'bearing isolator' (Machinery Lubrication; e.g. Inpro/Seal, brand, cited there), 'labyrinth seal ring' for pillow-block bearing housings ⚑ [uncertain], and 'shielded bearing' (a ball bearing with non-contact metal shields, marked ZZ or 2Z) ⚑ [uncertain]. For boxes: 'IP54 enclosure' with 'tongue and groove' joints (e.g. Bud Industries PI series, brand), 'breather vent' or 'membrane vent' (a microporous membrane that passes air but blocks liquid water; RJC Mold), and 'louvred radiation shield' or 'Stevenson screen' for outdoor sensors.

## Rules of thumb

- Clearance: about 0.5 mm axial and radial in GMN's plastic gap seals; grease-filling increases sealing against dust; fit with the larger labyrinth diameter facing the contaminant (GMN Bearing USA 'Labyrinth Seal Guide').
- Not for pressure or standing liquid: a pressure difference or a standing level of liquid will overcome a labyrinth and leak (GMN).
- Machined bearing isolators run with a rotor-stator gap as small as 0.003 in (about 76 micrometres); the winding path, not a tight gap, does the work (Machinery Lubrication, 2013).
- Water: the gap must be large enough to prevent capillary attraction, and the design must let gravity drain water from its entrance; two nested channel-section washers, one turning inside the other, are enough for a bicycle hub (Jobst Brandt, rec.bicycles.tech).
- Moulded lip-and-groove joints (for injection moulding, not FDM): lip height 0.60-0.80 mm, root width 0.60-0.80 mm, mating side clearance 0.05 mm, vertical clearance 0.10-0.20 mm (RJC Mold). Printed versions need larger clearances ⚑ [uncertain].
- What to expect: dust protected (IP5X) and splashing water from any direction (IPX4) are the realistic targets; immersion (IPX7) needs a gasket or O-ring (Wikipedia 'IP code' for the test definitions; GMN for the pressure limit) (not verified for printed labyrinths) ⚑ [uncertain].
- A door flange that shields the gasket from a direct water blast greatly improves the chance of passing a wash-down test (Stockwell 'Enclosure Gaskets').

## Common failures

Gap too small, so water is drawn in by capillary action (the problem Jobst Brandt saw on SunTour freewheels) or the parts rub and wear. Openings facing up or no drain, so water pools and runs through. Fitted the wrong way round (GMN: the larger diameter must face the contaminant). Parts out of round or shaft wobbling more than the gap, so they rub ⚑ [uncertain]. Grease flung or washed out of a grease-packed labyrinth (GMN). Expecting it to survive immersion, jets or pressure, which overcome it (GMN). Moist air pumped in as the box heats and cools (Stockwell), leading to condensation.

## Safety

None known for the seal itself. A labyrinth-only box is not waterproof: don't rely on it for mains-voltage electrics outdoors. Spinning shafts with labyrinth rings still need guarding against fingers and hair ⚑ [uncertain].

## Related

- O-ring seal: squeezed rubber ring; watertight and pressure-proof, but rubs if parts move and needs a bought ring
- Gasket (flat, cord-in-groove or cast-in-place): squeezed soft seal for non-round lids; often combined with a labyrinth lip in front of it
- Tongue-and-groove joint (incl. sliding-lid grooves): the joinery version of the same interlocking shape, used for alignment and strength rather than sealing
- Cable [gland](../glossary.md#gland) and strain relief: seals cables through a wall, where a labyrinth can't
- Slip ring and rotary union: carries power or fluid across a spinning joint; a rotary union needs contact seals because a labyrinth leaks under pressure
- Breathable membrane vent: lets air in and out but blocks liquid water; a sealed alternative to a baffled vent (RJC Mold)
- Tesla valve: another fixed winding channel with no moving parts, whose baffles and recesses let fluid flow easily one way but resist it the other way (Wikipedia 'Tesla valve'; listed under 'see also' on Wikipedia 'Labyrinth seal')
