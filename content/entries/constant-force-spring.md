A coil of pre-curled steel strip that, once pulled out a short way, pulls back with an almost constant force over a very long distance, used to counterbalance, retract and keep things under steady tension.

**In plain words:** A tightly rolled-up ribbon of springy steel that tries to curl back up: pull the free end out and it pulls back with almost the same force whether you've pulled out a little or a lot. **Proper term:** Constant-force spring (constant-force extension spring).

**Also called:** constant force spring, constant-force extension spring (Lee Spring; Wikipedia); Neg'ator (brand, AMETEK Hunter Spring) and negator spring (Hunter Spring; the name is often used for the type); Conforce (brand, Vulcan Spring); constant-force strip spring, clip-on constant-force strip spring, constant-force retractor (McMaster-Carr catalogue names); prestressed flat strip spring, rolled ribbon spring (Lee Spring; Wikipedia); counterbalance spring, e.g. 'counterbalance springs for window sashes' (Engineers Edge; Lee Spring); constant-torque spring, spring motor, A-motor and B-motor (the two-drum version that gives a steady turning force) (Hayamizu Hatsujo; Hunter Spring); spring-return reel, retractor, cable or hose retractor (products built around it; many tape measures, badge reels and dog leads use a spiral 'power spring' instead, see related).

## How it works

The strip is made and heat-set so that its natural shape is a tight roll. When you pull the end, the strip leaves the roll and is bent straight; it stores energy because it keeps trying to return to its natural curl (Hunter Spring). All the bending happens in the short curved stretch between the roll and the straight part, and that stretch looks the same whether 10 cm or 1 m has been pulled out, so the force stays almost the same (Wikipedia 'Constant-force spring'). The full force is reached after pulling out about 1.25 times the coil's diameter; after that it stays roughly constant, and the force is set by the strip's thickness and width and the coil diameter (Lee Spring; Hunter Spring). If the outer end is wound onto a second drum instead of pulled straight, the same effect gives a nearly constant turning force (a constant-torque spring motor) (Lee Spring; Hayamizu Hatsujo).

## Parts

Single piece (the rolled steel strip, usually with a hole or shaped tab at the free end), normally used as a small assembly: the coil sits on a free-turning drum or spool, or in a pocket (cavity), and its free end is screwed or riveted to the moving part. Retractors add a housing, storage drum and cable; constant-torque 'spring motors' add a second (output) drum (Vulcan Spring; John Evans' Sons; Hunter Spring).

## Everyday examples

- Sliding sash windows held up by counterbalance springs in the frame (Engineers Edge)
- The carbon brushes pressed against the spinning part of an electric motor (Engineers Edge: brush springs for motors)
- Retracting cable and hose reels, and the cord or head return in some hair dryers, tools and gym machines (Lee Spring)
- Retractable tape measures, badge reels and dog leads work the same way but usually use a spiral 'power spring' wound inside the reel instead (Lee Spring; Printables 'Badge Reel Spring Quick Release')

## When to use it

Use a constant-force spring when you need a steady pull over a long distance in a small space: counterbalancing a sliding door, lid, monitor, window or robot elevator so it stays where you leave it; pulling back a cable, hose or lead; keeping a belt, tape or carriage under even tension; or pressing something (like motor brushes) with the same force as it wears (Lee Spring; Engineers Edge). Robotics teams use them so the lifting force on an elevator is constant (Chief Delphi forum). Its predictable life makes it good for products that cycle a known number of times (Vulcan Spring).

## When not to use it

Don't use one when you need a push (use a compression or gas spring), a force that grows with travel (use a coil extension spring), or very high forces in a small space (use a gas spring or a counterweight). For tape-measure-style reels that wind many turns, a spiral power spring is the usual choice (Lee Spring). Avoid it where the strip can't be guided or would be bent backwards over a small pulley (Lee Spring), where it would see high temperatures or corrosive chemicals for long periods (Vulcan Spring), or where the required number of cycles is far above the spring's rating. For a printed, metal-free alternative at light loads, try a compliant constant-force mechanism (BYU CMR).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - store energy: holds energy to lift, return or counterbalance a load
  - tighten and hold tension: keeps a cable, hose, belt or moving carriage under steady pull and takes up slack
- **Movement:** slide (the free end pulls straight out and back) with rotate (the coil or drum turns as it unrolls); very long travel is possible, with working [deflections](../glossary.md#deflection) of up to 50 times the drum diameter (Hunter Spring).
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 1: the free end moves in and out along the strip's length (the drum turns with it). Sideways and twisting movement is not controlled, so long extensions need guiding (Lee Spring).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): pulls a cable, hose or carriage back with a steady force wherever it is (Lee Spring: cable retractors and hose retrievers)
  - none of the other listed behaviours; its own trick is a nearly constant force over a long [stroke](../glossary.md#stroke) instead of a force that rises as it stretches, which is what makes it a good counterbalance (Wikipedia 'Constant-force spring')
- **How it holds:**
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): the strip's pull holds or returns the load
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): it provides force; the end is fixed with a screw or rivet and the coil simply wraps a drum without being fastened to it (Vulcan Spring)
- **Input and output:** Linear pull-out <-> rotation of the coil or drum. Extension type: nearly constant force over the stroke after the first 1.25 x coil diameter (Lee Spring), with a standard force [tolerance](../glossary.md#tolerance) of +/-10 percent (Vulcan Spring). Constant-torque motor type: an A-motor turns the output drum the same way as the storage drum, a B-motor the opposite way, giving nearly constant [torque](../glossary.md#torque) over many turns (Hayamizu Hatsujo). Back-drivable: yes, always: it is a spring, so it pulls back whenever the load lets go. Efficiency is reduced by friction, most in cavity mounts (John Evans' Sons) and least on a free-turning spool (Vulcan Spring).
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Light to medium per spring: for example, a 0.475 x 0.009 inch strip with a 0.75 inch coil pulls about 2.6 lbf (11.6 N) (MIT 2.007 kit), and Vulcan's example spring J12G21 about 3.42 lb. More force comes from mounting springs back-to-back, in tandem, or laminated (wound together), with total force equal to the sum of the springs, e.g. 9 laminated springs give 9 times the force (Vulcan Spring). It only pulls along the strip; it cannot push, and it is weak and unstable against sideways and twisting loads, so guide it (Lee Spring). Don't use the spring as the end stop (Vulcan Spring).
- **Play and precision:** not applicable (it doesn't locate parts). Its force is only accurate to about +/-10 percent, and the coil's inside and outside diameters also vary by +/-10 percent (Vulcan Spring); force builds up over the first 1.25 coil diameters of travel (Lee Spring).
- **Taking it apart:** removable: the coil just wraps tightly on its spool and is not fastened to it (Vulcan Spring); the free end is screwed, riveted or clipped on and can be undone. Take care, because it is under tension.
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: a constant-force spring has a predictable, rated [fatigue](../glossary.md#fatigue) life of about 2,500 to over a million extend-and-retract cycles depending on stress and size (Hunter Spring); common stock designs are 4,000-25,000 cycles (Vulcan Spring); Lee Spring stocks 2,500, 4,000, 13,000 and 25,000-cycle ranges.
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** no for a given spring: its force is fixed by strip thickness, width and coil diameter (Lee Spring). Change the force by swapping springs or adding more springs in parallel (Vulcan Spring), or change the lever or pulley ratio it acts through.
- **Access needed:** not applicable: fitted during assembly, with access to the drum end and the load end
- **Materials:** Usually high-yield Type 301 stainless steel strip (Lee Spring; Vulcan Spring; Hunter Spring; McMaster-Carr), which resists corrosion; also high-carbon steel, Inconel and Elgiloy for special cases (Vulcan Spring; Hunter Spring). Drums and spools can be HDPE, PVC pipe or 3D-printed plastic running on bearings (Chief Delphi forum). Printed plastic is not a substitute for the steel strip itself: plastics [creep](../glossary.md#creep) and lose force under constant stress ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Type 301 stainless resists corrosion in normal indoor and outdoor use (McMaster-Carr), but 'corrosive atmospheres or extreme temperatures will affect a spring's life and material selection' (Vulcan Spring). Highly stressed springs slowly lose force through [stress relaxation](../glossary.md#stress-relaxation), which is sped up by heat and temperature cycling: aerospace hinge springs lost 7-10 percent of torque after cycles peaking at only about 37 °C (Postma 2006). Printed plastic drums and housings soften with heat (PLA about 60 °C, PETG about 80 °C, ASA about 110 °C in CNC Kitchen's test). Choosing a spring rated for too few cycles leads to premature failure (Vulcan Spring).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: the spring itself (constant-force strip springs, constant-force retractors, spring-motor assemblies)
  - 3D print (FDM): drums, flanged spools, cavity housings, strip guides and mounts; a 3D-printable compliant 'constant-force mechanism' can stand in for light loads (Printables, BYU CMR)
  - CNC: turned or milled drums and brackets
  - woodwork: brackets and counterbalanced lids or panels ⚑ [uncertain]
  - sheet metal: brackets and spring housings ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the steel spring is bought and slid onto a printed drum. (A compliant constant-force mechanism can be printed, but the BYU CMR demonstrator prints as four identical parts that snap together.)
- **Tools:** screwdriver or rivet tool to attach the free end, and something to make or buy a drum or spool; work gloves, because the strip edges are sharp and the spring snaps back (Chief Delphi forum). Otherwise no special tools; do not cut, drill or otherwise modify the spring (MIT 2.007 kit notes).
- **Difficulty:** easy to use, hard to make: buy the steel spring and 3D print the drum, housing and guides. Rolling and heat-setting your own spring strip is not practical at home ⚑ [uncertain].

### 3D printing tips

1) Size the drum right: Lee Spring says a drum should be 10-20 percent larger than the spring's natural inside diameter, and Vulcan Spring says a spool 15-20 percent larger than the nominal inside diameter, because the inside diameter itself varies by +/-10 percent; the spring should grip the spool tightly. Alternatively run it loosely on a freely rotating mandrel about 10 percent smaller than its inside diameter (MIT 2.007 kit notes) or on an undersized pin (Vulcan Spring). 2) Let the drum spin freely, ideally on pressed-in ball bearings: a free-turning spool gives 'smooth, repeatable operation due to the reduction of friction', while a spring rubbing in a pocket (cavity mount) is easier to assemble but has the most friction (Vulcan Spring; John Evans' Sons; Chief Delphi forum: 'Blue drum and white flanges where all 3d printed, the drum had some bearings pressed into it'). 3) Let a metal bolt or shaft carry the load and use the printed spool only to guide the coil; one FRC mentor 'wouldn't trust a printed part to carry the load itself' (Chief Delphi forum). Support the spring on both sides. 4) Give the strip room: it doesn't leave the coil at a tangent, so allow at least 0.8 x inside diameter between the drum centre and the strip's straight path (Vulcan Spring). 5) Add flanges or guides to keep long extensions from twisting or kinking on the way back (Lee Spring). 6) Never make the strip bend backwards: any [idler](../glossary.md#idler) pulley must be larger than the natural coil diameter and must not bend the strip against its curl (Lee Spring; MIT 2.007: back-bending 'will drastically limit its life'). Vulcan suggests reversing long, heavy springs before winding them onto a spool, taking care not to back-bend them; AMETEK's video shows a way to fit a spring to a drum without reversing it. 7) Keep at least 1.5 turns on the drum at full extension, and add a hard end stop on the moving part rather than letting the spring act as the stop (Vulcan Spring). 8) Print drums upright so they are round, with smooth walls and the seam away from the contact face ⚑ [uncertain]. 9) Heat: printed PLA parts begin to soften around 60 °C (CNC Kitchen), and a spring gripping a PLA drum puts it under constant pressure, so use PETG, ASA or nylon near heat and check for creep over time ⚑ [uncertain]. 10) For very light loads you can print a spring-free alternative: BYU CMR's 'Constant-Force Mechanism' uses a bending plastic beam riding on a cam to give nearly constant force (Printables).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Search for: 'constant-force springs' or 'constant force extension springs' (Lee Spring, Vulcan Spring 'Conforce', AMETEK Hunter Spring 'Neg'ator'); 'constant-force strip springs', 'clip-on constant-force strip springs' and 'constant-force retractors' (McMaster-Carr); 'constant torque springs' or 'spring motors' (A-type and B-type; Neg'ator spring motor assemblies with storage and output drums) (Hunter Spring; Hayamizu Hatsujo); 'spool' or 'drum' plus ball bearings for mounting; 'power springs' (spiral) for tape-measure-style reels (Lee Spring).

## Rules of thumb

- Full rated force is reached after pulling out 1.25 times the coil diameter; after that the force stays nearly constant (Lee Spring 'Learn About Constant Force Springs'; Hunter Spring 'What is a Negator Constant Force Spring').
- Drum diameter 10-20 percent larger than the spring's natural inside diameter; keep 1.5 wraps on the drum at full extension; guide long extensions; idler pulleys larger than the natural diameter and never back-bending the strip (Lee Spring).
- Spool 15-20 percent larger than the nominal inside diameter (because of the +/-10 percent tolerance on the inside diameter); spring length = (inside diameter x 3.14159 x 1.5) + maximum extension; leave at least 0.8 x inside diameter of [clearance](../glossary.md#clearance) from the drum centre to the strip; force tolerance +/-10 percent (Vulcan Spring '8 Steps to a Constant Force Spring Design' and 'Constant Force Spring Mounting Methods'). Note: MIT's 2.007 kit notes instead suggest a free-turning mandrel about 10 percent smaller than the inside diameter, a looser mounting that Vulcan also allows ('an undersized bushing or pin').
- Rated life ranges from 2,500 to over a million cycles; working deflections up to 50 times the drum diameter (Hunter Spring); common life ratings are 4,000-25,000 cycles, and a cycle means one extension and retraction of any given part of the strip (Vulcan Spring).
- Force formula: P = (E b t^3 / 24) x [1/Rn^2 - (1/Rn - 1/R1)^2], where b is strip width, t thickness, Rn the natural coil radius and R1 the radius of the shaft or drum it is wound onto; the natural radius shouldn't be less than about 40 times the strip thickness or the strip yields when pulled straight (Hayamizu Hatsujo 'About Design'). Engineering handbooks write the simple case as P = E b t^3 / (c Dn^2), with c a constant for cross-curvature, and the bending stress as S = E t / Dn (Engineers Edge 'Constant Force Spring Design'); many calculators use c = 26.4 ⚑ [uncertain].
- Several springs can share a load: total force is the sum of the springs; back-to-back mounting is the most stable for long extensions; laminated (interwound) springs suit short extensions only because they bind if pulled too far (Vulcan Spring 'Creating more force'; John Evans' Sons 'Mounting Methods').
- Highly stressed constant-torque springs lost 7-10 percent of their end torque after thermal cycling that only reached about 37 °C, because of stress relaxation (R. W. Postma, 'Torque Loss and Stress Relaxation in Constant Torque Springs', 38th Aerospace Mechanisms Symposium, 2006).

## Common failures

Back-bending the strip over a small pulley or while fitting it, which 'will drastically limit its life and reduce its coil effectiveness' (MIT 2.007 kit notes; Lee Spring). Twisting or kinking on the way back at long extensions because the strip isn't guided (Lee Spring). Pulling off the drum or losing grip because fewer than 1.5 wraps remain (Vulcan Spring). Too much friction in a cavity mount or a fixed post, which reduces the useful force and shortens life (John Evans' Sons; MIT 2.007 kit notes). Laminated springs binding on long strokes and tandem springs rubbing together (Vulcan Spring). Premature failure because the spring's rated cycle life was underestimated (Vulcan Spring). Slow loss of force through stress relaxation, especially when warm (Postma 2006). Printed drums or supports breaking or creeping under the load; let a metal bolt carry it (Chief Delphi forum). A modified (cut or drilled) spring failing early (MIT 2.007 kit notes).

## Safety

Stored energy and sharp edges: a released or broken constant-force spring snaps back very fast. An FRC mentor warned that 'if it came loose, it would coil up very fast and hard, and could seriously injure anyone', and teams report 'nasty' cuts to fingers and hands; they require work gloves for anyone handling them and keep bystanders back (Chief Delphi forum). Never let go of an extended spring, keep 1.5 turns on the drum so it can't pull off, support it on both sides, and don't use it as an end stop (Vulcan Spring; Chief Delphi forum). Wear eye protection when fitting or removing springs ⚑ [uncertain]. Pinch points where the strip rolls onto the drum ⚑ [uncertain].

## Related

- Spiral power spring (clock spring): a flat spiral wound inside a reel that stores turning energy; force rises as it is wound; used in tape measures, badge reels, dog leads and seat belts
- Coil spring (extension): force rises steadily with stretch (Hooke's law), unlike the nearly flat force of a constant-force spring
- Spring balancer (gravity compensation): holds a tool or arm weightless, often using a spring with a cam or cone-shaped drum to even out the force
- Constant-torque spring motor (A-motor, B-motor): the same strip wound between two drums to give a steady turning force
- Gas spring: a sealed gas cylinder that pushes with a fairly steady force and adds [damping](../glossary.md#damping)
- Ratcheting reel closure (dial lacing system) and other reels & take-up mechanisms: wind up cord or strap, but lock rather than pull back continuously
- Compliant constant-force mechanism: a printed bending beam plus cam or [linkage](../glossary.md#linkage) shaped to give nearly constant force (BYU CMR)
- Counterweight: gives a truly constant force but is heavy and bulky
