A bow-tie-shaped key, wide at both ends and narrow in the middle, is fitted into a matching pocket that spans a joint or crack, so its wide ends lock into both sides and stop them pulling apart; used to stop cracks in wooden slabs and to join CNC-cut or 3D-printed panels.

**In plain words:** A small bow-tie-shaped piece set into a matching hole that straddles a crack or seam, so the two sides can't pull apart. **Proper term:** Butterfly key (butterfly joint, bow-tie key).

**Also called:** butterfly joint, bow tie, bowtie inlay (Wikipedia 'Butterfly joint'; Rockler); dovetail key, double dovetail key (Wikipedia 'Butterfly joint'); Dutchman joint (Wikipedia; note that 'Dutchman' more often means a patch that replaces damaged wood); Nakashima joint, after the woodworker George Nakashima (Wikipedia; Popular Woodworking); bow-tie connector, butterfly connector, butterfly wedge lock (maker and CNC names; WikiHouse; Printables 'Gridfinity Refined'); butterfly key inlay, slab key.

## How it works

The key is two dovetails joined at their narrow ends. When it sits in its pocket across the seam, each wide 'wing' is trapped in one part, so pulling the parts apart would mean crushing the pocket walls or snapping the key's narrow neck (Wikipedia 'Butterfly joint'; WikiHouse bow-tie tests). In wood the key's grain runs from end to end, across the crack, because wood hardly changes length along its grain; a key cut the other way would move with the slab and not hold the crack (Katz-Moses Tools). In CNC plywood building systems the keys are tapped into matching holes on both faces of neighbouring blocks and carry both pulling and sliding ([shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear)) loads between them (WikiHouse).

## Parts

A few parts: the two parts being joined (or the two sides of a crack), a bow-tie-shaped pocket cut half into each, and the key itself (wood, plywood, OSB or printed plastic), usually glued in woodworking and tapped in dry with a mallet in CNC building systems (Wikipedia 'Butterfly joint'; WikiHouse assembly guide).

## Everyday examples

- Live-edge wooden slab tables with contrasting bow-ties across their cracks, made popular by George Nakashima (Popular Woodworking; Wikipedia)
- 3D-printed Gridfinity storage baseplates joined with small printed butterfly keys (Printables 'Gridfinity Refined')
- WikiHouse CNC-cut plywood house blocks joined on site with bow-ties tapped in with a rubber mallet (WikiHouse assembly guide)
- Reinforced cracks and glue joints in doors, picture frames and drawers (Wikipedia 'Butterfly joint')

## When to use it

Use a butterfly key to stop a crack or check in a wide board or slab from opening further, to strengthen a glue joint between boards while showing it off as a feature, or to hide a defect as a decorative inlay (Rockler; Katz-Moses; Wikipedia). In CNC and 3D-printed work, use bow-tie keys to join flat panels, blocks or baseplates edge to edge without screws, since they resist both pulling apart and sliding (WikiHouse; Printables).

## When not to use it

Not for a crack that is still moving because the wood is wet: dry the wood first ⚑ [uncertain]. Not for joints that must come apart often: use screws, cam locks or a sliding dovetail. Not where the joint bends out of its plane: a surface key only holds one face, so use a [spline](../glossary.md#spline), a bolted plate or keys on both faces (Popular Woodworking). For filling a missing chunk, use a Dutchman patch instead. For thin printed parts, formed interlocking edges (a dovetail or puzzle cut) avoid a separate part (Formlabs; Boxes.py dovetail edges).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: joins panels or blocks edge to edge, and holds a crack or glue joint closed (Wikipedia; WikiHouse)
- **Movement:** none: it is a rigid joint
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 when seated and glued. A dry key stops the parts pulling apart or sliding past each other within the panel's plane; only lifting the key out of its pocket (perpendicular to the face) is not blocked by shape, so it relies on glue or a tight fit for that ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it is a static joint with no clever behaviour
- **How it holds:**
  - shape (the parts interlock; [form-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): the wide wings are trapped in pockets on each side of the seam (Wikipedia)
  - material bond (glue, solvent or melting): woodworking keys are glued into the pocket (Rockler; Popular Woodworking)
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): CNC and printed keys are a tight tap-in or press fit, used dry (WikiHouse assembly guide; Printables 'Gridfinity Refined')
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Medium: strong against the parts pulling apart across the seam (tension) and sliding along it (shear) in the panel's plane; weak against lifting or bending out of the plane, which only glue or a tight fit resists ⚑ [uncertain]. WikiHouse tests on 18 mm Metsä plywood: one bow tie in tension failed at 7.4-9.0 kN (about 750-920 kgf) by crushing and shearing at the contact faces; three bow ties together took 19.2-24.1 kN; in shear, the plywood bow ties took 10.8-13.5 kN per shear plane (four bow ties, two per face, joined three mock columns, so about two per shear plane ⚑ [uncertain]) and OSB ones 16.6 kN, failing at the narrow neck (Granello, WikiHouse 'Bow ties subjected to tensile forces' and 'Bow ties subjected to shear forces'). A thin surface key only holds the top of a thick slab, so through-cracks need keys on both faces (Popular Woodworking).
- **Play and precision:** small: gaps between key and pocket allow a little slip; WikiHouse measured a slip [stiffness](../glossary.md#stiffness) of about 1.4-1.7 kN/mm for one 18 mm plywood bow tie in tension and a design value of 1.5 kN/mm (WikiHouse 'Bow ties'). Glued wooden inlays have no play. Printed keys are tuned by scaling: e.g. scale the pocket to 100.5% for a looser fit (Printables 'Bowtie (connector)'), or print keys at 101-104% and pick the ones that fit (Printables 'Harbor Freight Workbench Grid for Gridfinity Refined').
- **Taking it apart:** permanent when glued (woodworking inlays); removable when fitted dry, e.g. 3D-printed baseplate connectors that can be pushed back out ⚑ [uncertain]; semi-permanent for WikiHouse bow-ties malleted flush into building blocks ⚑ [uncertain]
- **How often it can be reused:** once when glued; a few to many times for dry printed or plywood keys, which slowly loosen as the pocket walls crush ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Sealing:** none: it holds parts together but doesn't seal the gap; cracks are often filled separately (Popular Woodworking prefers not to fill with epoxy)
- **Adjustable:** no
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind): a key is set into one face; for a crack that goes right through, or for structural panels, add keys on both faces (Popular Woodworking; WikiHouse fits bow-ties on inner and outer panels)
- **Materials:** Wood keys, usually a hard, contrasting species such as walnut or ebony, or the same species as the slab; a harder key than the slab so the slab gives way when it is driven in (Wikipedia; Popular Woodworking; Timber Biscuit Woodworks). Plywood (e.g. 18 mm birch) and OSB for CNC building connectors (WikiHouse). 3D-printed PLA or PLA+ for printed baseplate connectors (Printables 'Gridfinity Refined'). Joins wood, plywood, OSB and printed plastic; for metal or glass, use other fixings.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Wood moves much more across the grain than along it (0.1-0.2% along the grain from green to ovendry; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 4); the key's long grain holds the crack, but the slab keeps moving around it. Epoxy fillers in the same crack can pop out as the wood moves (Popular Woodworking). Plywood and OSB keys in buildings must stay dry and protected like the blocks themselves ⚑ [uncertain]. Printed PLA keys soften at about 60 °C (CNC Kitchen).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - woodwork: keys cut by bandsaw, table saw, router table or hand saw; pockets routed and chiselled (Katz-Moses)
  - CNC: cuts keys and pockets together, e.g. in WikiHouse plywood building blocks and in slab furniture (WikiHouse; Katz-Moses)
  - 3D print (FDM): press-fit bow-tie connectors for joining printed baseplates and panels (Printables); also printed router templates for cutting inlays (Printables)
  - laser cut: keys and pockets in thin sheet ⚑ [uncertain]
  - off-the-shelf part: acrylic butterfly router templates and inlay kits (Rockler)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the key is printed separately and pressed into pockets in the printed parts
- **Tools:** Woodwork: a bandsaw, table saw, router table or hand saw to make the key; a marking knife, a router with a straight or spiral bit (often with a template and guide bushing), chisels to square the corners, glue, a mallet, and a plane or sander to level it (Rockler; Katz-Moses; Popular Woodworking). CNC: the CNC router cuts keys and pockets; a rubber mallet taps them in (WikiHouse). 3D printing: none to assemble, just press in.
- **Difficulty:** moderate for a neat wooden inlay: the pocket must match the key exactly or gaps show (Popular Woodworking). Easy with a router template, a CNC or a 3D printer, which cut matching keys and pockets automatically.

### 3D printing tips

1) Fit: printed keys need tuning. Print the key and use the same shape as a negative in the slicer, scaling the negative to about 100.5% for a looser fit (Printables 'Bowtie (connector)'); or print several keys at 101%, 103% and 104% and use the one that snaps in (Printables 'Harbor Freight Workbench Grid for Gridfinity Refined'); Gridfinity Refined's author suggests printing a few test keys and scaling them if they don't fit. 2) Orientation: print keys flat on the bed so their layers run along the key; layer bonds are much weaker (PLA about 55% of flat strength in CNC Kitchen's test), and the narrow neck is the likely place for a printed key to break ⚑ [uncertain]. 3) Round the sharp corners of the wings slightly (a remix added fillets for easier assembly; Printables 'Bowtie Connector Remix') and [chamfer](../glossary.md#chamfer--fillet) the bottom edges so it starts into the pocket (Katz-Moses, for wood). 4) Formlabs rates separate 'key joints' as not ideal for 3D printing, because every extra part adds [tolerance](../glossary.md#tolerance) variation, so test-print one joint first. 5) Material: PLA and PLA+ are tuned in Gridfinity Refined for 0.4 or 0.6 mm nozzles; printed PLA softens at about 60 °C (CNC Kitchen). 6) Router templates for wooden bow-ties can also be printed (Printables 'Butterfly or Bowtie Router Inlay Template').

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Acrylic 'butterfly key' or 'bowtie inlay' router templates used with a plunge router, a 'template guide' (guide bushing) and a 1/8 in. straight or spiral bit (Rockler); 'inlay kits' with a removable bushing so the same template cuts both the pocket and the key (Rockler). Pre-made keys are also sold ⚑ [uncertain].

## Rules of thumb

- Key thickness: at least 1/4 in. for strength, 3/8 in. for big splits in big slabs; dovetail angles of 8-10 degrees; side bevels of about 1 degree (0.5 degrees in very hard wood, up to 2 degrees in soft pine or fir); set the router about 1/16 in. shallower than the key is thick; choose a key harder than the slab; add a second key on the back if the split goes right through (Asa Christiana, Popular Woodworking 'Butterfly Keys Made Easy').
- Key thickness about 1/4 to 1/3 of the slab thickness; cut the key with long grain running end to end; make the pocket walls 90 degrees or slightly [undercut](../glossary.md#undercut) (Katz-Moses Tools 'Butterfly Keys').
- For purely decorative inlays a pocket 1/8-1/4 in. deep is enough (Rockler 'Making Butterfly Inlay Keys').
- Grain of the key runs across the key's length and at right angles to the grain of the slab (Timber Biscuit Woodworks).
- Glue goes in the pocket, not on the key; drive the key evenly (Popular Woodworking), with a scrap block over it to spread the blows (Katz-Moses).
- WikiHouse design values for its standard 18 mm plywood bow tie: shear area 1008 mm², shear stiffness 3 kN/mm, tensile area 1008 mm², contact area at failure about 767 mm², tensile stiffness 1.5 kN/mm (WikiHouse 'Bow ties' engineering guide).
- An Eurocode 5 based model overestimated bow-tie tensile capacity by about 30%, because the panel around the key deforms and reduces the contact area (Granello, WikiHouse 'Bow ties subjected to tensile forces', 2023).
- A connection with three bow ties was on average 23% stiffer and 61% stronger in tension than WikiHouse's earlier pegs-and-combs connection (WikiHouse Weeknotes #29, 2022).

## Common failures

Visible gaps around the key from a poorly matched pocket; trace the key with a knife and cut the key first (Katz-Moses; Popular Woodworking). A key cut with short grain that snaps when hammered in and doesn't hold the crack (Katz-Moses). Pocket walls not square, so the key won't sit flat on the bottom (Katz-Moses). Key splintering or cracking when driven into hard wood; ease the bevel and drive through a flat block (Popular Woodworking). Sanding a key flush can round it over and leave a dip around it (Katz-Moses). In CNC plywood, failure by crushing and shearing where the wings bear on the pocket, or by the neck breaking in shear (WikiHouse tests). Printed keys too tight or too loose; tune by scaling (Printables).

## Safety

Cutting small keys is risky: the table-saw method is 'expert-level (a.k.a. dangerous)' (Katz-Moses); use a bandsaw or router table with push blocks for small parts ⚑ [uncertain]. Router and chisel work need firm clamping. On building sites, bow-ties on roofs and upper walls need proper working-at-height safety (WikiHouse assembly guide).

## Related

- Sliding dovetail: a single flared tongue slid into a groove, instead of a separate key dropped in from the face
- Dovetail joint: the same flared shape cut into the ends of two boards
- Split-part joints for large prints (dovetail keys, puzzle joints): interlocking shapes cut into the faces of printed pieces
- Dovetail and finger edges in Boxes.py: in-plane dovetail edges that join laser-cut sheets edge to edge without a separate key (Boxes.py edges.py)
- Dutchman patch: an inlaid piece that replaces damaged wood rather than locking a crack
- Tab-and-slot joint: sheet parts held by tabs through slots, for joints at an angle rather than edge to edge
- Dowel and biscuit joints: hidden alignment pieces in edge joints, with no visible key
- Mortise and tenon with a separate loose tenon: another separate insert joining two parts
