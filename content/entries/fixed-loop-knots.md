Knots that make a loop that won't shrink or slip, at the end of a rope (bowline, figure-eight loop) or in its middle (alpine butterfly), to hook, clip or drop over something, and that untie by hand afterwards.

**In plain words:** A loop tied in a rope or cord that stays the same size when you pull on it, either at the end of the line or anywhere along its middle, so you can drop it over a hook, post or peg, or clip a carabiner or snap hook into it. **Proper term:** Fixed loop knots (eye knots): the bowline, the figure-eight loop and the alpine butterfly loop, plus the more secure bowline variants.

**Also called:** bowline, boling knot (archaic), 'king of knots' (ABOK #1010; Wikipedia also lists #1716) (Animated Knots 'Bowline'; Wikipedia 'Bowline'); figure-eight loop, figure-eight on a bight, Flemish loop, Flemish eight (ABOK #1047; Wikipedia also lists #531) (Wikipedia 'Figure-eight loop'); figure-eight follow-through, figure-eight retrace, rethreaded figure eight: the same loop tied round a closed ring or harness (Wikipedia 'Figure-eight loop'; Animated Knots); alpine butterfly loop, butterfly loop, butterfly knot, lineman's loop, lineman's rider (ABOK #1053; Ashley also shows it as the harness loop, #532) (Animated Knots; Wikipedia 'Butterfly loop'); double bowline, round turn bowline (ABOK #1013) (Wikipedia 'Double bowline'; Animated Knots 'Water Bowline'); water bowline: a bowline with an extra half hitch forming a clove hitch (ABOK #1012) (Animated Knots; Wikipedia 'Water bowline'); Yosemite bowline, bowline with a Yosemite finish or tie-off (Wikipedia 'Yosemite bowline'; Animated Knots 'Bowline'); left-hand bowline, cowboy bowline, Dutch bowline (ABOK #1034½) (Animated Knots 'Bowline'; Wikipedia 'Bowline'; Šimon et al. 2020); Eskimo bowline, Cossack knot; its slipped form is the Kalmyk loop (Wikipedia 'Bowline'; Animated Knots 'Bowline'); bowline on a bight: a two-loop bowline tied in the middle of a rope (ABOK #1080) (Animated Knots; Wikipedia 'Bowline on a bight'); directional figure eight, inline figure-eight loop (ABOK #1058) (Animated Knots; Wikipedia 'Directional figure eight'); double figure-eight loop, bunny ears, dog-eared loop (ABOK #1085) (Animated Knots 'Figure 8 Double Loop'); farmer's loop (ABOK #1054) and double dragon loop (not in ABOK): other mid-line and end loops (Animated Knots); eye: the proper name for the fixed loop a knot or splice makes; if it slipped it would be a noose (Wikipedia 'List of knot terminology').

## How it works

Each knot pinches the rope against itself so the loop (the eye) can't slide smaller; a loop that slips is a noose, not an eye (Wikipedia 'List of knot terminology'). The bowline is a small loop (nipping turn) in the long part of the rope (standing part) with the tail passed up through it, round the standing part and back down (Animated Knots 'Bowline'); it 'tightens when loaded at (pulled by) the standing part' (Wikipedia 'Bowline'), so with no load nothing squeezes the tail, which is why 'With no load it can be untied easily' and also why, unloaded, it 'can very readily work its way untied' (Animated Knots). The figure-eight loop is a figure-eight knot tied in doubled rope, either on a bight or retraced round a ring, giving two parallel strands that are easy to check (Animated Knots 'Figure 8 Follow Through Loop'). The alpine butterfly is twisted into a bight in the middle of the rope, so it can be pulled 'from the loop to either end of the rope; between the two ends with the loop hanging free; or to the loop with the load spread between the two ends' (Animated Knots 'Alpine Butterfly Loop').

## Parts

Single piece: just the rope or cord itself, plus the hook, post, ring or clip the loop goes onto. A backup knot (stopper) is often tied in the loose end (tail).

## Everyday examples

- A mooring line made fast to a ring or post with a bowline (Animated Knots 'Bowline')
- Climbers at an indoor wall tying the rope to their harness with a figure-eight follow-through, the standard method (Wikipedia 'Figure-eight loop')
- Light aircraft tied down with bowlines, as the US Federal Aviation Administration recommends (Wikipedia 'Bowline')
- A line of hikers clipping along one shared rope, or the first loop of a trucker's hitch for tying down a load, both made with an alpine butterfly (Animated Knots 'Alpine Butterfly Loop')

## When to use it

When you need a loop that won't close up: to drop over a hook, post, peg or cleat, to clip a carabiner or snap hook into, to tie a line to a ring or eye, or to link two ropes loop-to-loop (two bowlines can be linked to join two ropes, Animated Knots 'Bowline'). Pick the bowline for an end loop you will want to untie easily after a heavy load, such as a mooring or tie-down line; the figure-eight loop where security and easy checking matter more than easy untying; and the alpine butterfly when you need a loop in the middle of a line (hand loops along a rope, the first loop of a trucker's hitch, a tie-in point between the ends) or a way to put a damaged section out of action (Animated Knots; Wikipedia 'Butterfly loop'). Good for prototypes, hanging things from printed hooks and temporary rigging where no hardware is wanted.

## When not to use it

Never for climbing, rescue, swings, hammocks, lifting or anything that holds a person without training and a rated system; for anything that holds a person, use rated gear. Don't use a bowline where the line may have to be released under load, because it 'cannot be tied, or untied, when there is a load on the standing end' (Animated Knots 'Bowline'): use a round turn and two half hitches, which 'can be tied and untied under load' (Animated Knots 'Safety'), see Hitches to a post, rail, ring or eye. Don't use a plain bowline on a line that will flap or go slack repeatedly, or in slippery Dyneema: use a water bowline, double bowline with a backup, figure-eight loop or double dragon loop. Don't load a directional figure eight from the wrong end, and don't use a figure-eight loop where it must be untied easily after big loads. For a loop that should slide to tension a line, use Tensioning and adjustable hitches; for a permanent loop at nearly full strength, an eye splice (Rope splices); for steel cable, a thimble and clips or sleeves (Wire-rope terminations). Never use a sheepshank to shorten a rope or bypass damage (Animated Knots 'Sheepshank').

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: makes a fixed attachment point (an eye) in a rope to hook, clip or drop over a post, or to link to another loop
  - lock, and release by hand: unties by hand with no tools; the bowline and alpine butterfly stay easy to undo even after a heavy load
- **Movement:** none: the loop keeps its size and the knot should not move; the rope on either side stays flexible and the loop can swing on its hook.
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 within the knot. A loop on a hook, ring or carabiner can still pivot and swing about it, like a simple pin joint.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: the useful property is simply that the loop is fixed and does not tighten (an eye rather than a noose) (Wikipedia 'List of knot terminology'). Note that the directional figure eight is made to be pulled one way only: 'a strain from the wrong end actually capsizes the knot into one that slides, i.e., it functions as a noose' (Animated Knots 'Figure 8 Directional Loop')
  - quick-release: only in slipped forms, such as the Kalmyk loop (a slipped Eskimo bowline); a slipped knot is less secure but releases more easily (Animated Knots 'Bowline', 'Terminology')
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping ([force-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): turns of rope squeeze the tail and standing part together when loaded (Wikipedia 'Knot')
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the closed loop surrounds the hook, post, ring or carabiner it hangs on
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Medium for maker uses, limited by the rope: a loop knot keeps only part of the rope's strength, and sources give different figures, side by side. Bowline: 'approximately 2/3 of its strength' (Wikipedia 'Bowline'); double bowline 70-75% (Wikipedia 'Double bowline'). CMC Rescue tests on 12.5 mm rescue rope (McKently, ITRS 2014): bowline 67% (1987, nylon), 75% (2008, nylon), 58% (2014, polyester); figure-eight loop 80%, 77%, 70%; alpine butterfly 77% and 61% loaded loop-to-end, 66% and 60% end-to-end; directional (inline) figure eight 73% and 63% loop-to-end but only 51% and 49% end-to-end; Hudson and Vines, quoted in the same paper: bowline 73-74%, figure eight 78-80%. Šimon, Dekýš and Palček (2020) on 10.5 mm static rope: figure-eight loop about 64%, bowline on a bight 61%, left-hand bowline 60%, bowline 58.5%; pulled sideways (cross-loaded), alpine butterfly 54.6% against 44.1% for the figure-eight loop. CMC's 2023 tests on thinner ropes led them to recommend assuming 50% 'until further testing proves otherwise' (CMC Pro), and Animated Knots says to assume even new rope gives 'no more than 50% of its rated breaking strength'. Direction matters: strongest pulled along the standing part with the loop loaded; weakest when the loop is pulled apart from inside (ring-loading), where bowlines untied rather than broke, the standard bowline at about 1.95 kN and the left-hand bowline at about 10.8 kN (Šimon et al. 2020). Never for holding people without training and a rated system.
- **Play and precision:** not applicable: it doesn't locate parts precisely. The knot settles and the rope stretches when first loaded, so dress (neaten) and tighten it before use to limit slipping (Wikipedia 'Knot', Slipping).
- **Taking it apart:** removable: unties by hand. The bowline 'With no load ... can be untied easily' but 'cannot be tied, or untied, when there is a load on the standing end' (Animated Knots 'Bowline'); the alpine butterfly 'remains reasonably easy to undo' after a heavy load (Animated Knots); the figure-eight loop 'can become difficult to untie after heavy loading, and can jam badly' (Wikipedia 'Figure-eight loop'), and the directional figure eight 'can be difficult to undo after a shock load' (Animated Knots).
- **How often it can be reused:** effectively unlimited: the same rope can be tied and untied many times; worn, chafed or shock-loaded rope is the limit, not the knot ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** no: the loop size is fixed once tied; to change it, untie and re-tie. Double-loop knots (double figure-eight loop, bowline on a bight) let you set the two loop sizes while tying (Šimon et al. 2020). For a loop that slides to adjust tension, see Tensioning and adjustable hitches.
- **Access needed:** not applicable: no panel access is involved. The bowline and figure-eight follow-through need a free end (the follow-through can be tied round a closed ring or post); the alpine butterfly, figure-eight on a bight, directional figure eight and bowline on a bight can be tied in the middle of a rope without using either end (Wikipedia 'Butterfly loop', 'Figure-eight loop'; Animated Knots 'Mid Loop Knots').
- **Materials:** Any rope, cord, paracord or string; natural fibres, nylon and polyester grip best. The bowline 'when tied in certain materials or loading conditions ... may not hold' (Wikipedia 'Bowline'); for slippery high-modulus polyethylene (HMPE, e.g. Dyneema (brand)) the double dragon loop 'works with high modulus and slippery ropes such as Dyneema' (Animated Knots 'Double Dragon Loop'). The alpine butterfly 'works best with softer ropes' (Wikipedia 'Butterfly loop'). Manuals recommend the figure-eight loop in rope of at least 9 mm because it is hard to untie after heavy loading (Šimon et al. 2020). Flat webbing uses an overhand loop instead (65% in 1-inch tubular nylon webbing, CMC 1987 test quoted by McKently 2014). Fishing line has its own loops (perfection loop, surgeon's loop, Bimini twist) (Animated Knots 'End Loop Knots'), and hollow-braid rope can be spliced into an eye instead.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Repeated loading and slackening, flapping and shaking: the bowline 'Shakes Undone If Not Loaded' (Animated Knots) and 'tends to spontaneously loosen under cyclic loading' (Šimon et al. 2020), whereas the figure-eight loop 'does not tend to untie under cyclic loading' and the alpine butterfly 'does not tend to disintegrate under cyclic loading' (Šimon et al. 2020). Water: nylon 'absorbs water and is 10-15% weaker when wet', while polyester 'is not affected by water' (Wikipedia 'Rope'); after an hour in tap water CMC measured bowline 67% (nylon) and 58% (polyester), figure-eight loop 91% and 68% (McKently 2014); the water bowline was devised for wet use where other knots may slip or jam (Wikipedia 'Water bowline'). Sunlight: some synthetic ropes 'can be damaged more easily by UV light' (Wikipedia 'Rope'). Slippery modern fibres (HMPE) make many knots slip; choose a knot that suits them (Animated Knots 'Double Dragon Loop').

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: rope or cord, tied by hand
  - 3D print (FDM): hooks, posts, rings and knot-practice boards that the loop goes onto (see make_it_yourself)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: knots are tied in cord; only the parts the loop goes onto, or practice boards, are printed.
- **Tools:** none. A fid (a tapered spike) can help loosen a jammed figure-eight loop, and a knife or hot cutter cuts and seals synthetic cord (Wikipedia 'Knot', Tools).
- **Difficulty:** easy: no tools or parts. The hard part is tying them correctly every time (a bowline 'is too easily tied incorrectly', Animated Knots) and choosing the right knot for the direction of pull.

### 3D printing tips

Loops aren't printed, but they are often dropped over printed hooks and posts or clipped to printed rings. 1) Give a printed hook or post a smooth, generously rounded seat: rope is weakest where it bends tightly, and tests show thicker ropes, which bend more gently in a knot, keep more of their strength (CMC 2023: average 54%, 60% and 65% in 9.5, 11 and 12.5 mm rope) ⚑ [uncertain as a hook-sizing rule]. 2) Don't stretch a loop wide over a broad post or two hooks: a bowline pulled apart from inside (ring-loaded) can spill at low force (Šimon et al. 2020); if the loop must sit wide, the Eskimo bowline 'is preferred when the loop will be stretched wide' (Animated Knots 'Bowline'). 3) Printed hooks, rings and thimbles are for light loads only; one printed rope-ring designer notes such rings 'are for light rigging and organization, not life-safety loads' (Printables 1772939). 4) Orient a printed hook so the pull runs along its layers rather than trying to [peel](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear) them apart ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) Practise on a printed ring-and-cleat trainer or knot board before relying on a knot (Printables 588348, 979503).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Rope and cord sold by fibre, diameter and breaking strength (braided nylon or polyester cord, paracord, accessory cord, static kernmantle rope); hooks, eye bolts, rings, cleats and posts to drop a loop over; spring-gate hooks (carabiners, snap hooks) to clip into it; rope thimbles to protect the inside of a loop from wear. Where it really matters, use rated parts instead: factory-sewn slings or spliced eyes. Choose rope by its breaking strength divided by a design factor of 5:1 to 12:1 (Cordage Institute CI 1401-15, quoted by Animated Knots 'Safety').

## Rules of thumb

- Choose by where the loop goes and which way it is pulled: end of the line and easy to untie, a bowline (ABOK #1010) with a backup; end of the line, most secure and easiest to check, a figure-eight loop or follow-through (ABOK #1047); middle of the line, or pulled in several directions, an alpine butterfly (ABOK #1053); directional figure eight (ABOK #1058) only when the pull always comes from one known end, otherwise 'the Alpine Butterfly Loop is preferred' (Animated Knots 'Bowline', 'Alpine Butterfly Loop', 'Figure 8 Directional Loop').
- Back up a bowline: when climbing 'A Safety Knot is essential', e.g. a double overhand (strangle knot) round the loop or the standing end, or a Yosemite tie-off; tighten the bowline first and then the tie-off, or it can become a slip knot (Animated Knots 'Bowline'). Because a badly dressed Yosemite bowline can collapse into a noose, some advise a standard or double bowline with a double overhand backup instead (Wikipedia 'Yosemite bowline').
- Tail length, side by side: bowline tail 12 times the rope's circumference by a rule some texts quote, though this 'is rarely seen in practice' (Animated Knots 'Bowline'); figure-eight loop without a backup knot, 4 to 8 inches (about 10-20 cm) (Wikipedia 'Figure-eight loop'); Yosemite bowline, at least 10 cm, more in thick rope (Wikipedia 'Yosemite bowline').
- Dress it before loading: the figure-eight loop should show two strands side by side all the way round, with the outer turns snugged in; 'Dressed this way the knot withstands a load better' (Animated Knots 'Figure 8 Follow Through Loop'). Crossed strands in a figure-eight loop lose strength (Wikipedia 'Knot').
- Strength for planning: assume about 50% of the rope's rated strength (CMC 2023; Animated Knots 'Safety'); measured values range from about 44% (figure-eight loop pulled sideways) to 82% depending on knot, rope and direction (see load) (McKently 2014; Šimon et al. 2020).
- Pulled sideways (cross-loaded), the figure-eight loop loses about 20% of its efficiency; for a mid-line loop pulled across the rope the alpine butterfly did about 10.5 percentage points better (Šimon et al. 2020).
- To bypass a damaged section of rope, tie an alpine butterfly with the damaged part inside the loop, which then carries no load; it is 'far safer than The Sheep Shank' (Animated Knots 'Alpine Butterfly Loop'; Wikipedia 'Butterfly loop'). Animated Knots says the sheepshank 'should be avoided'.
- On a bowline on a bight, clip through both loops: 2011 testing showed it 'might slip when only one loop is loaded' (Wikipedia 'Bowline on a bight').
- Design factor: rope breaking strength 5 to 12 times the load, 12:1 for live or critical loads (Cordage Institute CI 1401-15, quoted by Animated Knots 'Safety').

## Common failures

Shaking undone: an unloaded bowline works loose, as when Grog's bowline 'floated undone' and lost a scrubbing brush (Animated Knots 'Safety'); prevent it with a backup knot, a Yosemite tie-off, a water bowline or a more secure knot. Wrong tying: the bowline is easily mis-tied; a mis-tied alpine butterfly gives a 'false butterfly' that 'is prone to slipping' (Wikipedia 'Butterfly loop'); leaving the tail outside the first loop of a directional figure eight ties a different knot (ABOK #1057) (Animated Knots). Spilling under ring-load or sideways pull: bowlines untied at low force when the loop was pulled apart (Šimon et al. 2020) and can 'slip when pulled sideways' (Wikipedia 'Bowline'). Capsizing into a noose: the directional figure eight pulled from the wrong end, or a badly dressed Yosemite bowline (Animated Knots; Wikipedia 'Yosemite bowline'). Jamming: the figure-eight loop after heavy loading (Wikipedia 'Figure-eight loop'). Slipping of a bowline on a bight with only one loop loaded (Wikipedia 'Bowline on a bight'). Tails too short to stay in the knot. Breaking at the knot well below the rope's rated strength (Wikipedia 'Knot'). The sheepshank failing under modest load in nylon (Animated Knots 'Sheepshank').

## Safety

A home-made knot must never hold a person: for anything that holds a person, use a rated system and get training. Researchers no longer recommend the bowline 'in life-critical applications' (Šimon et al. 2020), and Animated Knots warns that it 'should be used with great care when climbing ... A Safety Knot is essential'. Inspect and stress-test every knot, 'but not in the direction of fall!' (Animated Knots 'Bowline'). Never stand in, or put a hand or limb in, a loop or bight that may suddenly tighten, and don't wrap rope round your hand (Animated Knots 'Safety'). Use a design factor of 12:1 for live or critical loads, and remember a rope pulled sideways (as when rigging between two points) or jerked by a falling load carries far more than the load's weight (Animated Knots 'Safety').

## Related

- Stopper knots and cord-end anchors: the double overhand and figure-eight knots used as a backup in a loop's tail
- Basic knots (overhand knot, half hitch, reef knot): the overhand loop is the simplest fixed loop, but it jams
- Bends for joining two cords: every bend has matching loop knots; the sheet bend has the same structure as the bowline, and the alpine butterfly bend is tied like the alpine butterfly loop
- Hitches to a post, rail, ring or eye: tie straight onto the object instead of making a loop to drop over it; the round turn and two half hitches can be tied and untied under load
- Tensioning and adjustable hitches (trucker's hitch, taut-line hitch, Prusik): loops that slide to tension a line; an alpine butterfly can start a trucker's hitch
- Rope splices (eye splice, short splice, braided-rope splice): a permanent eye that keeps nearly all of the rope's strength
- Spring-gate hook (carabiner, snap hook, lobster clasp): the usual hardware clipped into a fixed loop
- Eye bolts, screw eyes and hooks: what a loop is dropped over or tied to
- Wire-rope terminations (wire-rope clip, thimble, crimped sleeve): the rated way to make an eye in steel cable
- Binding knots and seizings: a seizing can lash a loop's tail down semi-permanently for extra security
