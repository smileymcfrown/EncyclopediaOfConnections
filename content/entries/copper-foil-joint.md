Each glass piece's edge is wrapped in adhesive copper tape and pressed flat (burnished), the copper is brushed with [flux](../glossary.md#flux), and solder is run along every seam where two foiled edges meet, so the pieces become one rigid panel or 3D shape held together by a continuous metal network.

**In plain words:** Wrapping the edge of each piece of glass in sticky copper tape and then melting solder along the copper where the pieces meet, so the glass pieces are held together by little metal seams, as in Tiffany lamps and suncatchers. **Proper term:** Copper-foil joint (copper foil method, Tiffany method, foiled and soldered stained glass).

**Also called:** copper foiling, foiling (the wrapping step); Tiffany method, Tiffany technique, Tiffany-style (after the Tiffany lamps; Tiffany (brand) used only as a style name); foiled glass, foil-and-solder stained glass, copper foil construction (Everything Stained Glass); tinning (a thin coat of solder on the foil) and beading or a beaded seam (a raised rounded bead of solder) (SCW Stained Glass Club; Everything Stained Glass); tinning copper foil (Fire Mountain Gems, for glass jewellery and slides); 'Joining Glass Mosaics' (title of Sanford Bray's 1886 patent; Wikipedia 'Came glasswork'); copper foil glasswork, an alternative to came glasswork (Wikipedia).

## How it works

Glass can't be soldered, so the copper foil gives every edge a metal skin: the tape's glue only holds it in place until soldering (Everything Stained Glass: the glue 'is only there to hold the foil in place before and during soldering'). Flux cleans the copper so molten solder wets it (Fire Mountain Gems: 'Solder will not stay on the foil if it isn't fluxed properly'). The solder flows into the narrow gap between neighbouring foiled pieces and over the foil on both faces, and as it cools it locks the pieces together; a rounded bead that wraps round the edge holds better than a flat tinned coat because 'the solder clings to the sides of the glass rather than just being held by the copper foil glue' (Everything Stained Glass). Because every seam is soldered along its full length and the foil bends round tight curves, it suits small intricate pieces and 3D forms such as 'cylinders, cones, and globe-shaped shades' (Wikipedia 'Came glasswork').

## Parts

A few materials made into one unit: cut glass pieces, adhesive-backed copper foil tape (plain, black-backed or silver-backed), flux, solder (usually 60/40 or 63/37 tin-lead, or a lead-free alloy), and optionally hanging loops or jump rings, reinforcing wire or a zinc or lead came border; often a patina to colour the solder. Tools: glass cutter and grinder, burnisher (fid) or a foiler, flux brush, soldering iron of 80-100 W class with stand, and a heat-resistant work board with pins or a jig.

## Everyday examples

- Tiffany-style table lamps with leafy or floral glass shades (Wikipedia 'Came glasswork')
- Stained-glass suncatchers and ornaments hanging in a window
- Small glass jewellery boxes, terrariums and 3D glass succulents (Printables 764711)
- Glass or stone pendants with a silver-coloured soldered rim and a soldered jump ring (Fire Mountain Gems)

## When to use it

Use copper foil for small, detailed stained-glass work: suncatchers, lamp shades, panels with many small or curved pieces, 3D shapes (boxes, terrariums, polyhedra), mirror mosaics, and for adding a metal rim and a hanging loop to a glass or stone pendant. Copper foil 'is an easy, versatile alternative to came and is particularly useful for small projects', and it can make 'cylinders, cones, and globe-shaped shades' that lead came can't (Wikipedia 'Came glasswork'). It needs only a soldering iron, not a kiln.

## When not to use it

For large windows and panels that must take wind and weather, use lead came (Lead came joint) or add reinforcement and a strong border; a stained-glass worker reinforces anything over about 24 x 24 in (Reddit r/StainedGlass). To join glass to glass invisibly, use a clear glue (UV-curing adhesive, Two-part epoxy, or Silicone and MS-polymer adhesive sealants for aquariums). To melt compatible glass into one piece, use Glass fusing. For plastic or printed 'stained glass' effects, use clear filament or acrylic with glue, not solder. Don't use leaded solder on anything children will handle or mouth, or near food; use lead-free solder (Soldered metal joint entry; Illinois DPH).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: joins glass to glass (and to wire loops, jump rings and brass parts) permanently
  - glue or bond: the solder acts as a metal glue along every seam, though it is a metallurgical bond to the copper, not to the glass
- **Movement:** none
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: the soldered network makes the panel or shape a single rigid piece
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none
- **How it holds:**
  - [material bond](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond) (glue, solvent or melting): solder melts onto and bonds with the copper foil, and the foil's adhesive holds it to the glass until then
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): a beaded seam wraps over both faces of each piece, so the glass is also trapped between two lips of solder (Everything Stained Glass)
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed once made); making it needs a soldering iron
- **Load:** light: good for suncatchers, lamp shades, small boxes, terrariums and panels; strength comes from the solder, so large flat panels sag or bend and need reinforcement (a Reddit stained-glass worker reinforces 'anything over 24 by 24' in, about 60 x 60 cm, depending on the design). Thin foil makes larger projects and lamps noticeably weaker (Downeast Stained Glass). Strong in the plane of the panel, weak against bending and twisting, and joints crack if dropped or knocked.
- **Play and precision:** not applicable: there is no play, but the gaps between pieces should be small and even because solder fills the gaps; pieces are ground to fit the pattern so they sit close together (Everything Stained Glass) ⚑ [uncertain: no gap figure found]
- **Taking it apart:** permanent: pieces can only be separated by melting the solder out with the iron, which usually means re-foiling the piece; repairs are possible but slow
- **How often it can be reused:** once
- **Sealing:** loose to watertight ⚑ [uncertain]: a well-soldered seam on both faces has no gaps, but foiled panels are not made to be weatherproof windows; outdoor panels usually go behind other glazing or are framed in came and puttied
- **Adjustable:** no: once soldered, the shape is fixed (seams can be reheated to correct small faults)
- **Access needed:** both sides: both faces of a flat panel are soldered, and 3D shapes are soldered inside and out where reachable
- **Materials:** Joins any glass (art glass, mirror, bevels, glass jewels and slides) and any material that can take the iron's heat, Fire Mountain Gems says 'up to 1000 degrees Fahrenheit', e.g. glass, stone slices, shells ⚑ [uncertain] and copper, brass or tinned wire for loops and frames. Solder also bonds to brass, copper and lead came. It can't join bare glass (the foil is essential), plastic, acrylic or 3D-printed parts (they melt), or aluminium. Don't put copper foil on steel reinforcement: 'Electrolysis will deteriorate the copper in short time and cause failure. Always use lead on steel or copper on brass' (Downeast Stained Glass). Very hot solders (under 60% tin) can crack hand-made glass from heat (AA Glass Studio).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Copper oxidises: store opened foil sealed and solder it before it tarnishes (SCW; Everything Stained Glass). Leftover flux turns into a white corrosive film over time, so wash pieces straight away (SCW). Solder softens far below glass temperatures, but well above room conditions; tin-lead solder melts at about 183-190 °C (AA Glass Studio), so ordinary heat and sunlight are fine. Large panels sag over time, especially in heat ⚑ [uncertain]. Water and outdoor weather can corrode the seams; copper next to steel corrodes by electrolysis (Downeast Stained Glass). Uneven heating, such as a hot lamp bulb against one piece, can crack glass ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - soldering (by hand with a soldering iron)
  - glass cutting and grinding to prepare the pieces
  - 3D print (FDM): jigs that hold pieces at set angles, foilers that centre the tape, and pattern templates, not the joint itself (Printables 764711, 868753)
  - off-the-shelf part: copper foil tape, flux, solder, jump rings, and pre-cut glass bevels and jewels
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the joint itself can't be printed; printed parts are jigs and tools only
- **Tools:** soldering iron for stained glass (about 80-100 W, ideally temperature-controlled; most electrical irons are too small, Bullseye Glass) with stand and tip cleaner, flux and brush, solder, burnisher or fid (or a foiler), scissors or knife, glass cutter, grinder or carborundum stone, pins or a jig, gloves, safety glasses and fume extraction
- **Difficulty:** moderate: no expensive machines, but neat foiling and smooth beads take practice, and the lead, flux and hot-iron hazards need care

### 3D printing tips

The joint is not printed, but printed tools help. 1) Angle jigs: a printed jig holds glass pieces at the set angle for 3D shapes (Printables 764711 has 5- and 6-sided succulent jigs at 10, 20, 30 and 40 degrees plus regular polyhedra, and a tilting base so the joint being soldered lies flat and the solder doesn't run down it). 2) Keep the iron off the plastic: the iron runs at about 360-410 °C (Everything Stained Glass; Hakko settings at Warm Glass), far above PLA's softening point, so touch-solder (tack) the pieces at a few points, then lift the piece out of the jig to finish the seams ⚑ [uncertain]. 3) Foilers: a printed foiler with guide wheels sized to the tape width (e.g. 5 mm) feeds and centres the foil on the glass edge (Printables 868753: press-fit wheel, printed threads, spacer for thin rolls, optional bench clamp flap for 16-17 mm benches). 4) Print jigs in PETG or ASA rather than PLA if they sit near the hot work ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) Never solder over or onto a printed part, and never use printed parts as part of the finished seam.

## Rules of thumb

- Foil width: 7/32 in is the most common, leaving a lip usually less than 1/16 in over each face; 3/16 in for bevels, jewels and thin glass; 1/4 in for heavily textured glass, reinforcement areas and doubled glass (Downeast Stained Glass). Aim for an overlap of 1/32-1/16 in on each face; 3/16-1/4 in foil works for most pieces (Everything Stained Glass).
- Foil thickness: 1.25 mil is the standard and least recommended; 1 mil tears too easily; 1.5 mil is most robust but harder round inside curves (Everything Stained Glass, author's reply). Foil comes in about 1-1.5 mil (Downeast Stained Glass).
- Keep the foil centred on the edge: if it is uneven, copper shows through from the other side (Fire Mountain Gems). Start foiling away from the outside edge of an edge piece so the tape's join isn't on the perimeter (Everything Stained Glass).
- Flux all the foil before soldering, or the solder won't stay on (Fire Mountain Gems).
- Tin then bead: tin both sides with 50/50 solder first to stop drip-through, then bead with 60/40 or 63/37, which melts at a lower temperature (SCW Stained Glass Club). Solder the bead right after tinning, before the metal oxidises (SCW).
- Solder melting points: 63/37 is eutectic at 183 °C; 60/40 melts over 183-190 °C (AA Glass Studio; Wikipedia 'Solder' gives 188 °C for 60/40). Most lead-free replacements melt 50-200 °C higher (Wikipedia 'Solder').
- Iron temperature for beading: normally 360-410 °C (680-770 °F); 360 °C is a good start for beginners; go hotter only for embedding wire or brass, lower for lead came (Everything Stained Glass, Hakko FX-601). Hakko settings listed by Warm Glass: lead came 360 °C, copper foiling 410 °C.
- Let every soldered seam cool undisturbed: vibration while cooling forms large crystals that weaken the joint (SCW Stained Glass Club).
- Wash the piece straight after finishing with warm water, baking soda and detergent to stop white corrosion and to protect the silver backing of mirror glass (SCW Stained Glass Club); Fire Mountain Gems also washes with warm water and soap.
- Joining two pieces at right angles: set them to form a V and fill it with solder; it uses more solder but makes a much better joint (Everything Stained Glass, author's reply).
- Don't over-work a seam: soldering too slowly or going over it repeatedly melts the foil glue and lifts the foil (Everything Stained Glass).
- Never put copper foil against steel reinforcement; use lead on steel or copper on brass (Downeast Stained Glass).

## Common failures

Solder won't stick or balls up: flux missing, foil dirty or oxidised, or iron too cool (Fire Mountain Gems; SCW). Solder drips through the seam: iron too hot or too slow; tin first with 50/50, then bead (SCW; Everything Stained Glass). Foil lifts off the glass: seam reworked too slowly so the glue melted, foil overlap too narrow, or ground edges not cleaned of dust before foiling (Everything Stained Glass). Lumpy edges: skipped tinning (Everything Stained Glass). Weak or crystalline seams: the piece was moved while cooling (SCW). Panel bends or seams crack: too large without reinforcement, or foil too thin (Reddit r/StainedGlass; Downeast Stained Glass). Glass cracks from heat: high-melting solder or lingering on one spot (AA Glass Studio). White film or corroded mirror backing: flux not washed off (SCW). Copper shows from the front: foil not centred (Fire Mountain Gems).

## Safety

LEAD: 60/40, 63/37 and 50/50 solder contain lead. 'Soldering lead creates a poisonous vapor that is easily inhaled', and you are also exposed by handling leaded solder (Illinois Department of Public Health 'Lead Safety for Hobbyists'). Never eat, drink or smoke in the work area, keep children (and women who may be pregnant) away from it, wash hands with soap and water after work because 'Alcohol-based hand sanitizers do not remove lead', and clean with a HEPA vacuum or damp wipes rather than sweeping (Illinois DPH). Lead-free solders exist but need more heat (Wikipedia 'Solder'). FUMES AND BURNS: use a fume extractor and good ventilation (Bullseye Glass); acid fluxes can damage the lungs (Illinois DPH), and rosin-core solder gives toxic fumes and shouldn't be used (AA Glass Studio; Bullseye). The iron tip is at about 360-410 °C: use a stand, a heat-resistant board, heat-resistant gloves, closed shoes and long trousers (Bullseye; Everything Stained Glass); molten solder can drip through a seam. GLASS: cut and ground edges are sharp and foil edges can cut fingers; take the sharp edge off with a stone and wear safety glasses (Everything Stained Glass). Glass grinder dust harms the lungs (Illinois DPH). PATINA: black patinas can contain selenium dioxide and nitric acid; one is classed 'H300 Fatal if swallowed' and 'H318 Causes serious eye damage', so wear gloves and goggles and follow the label (Novacan black patina SDS on NextSDS).
