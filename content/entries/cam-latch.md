A latch fitted through a single hole in a door or lid, whose knob, key or tool turns a flat arm (the cam) a quarter turn so that it hooks behind the frame and holds the door closed.

**In plain words:** A knob, key or slot on the front of a door that turns a flat metal arm behind it, so the arm swings over the edge of the frame and holds the door shut. **Proper term:** Cam latch (quarter-turn cam lock).

**Also called:** cam lock (usually the key-operated version); quarter-turn latch / quarter-turn cam latch / 1/4-turn latch; cabinet lock, drawer lock, mailbox lock, locker lock (everyday names); wing-knob, T-handle or L-handle cam latch; tool-operated cam latch (slotted, hex, triangle, square or double-bit key); compression latch / compression cam latch (the cam also pulls the door tight); VISE ACTION (Southco brand); push-to-close cam latch; spring-cam latch; the arm itself: cam, tongue or [pawl](../glossary.md#pawl); Not to be confused with camlock hose couplings, Camlock electrical connectors or flat-pack furniture cam fasteners, which share the name (Wikipedia 'Camlock' disambiguation).

## How it works

The body goes through one hole in the door, shaped with flats so it can't spin, and a nut clamps it from behind. The cam is screwed to the back of the spindle. Turning the knob, key or tool a quarter turn swings the cam from alongside the door edge (open) to behind the lip of the frame (closed), where a stop halts it. Pulling on the door then presses the cam flat against the frame, so the pull goes straight into the cam and doesn't try to turn it; that is why it stays shut. Cranked (offset) cams set how far behind the door the cam sits (the grip); ramped cams ride up slightly as they close to take up slack; and in a compression latch the first 90° swings the cam behind the frame and the rest of a half turn draws it back to squeeze the door onto a gasket (Southco E3).

## Parts

A few parts: a body (a threaded barrel with a head or flange on the front, often holding a key cylinder); a mounting nut, and sometimes a sealing washer, [O-ring](../glossary.md#o-ring) or gasket; the drive (wing knob, T- or L-handle, key cylinder, or a slot, hex, triangle or square recess for a tool); the cam (a flat steel arm, which may be straight, offset or cranked, hooked, spring-wire or ramped); a screw that holds the cam on the square end of the spindle; and a stop (often a stop washer) that limits the turn to 90° or 180°. Some have a [detent](../glossary.md#detent) spring or a push-to-close spring.

## Everyday examples

- the lock on a letterbox or mailbox door
- filing cabinets, desk drawers, lockers and tool chests
- electrical and control cabinet doors opened with a triangle or square key
- small battery doors on electronics boxes opened with a coin or screwdriver

## When to use it

For a hinged door, drawer or lid on a box, cabinet or enclosure that needs to stay shut and open with a simple turn, by hand, key or tool. It fits through one hole, sits almost flush on the outside, keeps its moving parts inside, and can be keyed or tool-operated to keep people out. Choose a compression version when the door must press onto a gasket to keep out water and dust or stop rattling.

## When not to use it

Not for real security: cam locks give light to medium security (use a proper lock, hasp and padlock, or a safe). Not for heavy or structural loads (use bolts or several latches). Not for removable panels with no hinge (use quarter-turn fasteners, Dzus-type, or captive screws). Not for pulling a lid tight from outside without drilling the lid (use a draw latch). Not for round lids (use a bayonet mount or thread). Where the door must open with just a push, use a push-push latch or magnetic catch.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - lock, and release by hand: keeps a door, drawer, lid or access panel shut until someone turns the knob, key or tool
  - join and hold: holds a hinged or lift-off panel against its frame
  - seal it, or pass cables or fluid through: sealed and compression versions squeeze a gasket to keep out dust and water (NEMA 4 / IP65 with the gasket fitted, Southco)
- **Movement:** rotate: a quarter turn (90°) for most; some cam locks turn 180° (set by stop washers, SPEP; McMaster-Carr lists quarter-turn and half-turn). Compression versions are rotate+slide: a half turn in which the cam also pulls back 4.3-6.4 mm (Southco E3).
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 1: the cam turns with the knob (plus a short pull-in on compression types). When latched, the door has 0 freedom at the latch; the hinge or other latches hold the rest.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - hand-release only (it holds against pulling on the door and only opens when someone turns the knob, key or tool)
  - [self-locking](../glossary.md#self-locking) (a pull on the door presses the cam flat against the frame instead of turning it, so vibration and pulling don't open it)
  - quick-release (a quarter turn by hand on wing-knob, T- and L-handle versions)
  - indexing (optional detent housings click the cam into place at the open and closed positions so it can't move by accident; Southco E5)
  - tensioning (compression latches and ramped cams pull the door tight against the frame or gasket, removing rattle)
- **How it holds:** shape (the cam overlaps the lip of the frame; [form-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)), plus friction or clamping (force-fit) in ramped and compression cams that pull the door tight, and spring force in spring-wire cams, detents and push-to-close versions
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed). Electronic cam locks also exist: the electronic key powers the lock through three contacts, the lock checks the key's ID, and then it can be turned (Wikipedia 'Latch'); with no valid key it stays locked.
- **Load:** light to medium: Southco rates its CM cam lock cam at an average ultimate load of 180 N (about 18 kg), zinc E5 latches at 330 N maximum static load, stainless E5 at 550-670 N and the mini E5 at 130 N; E3 compression latches 300-450 N. Strong against the door being pulled straight open (the cam bears on the frame lip). Weaker if the door is prised or bent, because the cam bends or slips off the lip. Use several latches on large doors and don't use them as structural joints.
- **Play and precision:** small to noticeable: a flat cam on a fixed grip leaves a small gap and may rattle; Southco's E5 cams each cover a 2 mm band of grip (+2/-0 mm) and 'provide vibration resistance and grip variation [tolerance](../glossary.md#tolerance)'; compression latches remove the play entirely. It needs only enough overlap between cam and frame lip, so it tolerates a few millimetres of [misalignment](../glossary.md#misalignment) along the frame ⚑ [uncertain: no source gives a figure]. It doesn't locate the door precisely.
- **Taking it apart:** tool-free quick release for wing-knob, T- and L-handle versions; removable with a key or tool for keyed and tool-operated versions; the latch itself unbolts from the door with its nut
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: it is made to be opened and closed daily; no cycle rating was found in the catalogues checked ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Sealing:** none for a plain cam lock; sealed versions with an internal O-ring and a gasket reach watertight splash protection, NEMA 4 / IP65 (dust-tight and protected against water jets) (Southco E5 and CM sealed types; IP code from Wikipedia). Compression latches keep that seal tight by squeezing the door onto the gasket (Southco E3).
- **Adjustable:** yes, in steps: you set the grip by choosing the cam offset and length, and adjustable-grip versions have a threaded cam position with a jam nut (Southco E5); washers can shim the cam (Printables mailbox cam); cams can be fitted either way round to change which way it turns (Wikipedia: offset cams are reversible; Southco CM 'cam forward / cam reversed')
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind) to open and close; fitting the nut and cam needs the back of the door, which you can reach while it is open
- **Materials:** Bodies: die-cast zinc alloy (chrome plated or black powder coated), 304 or 316 stainless steel, or glass-filled nylon; cams: zinc-plated steel or stainless steel (Southco; Rencol). Doors and panels: sheet steel or aluminium, plastic, plywood, MDF or printed plastic; Southco's E5 suits doors 1-6 mm thick. Printed latches: ABS or PETG rather than PLA (1944GPW).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Plated zinc suits indoor use; for outdoor, marine or washdown use choose 304 or 316 stainless steel (Southco offers corrosion-resistant materials). Sealed versions with gaskets reach NEMA 4 / IP65 (Southco). Vibration can shift a cam that has no detent or ramp; detented housings stop accidental movement (Southco E5). No temperature ratings were found in the catalogues checked ⚑ [uncertain]. Printed PLA latches are [brittle](../glossary.md#brittle--ductile) and soften with heat; use ABS or PETG (1944GPW).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: the usual choice (cam locks and cam latches are cheap and come in many grips)
  - sheet metal: cabinet and enclosure doors, with the shaped hole punched or cut
  - laser cut: acrylic or plywood doors, with the D-shaped or square hole cut accurately so the body can't spin
  - woodwork: cabinet doors and boxes (drill the hole and file or chisel the flats)
  - 3D print (FDM): knobs, replacement cams and whole light-duty latches for boxes and enclosures
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the body, cam and knob are separate parts that are assembled through the door; one printable design prints all three parts together on a sprue for convenience, but they are cut apart and assembled
- **Tools:** To use: none (wing, T- or L-handle), a key, or a tool (flat screwdriver, hex key, or a triangle, square or double-bit key; Southco E5 head styles). To fit: a drill and file or a panel punch for the shaped hole, a spanner for the mounting nut, and a screwdriver for the cam screw.
- **Difficulty:** easy for a bought latch: cut one shaped hole and tighten one nut; moderate to print your own, because the cam must be solid and printed in a tough plastic to take the load

### 3D printing tips

Use 100% (solid) [infill](../glossary.md#infill) for the cam and spindle, and ABS or PETG rather than PLA, which is more brittle (1944GPW's printed quarter-turn battery-door latch, which fits a 10 mm hole in a 1.5 mm box wall, can be glued with CA or screwed on, and has small detent dimples that hold the arm in position). Make the hole in the door the same shape as the body (a flat or two flats, or a square) so the body can't spin, and drive the cam from the spindle with a square or flat rather than a round shaft. Keep a real screw holding the cam on (Southco's CM cams use a screw and lock washer; a printed Rittal replacement holds its cam with an M4 screw). Print the cam lying flat so the [layer lines](../glossary.md#layer-lines) run along the arm, since a cam printed standing up would snap along a layer ⚑ [uncertain: design suggestion, not tested]. A printed replacement cam for a mailbox lock was made thicker to be as strong as the metal one and came with spare washers to shim it into line with the frame edge (Zer0s). To replace a broken enclosure lock, a printed copy of Rittal's standard cam lock (cylinder, body, cam and retaining ring) fits a 22 mm square hole and is interchangeable with the original parts (snd). Test the grip: turn the cam by hand with the door shut and add or remove washers until it just slides behind the frame lip without binding.

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Cam locks (flat-key, tubular-key or tool-operated; keyed alike or keyed differently; fixed or removable core), quarter-turn cam latches (wing knob, T-handle, L-handle, flush wing, slotted, hex, triangle, square and double-bit heads; fixed or adjustable grip; sealed or non-sealed; detented), compression latches, push-to-close cam latches, spring-cam latches, and loose cams (straight, offset or cranked, hooked; in several lengths and offsets), stop washers, sealing washers and gaskets, and keeper brackets. McMaster-Carr lists 'Wing-Handle Cam Latches', 'L-Handle Cam Latches' and 'T-Handle Cam Latches', with compression, padlockable and spring-cam versions. ## Rules of thumb

- Work out the total grip first: everything between the latch's mounting face and the point where the cam touches the frame, including door thickness, compressed seals and gaskets, accessories and frame offset; then pick the cam for that grip (Southco E5 catalogue, p. 151, https://media.southco.com/media/static/Literature/E5.en.pdf).
- Southco E5 cams come in 2 mm steps of grip, each covering +2/-0 mm; they are 45 mm long and fit doors 1-6 mm thick through a 22.5 mm hole trimmed by a 20.1 mm square so the body can't turn (same catalogue, p. 152).
- Maximum static loads for Southco E5: 330 N for zinc, 550 N for 304 stainless, 670 N for 316 stainless and 130 N for the mini size; mounting nut [torque](../glossary.md#torque) up to 10 N·m (zinc) or 20 N·m (stainless) (same catalogue, pp. 152-158).
- Southco CM cam locks fit a 19 ± 0.2 mm hole with two flats 16.25-16.35 mm apart (a double-D hole); tighten the nut to 5 N·m and the cam screw to 4 N·m; cams come with 0, 3.2, 6.4 or 9.5 mm offset and 19, 31.8 or 44.5 mm length; average ultimate load on the cam 180 N (Southco CM catalogue, pp. 196-199, https://media.southco.com/media/static/Literature/CM.en.pdf).
- Compression latches use a half turn: the first 90° swings the cam behind the frame and the rest pulls the door in by 4.3-6.4 mm (depending on model) to squeeze the gasket; maximum static load 300 N for fixed grip and 450 N for adjustable grip on the E3 (Southco E3 catalogue, pp. 90-91, https://media.southco.com/media/static/Literature/E3.en.pdf).
- Cam locks can be set to turn 90° or 180° with removable stop washers and usually fit a double-D hole; quarter-turn latches turn 90° only and fit a square hole with rounded corners (SPEP, https://spep.com/blog/comparison-of-cam-locks-and-quarter-turn-latches/).
- Offset cams are reversible, so one cam can serve two grips or turn directions by flipping it (Wikipedia, 'Latch', Cam lock section, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latch).

## Common failures

(1) Wrong grip: the cam misses the frame lip or binds before it finishes turning; measure the total grip including any gasket, then pick the cam offset or use washers (Southco; Zer0s). (2) The body spins in the door: use a D-shaped or double-D hole, or a square one, matching the latch (Southco CM, E5). (3) The cam screw loosens and the cam turns on its spindle or drops off: tighten it to the stated torque (4 N·m on Southco CM). (4) The door rattles or the cam [creeps](../glossary.md#creep) open with vibration: use a detented, ramped or compression version (Southco). (5) Over-tightening the mounting nut cracks plastic panels or strips the thread: follow the torque limit (10 N·m on zinc E5) ⚑ [uncertain: cracking of plastic panels not documented in a source]. (6) The lock cylinder breaks (snd, printed Rittal replacement). (7) Printed cams snap: print solid in ABS or PETG and make the arm thicker (1944GPW; Zer0s).

## Safety

Tool-operated or keyed heads keep untrained people out of electrical enclosures and machinery (Southco sells tool-operated versions 'for security'); use them where a panel hides live parts or moving machinery. A lost key or a broken lock cylinder can leave a door stuck shut (a printed Rittal replacement notes the lock 'usually breaks in the lock cylinder'); keep spare keys and don't use a keyed latch on anything someone might need to open in an emergency. Fingers can be pinched between the cam and the frame if you turn it with the door half shut ⚑ [uncertain: general caution].

## Related

- Quarter-turn fastener (Dzus-type): a stud in a removable panel that twists into a spring or cam on the frame; for lift-off panels rather than hinged doors
- Compression latch: a cam latch whose cam also pulls back to squeeze a gasket (covered in this entry)
- Draw latch ([over-centre](../glossary.md#over-centre) latch): mounted on the outside and pulls a lid tight with a lever; no hole through the lid
- Turn button (cupboard turn): a flat bar screwed onto the frame that you swing over the door edge; the simplest surface-mounted relative
- Push-push latch (push-to-open): opens with a push, with no knob or key
- Magnet joint (magnetic catch): holds a light door shut with no turning, but gives no security
- Pin-tumbler lock: the key mechanism often built into a cam lock's body
- Bayonet mount (push-and-twist lock): the same short-twist idea for joining two round parts end to end
