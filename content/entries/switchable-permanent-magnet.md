A permanent magnet inside a steel housing that you switch on and off by hand: turning a knob rotates a magnet so its pull either flows out through the base into the steel you want to hold (on) or loops round harmlessly inside the housing (off).

**In plain words:** A strong magnet with an on/off knob or lever: turn it one way and it grabs steel hard, turn it back and it lets go, with no batteries or wires. **Proper term:** Switchable permanent magnet (switchable magnetic base, mechanically switched permanent magnet).

**Also called:** switchable magnet / switchable magnetic device / magnetic switchable device (MSD); magnetic base / mag base / on-off magnetic base (for dial indicators and test indicators); magnetic stand (machine-shop measuring stands with an on/off lever); on/off magnet / switch magnet / magnetic switch (maker name for printed versions; not the same as a reed switch); switchable magnetic clamp / magnetic jig clamp / magnetic featherboard base (woodworking); welding magnet with on/off switch; permanent magnetic lifter / manual lifting magnet (with a turn lever); manually operated (permanent) magnetic chuck (grinding and milling work holding); Magswitch, MagJig, MagSquare (brand, Magswitch Technology); Mitutoyo magnetic stand (brand of measuring stands).

## How it works

Magnetism prefers to flow through iron and steel rather than air, so the housing acts like pipes for it. In the simple single-magnet base, a round magnet sits in a hole between two iron halves joined by brass or aluminium: turned one way, each pole faces one iron half, so the halves become the magnet's poles and a steel surface [bridging](../glossary.md#overhang--bridging) them completes the circuit and is pulled on hard; turned 90 degrees, both poles face the non-magnetic joint, the iron halves just short the magnet out (like a keeper) and almost nothing reaches outside. In the two-magnet design (Magswitch type) a fixed lower disc and a rotating upper disc sit inside steel side walls: turning the knob 180 degrees lines the discs up so they act as one strong magnet (on), or sets them opposite so their fields loop from one magnet through the walls into the other and cancel outside (off). No power is used in either state; you only put in the effort of turning the knob.

## Parts

A small assembly that acts as one unit: one or two permanent magnets (usually neodymium discs magnetised across their diameter), a steel or iron housing or pole pieces that carry the magnetism to the base, a non-magnetic spacer (brass or aluminium) in simple bases, a shaft and knob or lever to turn the moving magnet, and often a [detent](../glossary.md#detent) or lock pin to hold the on and off positions.

## Everyday examples

- the magnetic base with an on/off lever that holds a dial indicator on a lathe or milling machine
- magnetic featherboards and jig clamps that lock onto a cast-iron table-saw top with a twist of a knob
- welding magnets and hand lifting magnets in metal shops that are switched off to release the steel
- the magnetic table (permanent magnetic chuck) on a surface grinder, switched by a lever

## When to use it

When you need a strong hold on steel that you can put on and take off in seconds, anywhere on the surface, with no power and no holes: jigs, fences and featherboards on cast-iron machine tables, measuring stands, welding set-ups, temporary brackets on a steel workbench or frame, camera or phone mounts on steel surfaces, and lifting or carrying steel sheet. Also when an always-on magnet would be a nuisance: it can be stored, carried and positioned while off, so it does not snap onto tools or pick up swarf.

## When not to use it

Not on aluminium, wood, plastic or most stainless steel (add a steel plate, or use a vacuum/suction gripper or a clamp instead). Not where large sideways, twisting or peeling loads act on it: add a fence, dowel or lip, or use a toggle clamp or bolts. Not for small, light, always-on catches such as box lids (a plain embedded magnet or magnetic catch is simpler). Not when a machine must release it automatically: use a holding electromagnet (lets go when power is cut) or an electropermanent magnet (switched by an electric pulse). Not for lifting over people or on thin, curved or dirty steel unless it is a rated lifting magnet used within its safe working load.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: clamps a jig, fixture, measuring stand, featherboard or workpiece to any flat steel surface
  - lock, and release by hand: a quarter or half turn of the knob or lever switches the hold on or off
- **Movement:** rotate: only the knob and internal magnet turn, usually 90 degrees (single-magnet base) or 180 degrees (two-magnet Magswitch type); the joint itself does not move while on
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 while switched on and seated on steel (held against pulling off and, less strongly, against sliding); 6 when switched off (the part simply lifts away).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - quick-release: switched off by turning the knob, then lifted away; switched on again anywhere on steel with no tools
  - hand-release only (partly): it keeps holding until a person turns the knob; power cuts cannot affect it because it uses none
- **How it holds:** magnetic (permanent-magnet pull, switched mechanically); sideways ([shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear)) hold comes only from friction between the base and the steel
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive: no power needed to hold, switch on or switch off; only hand effort on the knob or lever
- **Load:** Light to heavy depending on size: printed DIY versions hold a few kilograms (one 10-magnet printed base held 3.6 kg upside down); dial-indicator stands are typically rated about 60 kgf of magnetic pull; woodworking clamps are sold as up to 95 lb, 150 lb or 400 lb; hand-held lifting versions hold tens of kilograms safely. Strong against pulling straight off a thick, flat, clean steel surface; much weaker against sliding (one hand lifter is rated 89.4 kg breakaway but only 20.4 kg safe shear) and against peeling or prying; much weaker on thin, painted, rusty or curved steel.
- **Play and precision:** No play once on; it holds the part wherever it is set down, so it is not a precise locator. Rough, curved or dirty surfaces reduce contact and grip. For repeatable positions add a fence, dowel or kinematic seat against which the magnet clamps.
- **Taking it apart:** tool-free quick release
- **How often it can be reused:** effectively unlimited ⚑ [uncertain: the magnets are not worn out by switching, but no cycle rating was found; wear is in the knob, bearing, detent or lock pin and the base face]
- **Sealing:** none: it holds but does not seal
- **Adjustable:** No adjustment of force in most (fully on or fully off); position is set by where you put it down. Some industrial lifters and chucks are manually set to different strengths ⚑ [uncertain: not checked for hobby-size units].
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind): it grabs a steel surface from one side
- **Materials:** Inside: neodymium (NdFeB) magnets, usually magnetised across the diameter or across the width, in a mild-steel or iron housing or pole pieces, with brass or aluminium spacers in simple bases; plastic or aluminium knob. The target must be ferromagnetic: plain steel or cast iron (thick, flat, bare steel works best). It does not grip aluminium, brass, wood, plastic or most (austenitic) stainless steel; screw a steel plate onto such parts. Printed DIY versions use PLA or PETG housings with plain or nickel-plated steel nuts, washers or rods as pole pieces (not stainless washers, which are not magnetic).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Neodymium magnets lose strength when hot and can be permanently weakened above about 80 °C (K&J Magnetics), so keep them away from freshly welded or heat-treated parts; lifting-magnet guides warn that many rare-earth magnets lose capacity above 80 °C and some grades fail above 120 °C (AIMS vendor guide). Water and dust do not stop it working, but rust on the base or target adds an air gap and reduces grip. The hold does not fade over time because nothing is powered, but vibration can walk it sideways because sliding resistance is only friction; lifters use a lock pin in the on position 'to prevent accidental release under vibration' (AIMS). Printed housings (PLA especially) soften in heat and sunlight (Prusa lists PLA as low in temperature and UV resistance).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: magnetic bases and stands, switchable magnetic clamps (woodworking and welding), manual lifting magnets, permanent magnetic chucks
  - 3D print (FDM): housings, knobs and bases for DIY switchable magnets; jigs, featherboards and templates built around a bought switchable magnet
  - woodwork: routing a pocket so a bought switchable magnet sits inside a jig or featherboard
  - CNC: iron pole pieces and housings for machined versions
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: printed switchable magnets are printed as separate top and bottom housings; the magnets and steel pole pieces are pressed or glued in, then the halves are pressed or snapped together
- **Tools:** none to use (turn the knob or lever by hand); a screwdriver or bolts to fix the magnet into a jig or stand; for a home-made one, a 3D printer, strong magnets, steel pole pieces (bolts, rods, nuts or washers), glue and a vice or clamp to press parts together
- **Difficulty:** moderate: a printed version needs correctly oriented strong magnets, steel pole pieces and tight press or snap fits that resist the magnets' pull; K&J Magnetics found assembly 'a real challenge' and more expensive in parts than buying a ready-made unit, so buy one for heavy or safety-critical holding

### 3D printing tips

How printed versions are built: two printed discs or rings stacked on a central axle, each holding a ring of small magnets, with steel pole pieces (hex nuts, rod slices or stacked washers) standing between the magnets and passing through both layers; turning the top [layer lines](../glossary.md#layer-lines) the magnets up (on) or opposes them (off). Andrew Klein's design ('3D Print a Magnetic Switch, and how it works') inspired many remixes; a Printables remix by povsky uses 24 small 10 x 5 x 3 mm magnets and 12 #10 hex nuts with an internal locking feature and snap-fit assembly, and a parametric remix by bnorick lets you set magnet diameter, depth, [tolerance](../glossary.md#tolerance) and body tolerance. (1) Magnet direction matters most: the magnets must be magnetised across the face you mount against the steel pieces, not through their thickness; JCisMe warns that a design for magnets polarised across their length 'WILL NOT WORK if the north is on one large face', and that thick magnets leak more in the off position. Test each pair before gluing: JCisMe's check is to place two magnets large face to large face (attracting) and rotate one while keeping the faces touching; correctly magnetised magnets start to repel close to 180 degrees. (2) Use plain or nickel-plated steel pole pieces; stainless washers are not magnetic (RemiRafael's printed magnetic base uses 1 mm steel washers stacked to 3 mm, or 12 mm steel bar slices). (3) Print the magnet pockets as a snug press fit and glue them: the magnets fight each other hard in the off position and will twist or pop out of loose pockets; greasing the rotating cylinder helps the final press-fit (JCisMe) and a bench vice helps seat press-fit parts fully (povsky). (4) Add a detent or stop so the knob sits firmly at on and off; printed designs include an internal lock (povsky) and tab stops (JCisMe). (5) Keep the steel pole pieces flush with or just proud of the base: any plastic skin between them and the target is an air gap that cuts the grip. (6) Orientation: print the housings flat on the bed; povsky notes the handle body prints without supports if its orientation is reversed. PLA works for demonstrations and light jigs (Prusa lists PLA as having low temperature and UV resistance); PETG is tougher for tools that get knocked about. (7) Expect a few kilograms, not tens: RemiRafael's 10-magnet base (12 x 3 mm magnets) held 3.6 kg upside down and the 20-magnet version more than 5 kg. For woodworking jigs it is usually better to print a holder, featherboard or template around a bought switchable magnet (many such models exist for the Magswitch MagJig 95 and 150).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Switchable magnetic base / magnetic stand for dial indicators (on/off lever, V-groove base for round bars); switchable magnetic clamp / magnetic jig clamp (Magswitch MagJig 95 and 150, MagSquare 400); switchable welding magnet / on-off welding angle magnet; manual permanent magnetic lifter (lever-operated lifting magnet, rated by safe working load); manually operated permanent magnetic chuck (surface grinding, milling); for DIY builds: diametrically magnetised neodymium disc or ring magnets, block magnets magnetised through the width, steel hex nuts, steel dowel or drill-rod slices, steel washers, brass or aluminium spacer, axle screw and knob.

## Rules of thumb

- Rated pull is measured on very thick, clean, flat steel in ideal conditions; plan on lower numbers, especially on thin sheet (a 400 lb unit on an I-beam holds far less on a thin filing cabinet), painted or rusty surfaces and irregular shapes (K&J Magnetics, https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/magswitch).
- Sideways force is the weak direction: 'You're not going to be able to apply 150 lb of shear or sideways force against an MJ-150 and expect it not to move' (K&J Magnetics, https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/magswitch). Example: the Magswitch Hand Lifter 60 is rated 197 lb (89.4 kg) maximum breakaway on 2 in thick SAE 1018 steel, 66 lb (30 kg) safe working load and only 45 lb (20.4 kg) maximum safe shear (Magswitch spec sheet, https://downloads.magswitch.com/products/110673-8800487-Spec-Sheet.pdf).
- Thin steel cuts the grip: the same hand lifter needs 1/4 in (6.35 mm) steel for 'full saturation', and its safe working load chart (at a 3:1 safety factor) falls steadily for sheets from 3/8 in down to 26 gauge (0.4 mm) (Magswitch spec sheet, https://downloads.magswitch.com/products/110673-8800487-Spec-Sheet.pdf).
- For anything lifted, use a safety factor: lifting magnets are rated at a safe working load of about one third of breakaway force (3:1; 2.5:1 to 3.5:1 across brands), and a flat base on round bar or pipe keeps only about 30-50% of its flat-plate rating (AIMS Industrial lifting-magnet guide, vendor source, https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/magnetic-lifter-guide; Magswitch charts SWL 'at 3:1').
- Surface layers act as air gaps: the AIMS guide states a 0.5 mm layer of rust scale or paint 'can reduce capacity by 30-50%' (vendor source, https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/magnetic-lifter-guide).
- Keep neodymium switchable magnets below about 80 °C (176 °F) or they can be partly demagnetised (K&J Magnetics, https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/magswitch).
- Measuring stands: a typical dial-indicator magnetic stand is rated at 60 kgf of magnetic pull, switched by an on/off lever (Mitutoyo 7010SN listing, https://www.globaltestsupply.com/product/mitutoyo-7010sn-stand-magnetic-60kgf).
- Switch on only when it is in contact with the steel: Magswitch labels its lifter 'Do Not Operate Unless In Contact With Ferrous Target', and K&J advises the same to avoid pinched fingers (https://downloads.magswitch.com/products/110673-8800487-Spec-Sheet.pdf; https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/magswitch).
- DIY benchmark: a printed base with ten 12 x 3 mm magnets and steel washer pole pieces held 3.6 kg upside down; a 20-magnet version held more than 5 kg (RemiRafael, Printables model 1839898, read via the Printables API).

## Common failures

(1) Weak hold: thin, painted, rusty, dirty or curved steel, or a small contact patch; clean the surfaces and use thick flat steel, or add a steel plate. (2) Slides under sideways load: shear capacity is a fraction of pull-off force; add a fence, pin or lip. (3) Peels off when levered: a long arm on a stand or jig pries one edge up; keep loads close to the base. (4) Does not switch fully off or keeps a small residual pull: often a design or assembly issue in DIY builds (mismatched magnets, wrong magnetisation direction, thick magnets or missing steel pole pieces); check each magnet's direction before gluing. (5) DIY magnets twist or pop out of their printed pockets: glue them and use tighter pockets. (6) Knob [creeps](../glossary.md#creep) from on towards off: add a detent or lock. (7) Lost strength after heating above about 80 °C: the neodymium was partly demagnetised; replace it.

## Safety

Pinch hazard: switched on while loose it can jump onto steel and trap fingers, so switch on only when already in contact (K&J; Magswitch labels its lifter 'Do Not Operate Unless In Contact With Ferrous Target'). Never lift loads over people, and make sure a lifting magnet's lever is fully on and locked. Loose neodymium magnets used in DIY builds are [brittle](../glossary.md#brittle--ductile) and can shatter if they slam together (wear eye protection), can pinch skin badly, can affect pacemakers and implanted devices, and are very dangerous if swallowed, because swallowed magnets can attract through intestine walls (K&J Magnetics safety page). Keep them away from children.

## Related

- Magnet joint (embedded magnets, magnetic catch): always on and pulled apart by hand; simpler and cheaper for light holding
- Electropermanent magnet: the same on/off idea, but an electric pulse flips one magnet instead of a hand turning a knob
- Holding electromagnet (maglock): needs power all the time it holds and lets go when power is cut
- Vacuum (suction) gripper: grips non-steel smooth surfaces but needs a pump or a sealed cup
- Toggle clamp: clamps any material mechanically with a lever; needs a place to bolt it down
- [Kinematic coupling](../glossary.md#kinematic-coupling) (balls in V-grooves): locates a part exactly; magnets are often used only to hold it down ([preload](../glossary.md#preload))
- Manually operated permanent magnetic chuck: the same principle in a flat table form, with rows of poles to hold workpieces for grinding and milling
