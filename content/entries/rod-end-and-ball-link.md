A threaded end fitting holding a swivelling ball (a spherical bearing) or a snap-on ball cup, fitted at each end of a push rod or tie rod so the rod can pass push and pull between two levers that move out of line, with the rod length set by how far the ends are screwed on.

**In plain words:** A small swivelling ball joint on the end of a push rod, so the rod can push and pull a lever even when the two aren't perfectly lined up; it screws onto the rod, so you can make the rod longer or shorter. **Proper term:** Rod end (rod end bearing, heim joint or rose joint) and ball link.

**Also called:** heim joint (North America) and rose joint (UK) (Wikipedia; named after Lewis R. Heim and Rose Bearings); rod end bearing, spherical rod end; tie-rod end or tie rod with rod ends (steering links); ball link, ball cup and ball stud, ball end (RC cars, planes and helicopters); turnbuckle (a rod with a right-hand thread at one end and a left-hand thread at the other, carrying two rod ends or ball cups; RC camber and toe links); angle joint or angled ball joint (DIN 71802, made of a ball socket DIN 71805 and a ball stud DIN 71803); ball stud and socket (gas-strut end fitting; SpringFix); RC pushrod ends covered here: Z-bend, L-bend with a keeper (E/Z Link (brand), FasLink (brand)), snake bend, threaded or solder-on clevis (nylon or steel), set-screw pushrod connector (E/Z Connector (brand), Screw-Lock connector (brand)); [linkage](../glossary.md#linkage) stopper (seller name for a set-screw pushrod connector) ⚑ [uncertain].

## How it works

The ball has a hole for the bolt and sits in a matching spherical seat in the housing, so the bolt can turn and also tilt within a cone while the housing stays in line with the rod (Wikipedia). Because both ends of the rod can swivel, a rod with a rod end at each end carries force only along its length, pushing or pulling, and cannot carry sideways force or twist (Wikipedia). The threaded shank screws into or onto the rod, so turning it changes the rod's length, and a jam nut locks it (Wikipedia; SKF). RC turnbuckles have opposite threads at their two ends, so turning the rod lengthens or shortens the link without unhooking either end (AMain Hobbies). On a ball link the plastic cup springs over the ball and grips it; on the Z-bend the wire's double bend hooks through a hole in the servo arm so it cannot pull out (flyrc).

## Parts

A few parts per end. Metal rod end: a housing with a male or female threaded shank, a ball with a hole through it (the swivel), sometimes a liner (PTFE or bronze) between ball and housing, plus a bolt and a lock nut (jam nut) (Wikipedia; SKF). RC ball link: a plastic ball cup screwed onto a threaded rod and snapped over a metal ball stud that is bolted to the lever (flyrc). Industrial angle joint: ball socket, ball stud and a snap ring, with an optional safety clip (Elesa+Ganter; JW Winco). A complete link is a rod or turnbuckle with an end at each end. Simpler RC pushrod ends: a Z-bend in the wire, a clevis on a threaded rod, or a set-screw connector on the servo arm.

## Everyday examples

- RC car camber links and steering rods: turnbuckles with plastic ball cups on metal ball studs (AMain Hobbies)
- RC plane and helicopter control rods from the servo arm to the control horn (Model Aviation; Wikipedia)
- Gas struts on lift-up lids, hatches and gates that clip onto a ball stud (SpringFix)
- Race car, go-kart and motorcycle steering and suspension links (Wikipedia)

## When to use it

Use rod ends or ball links whenever a push rod joins two levers or arms that don't move in exactly the same plane, or whose mounting points aren't perfectly lined up: RC steering and suspension, control surfaces, linkages driven by a servo or [actuator](../glossary.md#actuator), gas struts and adjustable tie rods. They also let you set the rod length precisely and change it later without making new parts. The snap-on ball-and-socket type is handy where a link must be unclipped quickly without tools.

## When not to use it

Avoid them where the joint must carry sideways (bending) loads on the shank or large loads along the bolt: use a clevis or a proper bearing, or arrange the mounting so the rod end sees only push and pull (SKF; DesignJudges). Avoid snap-on ball cups where a pull could pop them off, unless you add a safety clip or a washer (JW Winco; flyrc). Where parts move in one plane only and must be stiff, a clevis or plain pin joint has less slop. For long or curved routes use a push-pull cable (Bowden cable). On small, light RC models, Z-bends and set-screw connectors are simpler, but a Z-bend can't be adjusted and a [set screw](../glossary.md#set-screw) can slip (flyrc).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): swivels and tilts at each end of a push rod
  - join and hold: connects a rod to a lever, servo arm, control horn or bracket
  - change the kind of motion: as the joints of a push-rod linkage it lets a turning servo arm or lever push and pull a flap, steering arm or suspension part
- **Movement:** multiple: turns freely about its bolt and tilts within a cone, e.g. 15° (with safety catch) or 18° (without) for DIN 71802 angle joints (Elesa+Ganter; JW Winco); high-misalignment rod ends with spacers or bushings allow much more, 40° or more (DesignJudges)
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 3: it can turn in all three directions about the ball's centre (a spherical joint), with the tilt limited to a cone. A Z-bend or clevis end has only 1 (it turns about the hole).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - self-aligning or self-centring: the ball takes up [misalignment](../glossary.md#misalignment) between the rod and the lever, so the joint doesn't bind as the parts move through their arcs
  - quick-release: RC ball cups and DIN 71802 ball sockets snap on and off a ball stud without tools; a safety clip can be added when the pull-off force is not enough (JW Winco)
- **How it holds:**
  - shape (the parts interlock; [form-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): the ball is trapped in its housing, so a metal rod end cannot come apart
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): plastic ball cups and ball sockets grip the ball through the springiness of the cup or a snap ring, which sets their pull-off force (JW Winco)
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): jam nuts lock the thread, and set-screw pushrod connectors clamp the wire with a screw (flyrc)
- **Input and output:** Push or pull along the rod -> the same push or pull at the other end; with a lever at each end, rotation -> rotation, with the ratio set by the lengths of the two [lever arms](../glossary.md#lever-arm) (usual linkage behaviour) ⚑ [uncertain]. Friction is low (ball links give a 'low-friction, slop-free connection'; Model Aviation). Back-drivable: yes. The levers can be parallel or at an angle, as long as each end stays inside its tilt cone.
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Medium to heavy along the shank (push and pull), which is what rod ends are made for (SKF). Weak sideways: the load component at right angles to the shank should never exceed 0.1 of the static load rating C0, because it bends the shank (SKF catalogue). Weak along the bolt: [axial](../glossary.md#axial--radial) loads are typically only about 10% of the radial rating (Aurora Bearing, quoted in Dragzine) or 5-10% (DesignJudges). A link with rod ends at both ends carries force only along its length (Wikipedia). RC plastic ball links, clevises and set-screw connectors are light-duty; flyrc suggests set-screw connectors only on '90-size planes and smaller'.
- **Play and precision:** None to small when new: PTFE-lined rod ends have zero [clearance](../glossary.md#clearance), while metal-on-metal ones always have a little (Aurora Bearing via Dragzine); RC ball links are 'slop-free' when new but wear (Model Aviation); igus plastic rod ends deliberately have a large bearing clearance (igus). Misalignment is its strength: it tolerates tilt within its cone (15-18° for DIN 71802, more with high-misalignment spacers; DesignJudges) and small offsets. Slop grows as the ball or cup wears; replace any bearing that develops slop (DesignJudges).
- **Taking it apart:** removable: unbolt the rod end or pop the ball cup off its stud; a Z-bend can only be removed by taking off the servo arm or horn it hooks through (flyrc)
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: metal rod ends and bolts can be refitted again and again; plastic RC ball cups wear and should be replaced when they show wear (Model Aviation); plastic set-screw connector holders should not be reused (flyrc)
- **Sealing:** none: open rod ends and RC ball links let dirt in, and wear then adds play ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Adjustable:** Yes: screw the rod end or ball cup further on or off the threaded rod and lock it with a jam nut (Wikipedia; SKF); turnbuckles with opposite threads adjust length without disconnecting (AMain Hobbies); RC clevises screw along a threaded pushrod (Model Aviation); set-screw connectors slide along the wire and clamp (flyrc). Z-bends and solder-on clevises are not adjustable (flyrc).
- **Access needed:** both sides when a bolt passes through the ball and takes a nut; one side only when the ball stud screws into a tapped lever and the cup snaps on from the front (RC ball links, DIN 71802 angle joints)
- **Materials:** Metal rod ends: steel body, ball and race, heat-treated in the best ones, either PTFE-lined or metal-on-metal (Aurora Bearing via Dragzine); SKF makes steel/steel, steel/bronze and steel/PTFE combinations (SKF). Plastic: igus igubal polymer rod ends, rated -40 to +200 °C and corrosion-resistant (igus). DIN 71802 angle joints: zinc-plated steel or AISI 303 stainless, with a hardened ball (Elesa+Ganter; JW Winco). RC: nylon ball cups and clevises, metal balls, steel wire or threaded rod; steel clevises can rust (Model Aviation). Printed: nylon with carbon fibre (Printables 490568), ABS (Printables 461610), nylon rather than PLA (Printables 160883).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** SKF steel/steel rod ends are rated about -50 to +200 °C (SKF catalogue, table 7); igus plastic igubal rod ends -40 to +200 °C, corrosion-resistant and suited to high-frequency vibration (igus). Steel RC clevises can rust (Model Aviation). Printed PLA starts to give at about 60 °C (CNC Kitchen) and [creeps](../glossary.md#creep) under steady load (Printables 160883). Grit gets into open rod ends and wears them, adding play ⚑ [uncertain]. Vibration loosens jam nuts and set screws; use [threadlocker](../glossary.md#threadlocker) (flyrc).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: rod ends, RC ball links, ball studs, turnbuckles, clevises and pushrod connectors are cheap and standard
  - 3D print (FDM): printable rod ends and ball joints, usually in tough materials such as nylon, sometimes printed in place
  - sheet metal: bent wire pushrods with Z-bends or L-bends made with bending pliers (Model Aviation)
  - CNC: machined turnbuckles, levers and brackets ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** partly: [print-in-place](../glossary.md#print-in-place) rod ends exist. One M6 design is printed as one part, then a bolt is pushed through the ball and tapped with a hammer to free it, giving an almost slop-free joint (Printables 160883); another prints the ball inside the housing with a 0.6 mm gap and no supports (Printables 461610). They still need a bolt through the ball and a thread cut or a nut added for the rod.
- **Tools:** Spanners for the jam nut and bolt; a hex key for set-screw connectors, plus threadlocker (flyrc); ball-link pliers or a ball-cup remover to pop RC cups off (Printables 30323 is a printable one); a turnbuckle wrench, usually included with RC models (AMain Hobbies); Z-bend pliers or a wire bender for Z-bends (Model Aviation).
- **Difficulty:** easy to fit: buy rod ends or RC ball links and screw them on; moderate to print one that works well: the ball-to-housing gap must be small and even, the material tough, and plastic softens and creeps (the designer of Printables 160883 advises buying one for anything critical)

### 3D printing tips

1) For anything that matters, buy metal rod ends or RC ball links: the designer of a printable heim joint warns that PLA softens at moderate temperatures, creeps over time and is likely to fail in critical uses, and suggests nylon (Printables 160883). 2) Print-in-place: leave a small, even gap between ball and housing, e.g. 0.6 mm (Printables 461610: ABS-based filament, 0.14 mm layers, 50% [infill](../glossary.md#infill), no supports), then free it by driving a bolt through the ball with light hammer taps (Printables 160883). 3) Print enough [perimeters](../glossary.md#perimeters) that the ring of the housing round the ball is solid plastic (Printables 160883). 4) Threads: print the shank hole slightly undersize and cut the thread with a tap, e.g. a 4.9 mm hole for M5 and a 5.1 mm hole through the ball for an M5 bolt (Printables 461610); keep the wall round a thread at least one thread diameter thick (Hubs). 5) Lubricate with a little lithium grease or petroleum jelly (Printables 160883). 6) Use tough materials: nylon with carbon fibre (Printables 490568, sized from a Traxxas truck), nylon or ABS; PLA starts to give at about 60 °C (CNC Kitchen). 7) Print the housing ring flat with the bolt hole vertical, so the push and pull along the shank runs along the layers rather than across them ⚑ [uncertain]. 8) On RC ball links, a small [O-ring](../glossary.md#o-ring) on the ball stud under the cup can take out slop by holding the cup at the right height (tip in Printables 30323) ⚑ [uncertain].

## Rules of thumb

- Rod ends are meant for loads along the shank; any load component at right angles to the shank should never exceed 0.1 x C0 (the static load rating), otherwise choose a bigger rod end (SKF, 'Spherical plain bearings and rod ends' catalogue, 2013, section 'Permissible loads for rod ends', printed p. 44).
- Lock a rod end on its threaded rod or tube with a counter lock nut tightened against the rod end or tube (SKF catalogue, mounting of rod ends, printed p. 95).
- Axial load (along the bolt) is typically about 10% of the radial rating (Aurora Bearing, in Dragzine) or 5-10% of the ultimate radial load (DesignJudges).
- A link with rod ends at both ends transmits force only along its long axis, never sideways or as a twist (Wikipedia, Rod end bearing).
- DIN 71802 angle joints swivel 15° with a safety catch and 18° without (Elesa+Ganter; JW Winco).
- Z-bend pliers suit wire up to .062 in (2-56); the E/Z Bender with swappable dies handles up to .093 in (4-40) (Model Aviation, May 2019).
- Sleeve clevises with silicone tubing, put a washer under the bolt head on a bolt-through ball link, and use threadlocker on set-screw connectors (flyrc; Model Aviation).
- Don't combine a ball link with a threaded, swinging control horn: the pushrod can twist the horn and change the linkage length (flyrc).
- Bent rod ends can't be unbent: replace them (DesignJudges).

## Common failures

Ball cups pop off their studs under load (add a washer, safety clip or a bolt-through ball) (flyrc; JW Winco); the ball or cup wears and develops slop (Model Aviation; DesignJudges); the shank bends or breaks at the thread when loaded sideways (SKF; DesignJudges); jam nuts or set screws work loose, so the length changes or the rod slips (flyrc; Model Aviation); RC clevises spring open (flyrc); metal clevises rust and linkage retainers slip over time (Model Aviation); printed rod ends creep, crack or loosen (Printables 160883).

## Safety

A rod end failing in steering, suspension or flight controls means loss of control: use metal parts, check regularly for play and bends and replace worn or bent ones (DesignJudges), and don't load the shank sideways (SKF). On critical one-sided (single-shear) mounts, makers fit a 'safety' washer or spacer that captures the housing if the ball pulls out ⚑ [uncertain]. Secure RC clevises and ball cups so they can't pop off in flight (flyrc; Model Aviation). Don't use printed rod ends for anything safety-critical (Printables 160883).

## Related

- Clevis joint: a fork and pin on the rod end; stiffer in its one plane but cannot tilt
- Ball-and-socket joint: the same spherical joint, often lockable, used for posable arms and camera heads rather than push rods
- Universal joint: passes rotation between shafts at an angle, which a rod end cannot do
- Spherical plain bearing: the same ball-in-ring bearing pressed into your own housing instead of on a threaded shank (SKF)
- Push-pull cable (Bowden cable): carries push and pull along a curved path
- Four-bar linkage: the mechanism that a push rod with a rod end at each end usually forms
- Servo saver: a sprung servo horn that gives way when the linkage is hit
- Plain pivot (pin in hole): the simplest joint when there is no misalignment
