Two pieces of soft or part-dried water-based clay are scratched (scored) where they meet, coated with slip (the same clay mixed with water to a thick cream), pressed together and blended, so that as they dry the clay knits into one piece; this is how handles, spouts, ears and other details are attached.

**In plain words:** Scratching criss-cross lines into both pieces of soft clay, painting on a creamy mix of the same clay and water, then pressing them together and smoothing over the seam so they grow into one piece. **Proper term:** Score-and-slip join (scratch and slip; luting).

**Also called:** score and slip / slip and score; scratch and slip (Wikipedia 'Slip (ceramics)'); luting: joining two pieces of dry or [leather-hard](../glossary.md#leather-hard) clay with a slip (Wikipedia 'Glossary of pottery terms'); a lute (the joint itself; Wikipedia 'Leather-hard'); slip as 'the glue of clay' (Lillian Gray); joining slip, joining clay; magic water (a sodium silicate and soda ash solution used instead of slip); magic mud, paper clay slip (slip with paper fibre added; Lakeside Pottery); vinegar join (white vinegar instead of slip; AMACO); sprigging (fixing moulded relief decoration on with slip; Wikipedia 'Slip (ceramics)').

## How it works

Damp clay sticks to damp clay, but two smooth, firm surfaces pressed together only touch at a few points and pull apart as they dry and shrink. Scoring both surfaces with criss-cross lines roughens them so they key together, and the slip, a thick clay-and-water paste, fills the scratches so the clay particles of both pieces meet and mingle (Wikipedia 'Slip (ceramics)'). Pressing and blending the seam, and often rolling a thin coil into the corner, makes a smooth, solid join. The join only stays whole if both parts are at about the same dampness and the piece dries slowly and evenly, because clay shrinks as it dries and parts that shrink at different rates pull apart (Wikipedia 'Leather-hard'; TeachKidsArt). Vinegar or magic water can replace slip: vinegar 'breaks down the clay a bit, and makes it sticky' (AMACO), and magic water is a deflocculant that lets clay particles pack tightly while staying fluid (Potclays).

## Parts

A few parts: the two clay pieces, the slip (or vinegar, or magic water), a scoring tool (needle tool, fork, serrated rib, toothbrush or taped toothpicks), a brush, and optionally a thin coil of clay pressed round the seam as a reinforcing [fillet](../glossary.md#chamfer--fillet).

## Everyday examples

- the handle on a handmade mug
- the spout and lid knob of a teapot
- ears, nose and lips added to a school clay mask or animal (Lillian Gray)
- raised moulded decoration on Jasperware (sprigging; Wikipedia 'Slip (ceramics)')

## When to use it

Whenever two pieces of water-based clay (pottery clay, paper clay or air-dry clay) are joined while still soft or leather-hard: handles, spouts, knobs, feet, slab boxes, coil pots, and modelled details on sculptures and masks. Vinegar or magic water are handy when several clay bodies are in use and the right slip isn't mixed (AMACO), or when joining parts at slightly different dampness (Lakeside Pottery). Paper clay slip ('magic mud') is for more extreme cases, such as joining wet clay to drier clay or repairing bone-dry pieces (Lakeside Pottery).

## When not to use it

Not for polymer clay: use the Liquid-clay bond. Not for pieces that are already fully dry and not paper clay: for air-dry clay use PVA glue (Art Shed); for dry pottery clay try paper clay slip, which can repair bone-dry pieces (Lakeside Pottery), or remake the piece. Not for fired ceramics or tiles (kiln firing and fired-ceramic repair are out of scope here). Do not use vinegar on slip-cast pieces (AMACO). Not for anything that must come apart again.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - glue or bond: the clay itself is the 'glue', so the parts become one material
  - join and hold: handles, spouts, knobs, feet, ears, noses and other modelled details on pottery and clay models
- **Movement:** none
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: once dry (and fired, for pottery) the joined parts are one rigid piece
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: no lock, release or spring action; it only works while the clay is still damp enough to be blended
- **How it holds:**
  - [material bond](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond) (glue, solvent or melting): the clay particles of both pieces merge through the slip, so the join becomes the same material as the parts
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the scored grooves key into each other and a pressed-in coil wraps the seam
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** light: for pottery handles, spouts and modelled details, which carry only the weight of the piece and its contents. A well-made join is as strong as the clay around it; Wikipedia says lutes 'increase the overall strength of the pot', but no strength figures were found ⚑ [uncertain]. Weak points are a crack along the seam from uneven drying and a handle levered off by a knock
- **Play and precision:** not applicable
- **Taking it apart:** permanent once dry: it can only be undone while still soft, or by breaking it
- **How often it can be reused:** once
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** yes, while the clay is still soft (it can be pressed into a new position or pulled off and rejoined); no once dry
- **Access needed:** both sides (both meeting faces are scored and slipped before joining; a hand inside a pot supports the wall while the handle is pressed on)
- **Materials:** Suits water-based clays while they are still soft or leather-hard: pottery clay of every kind (earthenware, stoneware, porcelain) and paper clay, before firing, and air-dry clay (Art Shed, Lillian Gray, Sculpey blog). The slip must be made from the same clay as the parts, or 'different drying and shrinking rates can cause problems' (TeachKidsArt). Paper clay can even be joined dry to dry or dry to soft (Wikipedia 'Paper clay'). Not for polymer clay, which is oil-based and joined with liquid clay instead (Sculpey blog; see Liquid-clay bond), not for epoxy putty, and not for fired ceramics (out of scope here; use a ceramic repair glue). Already-dry air-dry clay is glued with PVA instead (Art Shed). Vinegar should not be used on slip-cast pieces, which it starts to dissolve (AMACO)
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Unfired pottery clay and air-dry clay soften again in water: 'As air dry clay is a water-based clay, it needs to be waterproofed to survive the elements', with acrylic gloss medium, PVA or paint, and even then 'This wont make the clay food safe' (Art Shed). Air-dry clay should not be dried in the sun, with a hairdryer or by baking, 'as this will cause cracks' (Art Shed); Sculpey's FAQ, by contrast, allows air-dry clay in the oven at a low 200 °F, warning that it tends to crack if left in too long or too hot. The two sources disagree. Fast or uneven drying cracks the join.

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - hand building and wheel-thrown pottery (before firing)
  - air-dry clay modelling
  - off-the-shelf part: ready-made magic water or a bought scoring tool
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: this is a join between soft clay parts, not a printed joint
- **Tools:** a scoring tool (needle tool, fork, serrated rib, wire brush, stiff toothbrush, or a few toothpicks taped side by side; TeachKidsArt, Pottery Supplies Online, Ceramics Monthly), a small brush for the slip, a lidded pot of slip made from the same clay, a small dowel or skewer to seal and smooth the seam (Ceramics Monthly), plastic sheet to slow drying; optionally a spray bottle of vinegar and water or ready-made magic water; PVA glue for already-dry air-dry clay
- **Difficulty:** easy: needs only a pin and some slip, and is taught to schoolchildren; the skill is in matching the dampness of the two parts and drying slowly

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Pottery or air-dry clay, ready-made slip or casting slip, scoring tool (sold as a 'scoring tool', 'scratch tool' or 'clay scorer'; also serrated ribs and needle tools), slip brush, magic water (sold ready-made or mixed from liquid sodium silicate and soda ash), white vinegar, paper clay, PVA glue, handle maker (a loop tool for cutting handle strips; Ceramics Monthly)

## Rules of thumb

- Join at the leather-hard stage, about 15% moisture, which is the easiest time to add handles and other extensions (Wikipedia 'Leather-hard')
- Make the slip from the same clay as the pieces (TeachKidsArt); 'the efficacy will vary with different clay types' for magic water too (Potclays)
- Slip consistency: 'thick but still pourable' (TeachKidsArt); for air-dry clay 'like the texture of toothpaste' (Lillian Gray)
- Score both surfaces with criss-crossing lines before slipping (Wikipedia 'Slip (ceramics)'; TeachKidsArt; Pottery Supplies Online)
- Vinegar instead of slip: white vinegar and water 'about equal parts' in a spray bottle (AMACO); one potter scores and slips twice with neat vinegar on a toothbrush (Ceramics Monthly, 'Foolproof Handles', 2021)
- Magic water: 1 gallon of water, 3 tablespoons of liquid sodium silicate and 1 1/2 teaspoons of soda ash (Lakeside Pottery); Potclays suggests starting at 1 litre of water, 1 g soda ash and 2.5 g sodium silicate, used 'quite watery' because concentrated sodium silicate can form a glaze
- After pressing the parts together, seal the edges with a small dowel, then roll a thin coil into the joint and smooth it in (Ceramics Monthly, 'Foolproof Handles')
- Air-dry clay takes 24 to 48 hours to dry depending on thickness; turn it over after 24 hours so it dries evenly (Art Shed)

## Common failures

1) The join cracks or the part falls off while drying: the two parts were at different dampness or dried at different rates (Wikipedia 'Leather-hard' describes a handle drying before the body and cracking; Lakeside Pottery blames uneven [shrinkage](../glossary.md#warping--shrinkage) between wet and leather-hard parts); match their dampness, use magic water or paper clay slip for wet-to-drier joins (Lakeside Pottery), and dry slowly under plastic. 2) Skipping the slip and using only water gives 'attachment malfunctions' (TeachKidsArt). 3) Slip made from a different clay shrinks differently and cracks (TeachKidsArt). 4) Slip too runny makes 'one mushy mess' (Lillian Gray). 5) Only one side scored: score both. 6) A hairline crack round the seam: seal the edge with a dowel and add a thin coil (Ceramics Monthly). 7) Air-dry clay cracks from drying in the sun or with a hairdryer (Art Shed). 8) Details glued to dry air-dry clay soak up the PVA: use more glue (Art Shed). 9) Magic water used too strong can leave a glaze-like layer, so use it 'quite watery' (Potclays).

## Safety

Dry the piece slowly and evenly, or the join cracks: keep it under plastic, and remember a thin handle dries faster than a thick body (Wikipedia 'Leather-hard'; Lakeside Pottery; Diamond Core Tools). Vinegar dissolves slip-cast pieces (AMACO). Clay dust contains crystalline silica, which can cause silicosis and lung cancer if breathed over time: sanding dry pieces 'should be avoided, as a wet sponge can be used instead'; if you must, do it at the end of the day, wear an approved mask (e.g. P100) and mop and sponge surfaces afterwards; dust is easily stirred up again, even by wet mopping, and vacuuming with an ordinary vacuum 'can be extremely hazardous' (Ceramics Monthly, 'Techno File: Studio Dust'). Wikipedia also warns that slip is a fine silica-based material that could be hazardous if inhaled ('Leather-hard'). Magic water contains soda ash, which forms an alkaline solution (Pottery Supplies Online), so keep it out of eyes and off skin; gloves are sensible ⚑ [uncertain]. Air-dry clay pieces sealed with paint or PVA are still not food safe (Art Shed). Firing is out of scope; note that a piece that is not completely dry can burst in a kiln (Wikipedia 'Paper clay').
