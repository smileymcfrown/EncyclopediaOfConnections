A pole made of short tubes that plug into one another (socket, spigot or ferrule joints), with an elastic cord running through all of them under light tension, so the sections stay together as one set, pull themselves into line and home, and fold up small.

**In plain words:** Short hollow tubes with a stretchy cord running through all of them, so when you shake them out the cord pulls each tube into the next and the pole almost puts itself together. **Proper term:** Shock-corded pole (shock-corded tent pole; sectional pole with ferrule joints).

**Also called:** shock-cord tent pole / shock-corded tent pole; bungee-corded pole / elastic-corded pole; folding tent pole / collapsible pole; sectional or segmented pole (US 4,979,531 calls it a 'flexible, multi-segmented support structure'); ferrule-jointed pole (the joint sleeve is the ferrule; a plugged, narrowed end is also called a spigot or insert); folding cane (a white cane whose sections are held together by an elastic cord; Ambutech; Wikipedia 'Assistive cane'); shock cord = bungee cord = elastic cord ('occy strap' in Australia) for the cord itself (Wikipedia 'Bungee cord').

## How it works

The elastic cord is anchored at both ends of the pole and is shorter than the pole (about 70% of its length; Hilleberg), so it is always slightly stretched and pulls every section towards its neighbours. When you pull the sections apart and fold them side by side, the cord stretches further; when you unfold them, it pulls each section into the socket of the next, so you only need to guide each joint and push it fully home. The cord does not make the pole strong: once assembled, the overlapping tube at each joint carries the bending and pushing loads, and the cord just keeps the joints closed and all the sections together so none get lost (US 4,979,531).

## Parts

A few kinds of part, repeated: tube sections (aluminium, fibreglass or carbon fibre); a joint at each section end, either a ferrule sleeve glued onto the tube or a narrowed insert that plugs into the next tube; one elastic shock cord running the whole length; and two end tips or end-stops that anchor the knotted cord ends (US 4,979,531; Hilleberg; NEMO).

## Everyday examples

- Dome and tunnel tent poles (Wikipedia 'Tent')
- Folding white canes, whose sections are held together by an elastic cord (Ambutech FAQ; Wikipedia 'Assistive cane')
- Folding camp chairs and other camp gear with shock-corded aluminium poles (the Printables 'Aluminum Pole Shock Cord Retainer Insert' is meant for poles on 'tents, chairs, etc')

## When to use it

When a long, light pole or rod has to pack short and go up quickly, often in the dark, cold or wind: tent and tarp poles, folding canes, collapsible props and staffs, camera or lighting poles, kite spars and portable antenna masts. The cord keeps every section with its neighbours, so none are lost and the order is always right (US 4,979,531).

## When not to use it

When the joint must resist being pulled apart along the pole, use threaded joiners or spring-button (snap button) locks, because only the elastic holds a shock-corded joint closed in tension. When the length must be adjustable, use a telescoping pole with a twist lock or flip lock. For rigid, heavily loaded frames that stay up for years (furniture, ladders), use bolted or glued joints. Avoid cheap fibreglass poles where they will be bent hard, since they tend to break at the ferrules (US 4,979,531).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: turns a bundle of short tubes into one long pole that can be bent into an arch or pushed on
  - tighten and hold tension: the stretched cord keeps pulling the joints closed and keeps all the sections together as one set (US 4,979,531)
- **Movement:** none once assembled (each joint is a rigid push-fit); while folding, each section slides out of its joint along the pole and swings round beside the next, held on the cord
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 when assembled: each joint is a snug tube-in-tube fit held closed by the cord. Pulled apart, a section can move about on its loop of cord, limited only by how far the cord stretches
- **Special behaviour:**
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): the pre-stretched cord keeps pulling sections towards each other, so the pole almost assembles itself and a folded pole stays in one bundle (US 4,979,531)
  - self-aligning or self-centring: the cord lines each section up with the next and the ferrule or spigot guides it in; you only push it fully home
  - quick-release: pull a joint apart against the cord and fold it; no tools, buttons or latches
- **How it holds:**
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the spigot or ferrule overlaps the next tube and carries bending and pushing loads
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): the stretched elastic cord pulls every joint closed
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Light to medium. Strong in bending and pushing (compression) when every joint is fully seated, which is why tent poles can be bent into arches; weak in pure pulling apart, because only the elastic holds a joint closed in tension; and weakest in bending right at the end of a stiff metal ferrule on a fibreglass pole, where 'virtually all breakage of such tent poles occurs' (US 4,979,531).
- **Play and precision:** Small play at each joint, depending on how snug the fit is; a joint that is not pushed fully home is loose and overstressed when the pole is bent ⚑ [uncertain]. The cord returns the sections to the same order and alignment every time; sections must be restrung in their original order (Kelty; Hilleberg). A seated joint tolerates almost no angle, which is the point.
- **Taking it apart:** tool-free quick release: pull each joint apart against the cord and fold
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: the tubes last for years, but the elastic cord slowly stretches out or snaps and is replaced when it goes limp (NEMO; Kelty); folding-cane cord has a natural rubber core that deteriorates with age (Ambutech)
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** Cord tension only: retie the knot at an end tip to shorten or lengthen the cord (iFixit; Backpacking Light forum). The pole's length is fixed by its sections, though a broken section can be swapped for a spare (Hilleberg).
- **Access needed:** end of a tube or shaft: the cord is anchored, tensioned and replaced at the pole's end tips
- **Materials:** Tubes of aluminium alloy (the better poles), pultruded fibreglass (cheap poles, usually under 1 cm outside diameter; Wikipedia 'Tent') or carbon fibre; ferrules usually steel, glued onto the sections (US 4,979,531). Cord: shock (bungee) cord of rubber strands inside a woven cotton or polypropylene cover (Wikipedia 'Bungee cord'), usually 1/8 in (about 3 mm) thick for tent poles (NEMO; Kelty). End tips of plastic or metal. Maker versions: PVC, aluminium or carbon tube with printed joiners, end caps and cord anchors.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** The cord's rubber core deteriorates with age (Ambutech) and faster when stored hot, such as in an attic (Backpacking Light forum); tent-pole cord eventually stretches out or snaps and needs replacing (NEMO; Kelty). Sunlight (UV) and ozone also age rubber and the cord's cover ⚑ [uncertain]. In the cold the elastic stiffens and fibreglass becomes more brittle ⚑ [uncertain]. Aluminium poles can corrode if packed away wet or salty ⚑ [uncertain]. Printed end caps and plugs kept in hot cars or sun should be ABS or similar rather than PLA (Printables 'Aluminum Pole Shock Cord Retainer Insert').

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: tent-pole sections, ferrules, shock cord, end tips and repair sleeves are sold as spares (Tent Pole Technologies; Hilleberg)
  - 3D print (FDM): end caps, cord anchors and joiners for PVC, aluminium or fibreglass tube (Printables models)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the tubes are usually bought, and printed joiners, end caps and cord anchors are separate parts glued or pushed onto the tube and then strung with cord
- **Tools:** None to use. To restring: scissors, a clamp or locking pliers to hold the stretched cord, pliers to pull out the end tips, a lighter to heat-seal the cord ends, and a bent wire, bobby pin or string to fish the cord through (NEMO; Hilleberg; iFixit; Backpacking Light forum)
- **Difficulty:** easy: buy tube and shock cord, cut the sections, glue in or print joiners and end caps, and string the cord; restringing a tent pole takes a few minutes (NEMO; Hilleberg)

### 3D printing tips

1) Print only the joints and ends, and use real tube for the sections: printed end caps and cord anchors are the common maker parts (Printables 'Tent Pole Bungee End Caps', 'Aluminum Pole Shock Cord Retainer Insert'); long thin printed tubes are weak in bending ⚑ [uncertain]. 2) Anchor the cord with a knot trapped under a cap: feed the cord through the cap's hole, tie a knot, fit the cap and glue it with a flexible glue, optionally adding heat-shrink tube over it (Printables 'Tent Pole Bungee End Caps'); or stuff a double-knotted cord end into a slotted plug that fits inside the tube (Printables 'Aluminum Pole Shock Cord Retainer Insert', sized for tubes of about 10 mm inside diameter). 3) Use heat-resistant plastic for parts that live in a car or in the sun: the retainer-insert designer recommends ABS and 100% infill and printed it with the end cap on the bed and supports. 4) Make every surface the cord rubs on smooth and rounded, to stop the elastic wearing through (Printables 'Terminator for Bungee Cord'). 5) Fit: aim for a joiner that slides in snugly with no wobble; Prusa suggests starting at about 0.3 mm clearance for parts that must move and adjusting after a test print, and a looser fit for a joint that must pull apart by hand ⚑ [uncertain]. 6) Prints are stronger in one direction, like wood (Prusa): a joiner printed standing up has layer lines across the direction it bends and may snap at a layer, so use many perimeters, a generous overlap, or a metal or carbon rod or tube as the spigot ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) Chamfer or funnel the mouth of each socket so the cord can pull the next section in without catching ⚑ [uncertain]. 8) Parametric models such as the OpenSCAD end-cap generator come pre-sized for 6-16 mm poles; measure your tube's outside diameter first (Printables 'Tent Pole Bungee End Caps').

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Shock cord (elastic cord, bungee cord) in 1/8 in (3 mm) or 3/32 in; replacement tent-pole sections; ferrules sized by pole diameter (e.g. a ferrule for 8.5 mm aluminium poles); tent-pole end tips, end caps or end-stops; pole repair sleeves (splints); shock-cord replacement kits; aluminium, carbon-fibre or fibreglass tube; folding-cane repair kits (Ambutech).

## Rules of thumb

- Relaxed cord length about 70% of the assembled pole length (Hilleberg). Kelty's version: pull out about 25% of the pole length as stretch before tying off (about 75%); Backpacking Light forum users report about 65-75%, with around 75% described as the industry standard.
- Cord thickness: 1/8 in (about 3 mm) is standard for tent poles (NEMO; Kelty; Backpacking Light forum); 3/32 in saves a little weight, and 1/16 in is too light to be useful (Backpacking Light forum, Dan Durston).
- Flexible tent poles are typically 3-6 m long, made in sections about 30-60 cm long, each with a socket at one end for the next section (Wikipedia 'Tent').
- On fibreglass poles, the stiff steel ferrule causes a severe stress concentration at its end, where 'virtually all breakage' happens (US 4,979,531): keep the stiffness change at a joint gentle and don't bend a pole until every joint is seated.
- Heat-seal the cut ends of the new cord so it won't fray inside the pole (Hilleberg), and pull the knots very tight so they don't slip (NEMO).
- Keep the sections in their original order while restringing (Kelty; Hilleberg), and tie a peg or clamp to the stretched cord so it can't shoot back into the pole (Hilleberg; NEMO).
- Repair a cracked section with a repair sleeve slid over the damage, centred and taped at both ends, then replace the section later (Hilleberg pole repair sheet).
- Scale: a replacement ferrule for 8.5 mm aluminium poles is listed as about 3 in (76 mm) long (Tent Pole Technologies), i.e. roughly 4-5 tube diameters of overlap on each side of the joint (arithmetic from the listing).

## Common failures

1) Cord stretched out or perished, so joints no longer pull together: replace it with new cord at about 70-75% of the pole length (NEMO; Kelty; Hilleberg; Ambutech). 2) Cord snaps or a knot slips back inside the pole: pull knots tight, heat-seal the ends and use a clamp or peg while working (NEMO; Hilleberg). 3) Pole breaks at the end of a ferrule, especially fibreglass (US 4,979,531): seat every joint before bending, then splint the break with a repair sleeve and replace the section (Hilleberg). 4) Nicked or cracked tube ends from letting the cord slam sections together ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) Sections restrung in the wrong order so the pole doesn't fit the tent (Kelty). 6) Printed end caps pulling off, softening or wearing through the cord: glue with flexible glue, add heat-shrink, print in ABS and round every cord edge (Printables models).

## Safety

Elastic cords that snap or slip free can whip back and cause serious eye injuries (Wikipedia 'Bungee cord'), so hold the stretched cord with a clamp or peg while restringing (Hilleberg; NEMO). The cord can snap sections together hard, so keep fingers out of the gap when unfolding ⚑ [uncertain]. Broken fibreglass and carbon poles leave sharp, splintered ends (see the broken carbon pole photo on Wikimedia Commons) ⚑ [uncertain]. A pole bent into an arch stores energy and can spring out if a joint or pole end lets go ⚑ [uncertain].

## Related

- Spring button lock (snap button, push-button tube lock): a spring-loaded button locks joined or telescoping tubes so they can't pull apart
- Telescoping tube twist lock or lever (flip) lock: sections slide inside each other and clamp at any length
- Threaded (screw-together) pole joints: resist pulling apart but are slower to assemble (e.g. Printables 'Alastor's Staff - 4-Parts Threaded Pole')
- Poles linked only by a chain or non-stretch cord: keeps sections together without pulling them home (Wikipedia 'Tent' mentions poles 'linked by chain or cord')
- Constant-force spring (spring-return reel): another way to keep a line under steady tension
- Guy-line slider (friction line tensioner): tensions a tent's cords rather than holding its poles together
- Stopper knots and cord-end anchors: the knot trapped under an end tip or plug that anchors the shock cord at each end of the pole
- Wire-rope terminations: crimped stops and sleeves that anchor a steel cable end the way a knot anchors the shock cord, for poles or masts linked by non-stretch cable
