A rod threaded along its length (or at both ends) that, with nuts and washers, makes a bolt of any length, a tie rod that pulls parts together, a frame member, an axle or an adjustable spacer; a stud screws into a tapped hole and stays there so a part can be clamped over it with a nut, and a hanger bolt does the same job in wood.

**In plain words:** A long metal (or nylon) rod with a screw thread all the way along it, or just at both ends, that you cut to length and put nuts on, so it works as an extra-long bolt, a tie that pulls two things together, a frame bar, an axle or an adjustable spacer; a stud is a short one screwed into a threaded hole so a part can be slipped over it and clamped with a nut, and a hanger bolt does the same in wood. **Proper term:** Threaded rod (all-thread rod, studding) and stud (stud bolt), including the hanger bolt.

**Also called:** threaded rod, all-thread, all-thread rod (ATR), fully threaded rod, redi-rod, continuously threaded rod, TFL rod (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'; Portland Bolt 'All Thread Rods'); studding, threaded bar (UK trade names) ⚑ [uncertain]; stud, stud bolt: fully threaded stud; tap-end stud (threads of unequal length, the shorter end screwed into a tapped hole); double-end stud (equal threads at both ends); flange stud (fully threaded with chamfered ends); full-bodied and [undercut](../glossary.md#undercut) (reduced-shank, waisted) studs (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'); wheel stud: the threaded studs on a car hub that the lug nuts screw onto (Wikipedia 'Lug nut'); hanger bolt, wood hanger screw, stud screw: a wood-screw thread at one end and a machine thread at the other (Wikipedia 'Furniture screw'; The Handyman's Daughter; Wikimedia Commons 'Hanger bolt.jpg'); dowel screw, double-ended screw dowel: wood-screw threads at both ends, for joining two pieces of wood (Wikimedia Commons 'Double screw dowel.png'); coupling nut, extension nut: a long nut that joins two male threads, usually two lengths of threaded rod; reducing, sight-hole and left-hand versions (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut'); jam nut: a thin nut tightened against another nut to lock it, or a pair used to give a rod something to turn it by (Wikipedia 'Jam nut'); grades and standards to look up, not copy: ISO strength classes 4.6, 8.8 and 10.9 and stainless A2 (304) and A4 (316) for metric rod, marked by end colour (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'); ASTM A307, F1554, A193 B7, A449 and SAE J429 for inch rod, with dimensions to ASME B18.31.3 (Portland Bolt); M8 threaded rod frame: the rod-and-printed-corner frame of early RepRap 3D printers such as the Prusa Mendel (RepRap wiki 'Prusa Mendel').

## How it works

With a nut on each end, or a nut on each side of each part, tightening the nuts stretches the rod slightly and squeezes the parts between them, just as a bolt does; because the rod is threaded all along, the nuts can sit anywhere, so the same rod both sets a distance and holds it (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly'; Portland Bolt). Threaded rod is 'designed to be used in tension' (Wikipedia): it is good at pulling, but weaker and more flexible when bent than a plain bar of the same size, because the thread cuts into its section ⚑ [uncertain]. A stud's short thread is screwed into a tapped hole and left there, the part goes over it and a nut clamps it, so the tapped thread in the part is not worn by every assembly ⚑ [uncertain]. A hanger bolt's coarse wood thread is screwed into wood, leaving a machine thread sticking out for a nut, knob, threaded insert or bracket (Wikipedia 'Furniture screw'; The Handyman's Daughter). A nut on an ordinary thread won't be pushed round by a pull along the rod, because most screws are [self-locking](../glossary.md#self-locking) (efficiency below 50%; Wikipedia 'Screw (simple machine)'), but vibration can still work it loose, which is why a second nut is jammed against it (Wikipedia 'Jam nut').

## Parts

A few parts: the rod or stud, plus nuts and washers (often a nut on each side of every part it passes through, or two nuts jammed together), coupling nuts to join or extend lengths, and cap (acorn) nuts to cover ends. A hanger bolt is one piece with a wood thread at one end and a machine thread at the other. Example: the Prusa Mendel printer's frame is six lengths of M8 threaded rod for the side triangles plus front, rear and top rods, printed corner pieces, 83 M8 nuts and 93 M8 washers (RepRap wiki 'Prusa Mendel' bill of materials).

## Everyday examples

- Car wheels, held on by lug nuts screwed onto threaded wheel studs (Wikipedia 'Lug nut')
- Pipes, ducts and equipment hung from a ceiling on lengths of threaded rod (Portland Bolt)
- Table or stool legs and knobs screwed on with hanger bolts (The Handyman's Daughter)
- Early RepRap 3D printers such as the Prusa Mendel, whose frame is M8 threaded rod and printed corners (RepRap wiki)

## When to use it

Use threaded rod when you need a bolt longer than you can buy, or an odd length, since it 'can be cut to any length in the field and secured with nuts and washers' and can stand in 'when a headed bolt of the correct length is not available' (Portland Bolt); to clamp a thick stack of parts or tie two walls of a box, frame or bench together; to hang things at an adjustable height; as an adjustable spacer or levelling foot with nuts locked either side; and for quick, cheap, reconfigurable frames from rod and printed corners, as early RepRap printers did (RepRap). Use a stud where a soft or valuable part must be taken apart many times, such as a cover on an aluminium casting, so the tapped thread isn't worn ⚑ [uncertain], and a hanger bolt to give wood a machine thread for legs, knobs, pegs and brackets (The Handyman's Daughter).

## When not to use it

Not for lifting, swings or anything holding people or heavy overhead loads unless every part is rated and marked for it (HSE LOLER); use rated eye bolts, shackles and rated hanger rod and fixings. Not where it will be bent or used as a lever (it is 'designed to be used in tension', Wikipedia); use a solid bar or tube. Not as a guide rod for sliding bearings or bushings: the Prusa Mendel uses separate smooth rods for its moving axes and recommends stainless or tool steel because 'manufacturing (straightness) standards are higher' (RepRap); see Linear guides. Not as a precise lead screw: use a trapezoidal Lead screw and nut or a Ball screw. Not as a bare axle where a part rubs on the thread: carry the load on a ball bearing or a smooth sleeve. Not in a joint where the bolt carries [shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear) and must fit closely: use a bolt with a plain shank (see Ordinary screw joint). Not where something could catch on long exposed ends: cut it short or fit cap nuts.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: through-bolting thick or odd-sized parts, clamping a stack of parts, anchoring into concrete with epoxy or cast-in, and suspending pipes and equipment (Portland Bolt); frames of rod and printed corners (RepRap)
  - tighten and hold tension: a tie rod pulled tight with nuts, or rods joined with a coupling nut that can 'tighten a rod assembly inward or ... press a rod assembly outward' (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut')
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): as an axle carrying ball bearings clamped between washers and nuts, as on the Prusa Mendel's belt [idlers](../glossary.md#idler) and on printed spool holders (RepRap; Printables); the bearing should do the turning, not the thread
  - change the kind of motion: turned by a motor, plain M8 threaded rod was used as a cheap lead screw on the Prusa Mendel's Z axis (RepRap); see Lead screw and nut for proper versions
- **Movement:** none when used as a bolt, tie rod, frame or spacer; rotate when it carries bearings as an axle; rotate+slide (a turning rod drives a nut along it) when used as a crude lead screw.
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 when nuts are tightened on both sides of each part. Before locking, a nut has one combined freedom: turning it moves it along the thread. As an axle, the bearing on it has 1 (rotation).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - self-locking (holds under load, won't back-drive): an [axial](../glossary.md#axial--radial) pull can't turn a nut on an ordinary thread, because 'A screw will be self-locking if and only if its efficiency η is below 50%' and most are (Wikipedia 'Screw (simple machine)'); vibration can still loosen it, so jam nuts or locking nuts are added (Wikipedia 'Jam nut')
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): turning the nuts or a coupling nut pulls a tie rod tight and the thread holds that tension (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut')
  - motion conversion (e.g. rotary to linear): only when the rod itself is turned as a lead screw (RepRap Prusa Mendel Z axis)
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping ([force-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): nuts tightened along the rod clamp the parts between them, and [preload](../glossary.md#preload) and thread friction stop the nuts turning (Wikipedia 'Jam nut')
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the thread flanks interlock with the nut, the tapped hole or, for a hanger bolt, the wood fibres, and carry the pull
- **Input and output:** Usually not applicable (it is a fixing). When turned as a crude lead screw: rotation -> straight-line motion of a nut, one thread [pitch](../glossary.md#pitch) per turn (1.25 mm for M8 coarse thread ⚑ [uncertain]); low efficiency, so it is not back-drivable (self-locking) (Wikipedia 'Screw (simple machine)'); shaft in line with the motion. See Lead screw and nut.
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Medium to heavy in tension (a pull along the rod), set by the grade, diameter, nuts and the material being clamped; poor in bending and only moderate in shear, because it is 'designed to be used in tension' (Wikipedia) and its thread sits wherever it crosses a joint ⚑ [uncertain]. The grade matters hugely: unmarked metric rod is class 4.6 (tensile strength 400 N/mm2, yield 240 N/mm2), yellow-marked class 8.8 is twice as strong (800 and 640), and white-marked 10.9 stronger still (1000 and 900) (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'). The AISC allowable tension for ordinary threaded rod is P = 0.33 x Fu x pi x d^2 / 4 (Wikipedia); for M8 class 4.6 that gives about 6.6 kN (about 680 kgf) as a structural steel design value (calculated here), not a rating for lifting ⚑ [uncertain]. A tap-end stud in soft metal is limited by the tapped thread unless it goes in about 1 x its diameter in steel and 2 x in aluminium (Nord-Lock). A hanger bolt is limited by its wood thread's grip, like a coach screw ⚑ [uncertain]. Through printed plastic, the plastic crushes long before the rod fails, hence 'do not crush the plastic' (RepRap).
- **Play and precision:** Small: a loose nut on a thread has a little end play ([backlash](../glossary.md#backlash)), but once nuts are tightened against both sides of a part there is none. Positions are only as accurate as you set them: RepRap builders use a cut-to-length jig 'until you can just barely fit the jig J1 between them' (RepRap). The rod doesn't locate parts sideways by itself, because it sits in [clearance](../glossary.md#clearance) holes ⚑ [uncertain]. A rod that is not straight wobbles when turned: the Prusa Mendel notes wobbling 'if your threaded rods are not straight or your couplings are not aligned' (RepRap).
- **Taking it apart:** removable: nuts undo with spanners, and studs and hanger bolts can be backed out by gripping two nuts jammed together (Wikipedia 'Jam nut'; The Handyman's Daughter). Rod set in concrete with epoxy or cast in is permanent (Portland Bolt), and seized stainless threads may have to be cut off (Wikipedia '[Galling](../glossary.md#galling--fretting)').
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: steel rod and nuts can be refitted many times if the threads are kept clean and undamaged; stainless and aluminium threads can gall and seize (Wikipedia 'Galling'); hanger bolts in wood loosen their hole if taken out and refitted often ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Sealing:** none: fluid can [creep](../glossary.md#creep) along the thread; where a rod passes through a wet or pressurised wall, seal it with sealing washers or thread sealant ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Adjustable:** yes: run the nuts along the rod to change a spacing, height, length or tension, then lock them with a second nut (Wikipedia 'Jam nut'); RepRap builders set each frame side to 290 mm this way (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly'). Coupling nuts join rods end to end and can draw them together or push them apart (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut').
- **Access needed:** both sides for a through-rod with a nut on each end; one side only (blind) for a stud or hanger bolt once fitted, and for rod epoxied into a drilled hole (Portland Bolt).
- **Materials:** Made from galvanised, mild and stainless steel, nylon, brass, copper, aluminium and titanium (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'); finishes include plain black, zinc plated, hot-dip galvanised and stainless (Portland Bolt; Wikipedia). Joins or passes through: wood (through-bolting and hanger bolts), metal (tapped holes for studs), concrete (epoxy or cast-in anchors) (Portland Bolt), 3D-printed parts clamped between nuts and washers (RepRap), plywood and MDF. Stainless and aluminium threads gall easily, so lubricate them (Wikipedia 'Galling'); nylon rod is light and doesn't rust but is much weaker ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Corrosion: zinc-plated rod rusts outdoors and in damp places; use hot-dip galvanised or stainless steel (Portland Bolt offers plain black and hot-dip galvanised, and stainless 'when corrosion resistance is a priority'). Galling: 'Galling can often occur in screws and bolts, causing the threads to seize and tear free', especially in stainless steel and aluminium; grease or oil reduces it (Wikipedia 'Galling'). Mixed metals in the wet, such as steel rod in aluminium, can corrode each other ⚑ [uncertain]. Vibration loosens nuts; use jam nuts, lock nuts or [threadlocker](../glossary.md#threadlocker) (Wikipedia 'Jam nut', 'Nut (hardware)'). Wood: through-bolted timber that dries after assembly loosens as it shrinks (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Printed parts: plastic clamped on rod creeps and softens with heat, so a PLA part in a hot place loses its clamp ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: threaded rod, studs, hanger bolts, nuts, coupling nuts and washers are bought
  - 3D print (FDM): printed corners, brackets, knobs with nut pockets, spool holders and frame braces threaded onto rod (RepRap; Printables)
  - woodwork: through-bolting timber, and hanger bolts for legs, knobs and pegs (The Handyman's Daughter)
  - CNC: studs in a T-slot bed to hold down clamps, with printed knobs (Printables 'Knob for M5 threaded rod / stud')
  - laser cut: plywood or acrylic plates held apart at set distances by threaded rod and nuts ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the rod and nuts are bought; printed parts are slid onto the rod, and nuts are pressed into printed pockets after printing (Printables 'Knob for M5 threaded rod / stud').
- **Tools:** Two spanners (wrenches) or a socket set; to cut rod, a hacksaw, a chop saw (a cold-cutting blade leaves 'significantly less burring' than an abrasive one) or a threaded-rod shear; a grinder, file or cone-shaped chamfering tool (e.g. a Uniburr) to clean the cut end; gloves, since cut rod gets hot (Albany County Fasteners; Wikimedia Commons 'Gewindestangenschere Makita.jpg'). To fit studs and hanger bolts: two nuts jammed together, or a hanger-bolt driver bit in a drill (Wikipedia 'Jam nut'; The Handyman's Daughter); a drill for clearance and [pilot holes](../glossary.md#pilot-hole), and a tap to thread a hole for a stud ⚑ [uncertain]. A ruler or a cut-to-length jig to set spacings (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly').
- **Difficulty:** easy: cut it to length, clean the end and add nuts and washers; the points needing care are deburring the cut, keeping the rod straight, not over-tightening printed parts, and using a rated grade wherever a failure could hurt someone.

### 3D printing tips

1) Clamp printed parts between a washer and nut on each side, and tighten the nuts 'until they are firm, but do not crush the plastic' (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly'); plastic under constant clamping force slowly creeps, so re-check after a few days ⚑ [uncertain]. 2) Spread the load with washers, and large fender (mudguard) washers where needed; if you use serrated washers, put them only between nut and plastic (RepRap). 3) Clearance holes: make holes a little bigger than the rod so its thread doesn't chew the plastic as it slides through; printers are accurate to about 0.2 mm (Prusa 'Modeling with 3D printing in mind'), so test the fit ⚑ [uncertain]. 4) Set lengths with a jig: cut a piece of rod, wood or cardboard to the wanted distance and adjust the nuts until it just fits (RepRap). 5) Nut pockets: a printed knob or handle can hold a standard nut in a hex socket; one M5 knob uses a 'drafted hole designed for a snug fit', the nut pulled in with a screw and washer from the back, printed flat side down in ABS at 75% [infill](../glossary.md#infill) (Printables 'Knob for M5 threaded rod / stud'). 6) Orientation: 'FFF prints are stronger in one direction than another' (Prusa), so orient printed corners so the clamping force presses across the layers rather than prising them apart ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) Bearings on rod: let a ball bearing do the turning (a 608 bearing fits M8 rod), clamped between washers and nuts, instead of a printed part rubbing on the thread (RepRap Prusa Mendel idlers; Printables 'M8 threaded rod bearing wall spool holder': M8 rod, two 608 bearings, four M8 nuts, PETG). 8) Printed matching threads: BOSL2's threaded_rod() and threaded_nut() modules generate ISO/UTS threads to print (BOSL2 threading.scad). 9) Typical failures: cracked printed corners from over-tightening, nuts loosening with vibration (add a jam nut or nylon-insert nut), and wobble from a bent rod (RepRap).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Search for: threaded rod, all-thread or studding, metric or inch, by grade (class 4.6, 8.8, 10.9, A2, A4; ASTM A307, F1554, A193 B7; SAE J429) and finish (zinc plated, hot-dip galvanised, stainless, nylon) (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'; Portland Bolt); fully threaded, tap-end and double-end studs; hanger bolts (wood hanger screws) and dowel screws; hex nuts, jam (thin) nuts, nylon-insert lock nuts, flange nuts, cap (acorn) nuts, and coupling (extension) nuts including reducing and sight-hole types (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut'); flat and fender washers; hanger-bolt driver bits (The Handyman's Daughter); threaded-rod cutters and cone chamfering tools (Albany County Fasteners). For hanging services from ceilings, buy hanger rod and fittings with a published load rating (Portland Bolt lists 'suspending pipe and mechanical systems' as a use). ## Rules of thumb

- Use it in tension: threaded rod is 'designed to be used in tension' (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'), so use it to pull or clamp, not as a beam, lever or [cantilever](../glossary.md#cantilever); for bending use a solid bar or tube.
- Check the grade before trusting it: metric rod ends are colour-coded: unmarked class 4.6 (tensile 400 N/mm2, yield 240 N/mm2), yellow 8.8 (800/640), white 10.9 (1000/900), green A2 stainless, red A4 stainless (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod').
- Allowable tension (AISC) for non-undercut threaded rod: P = 0.33 x Fu x pi x d^2 / 4, where d is the nominal diameter and Fu the ultimate tensile strength (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'); for M8 class 4.6 about 6.6 kN (calculated here).
- Tap-end studs: thread engagement of about 1 x the nominal diameter in steel and 2 x in aluminium as a minimum (Nord-Lock 'The Experts: Thread engagement in a tapped hole').
- Locking with two nuts: tighten the inner nut to about a quarter to a half of the outer nut's [torque](../glossary.md#torque), hold it with a spanner and tighten the outer nut fully; a pair of jam nuts also gives a headless rod a place for a spanner, but jam nuts 'can be unreliable under significant loads' (Wikipedia 'Jam nut').
- Joining rods: a coupling nut engages more threads than a standard nut, which 'helps to spread the force over a larger number of threads' and reduces stripping; sight-hole coupling nuts let you check how far each rod has gone in (Wikipedia 'Coupling nut').
- Hanger bolts: drill a pilot 'slightly smaller diameter than the bolt (preferably the same size as the center shaft)', mark the depth with tape, and drive with two nuts locked together or a driver bit, never pliers, which strip the threads (The Handyman's Daughter).
- Through-bolting wood: holes should let the rod or bolt be 'inserted by tapping lightly with a wood mallet'; a 1.6 mm (1/16 in) oversized hole gives slightly lower load, smoother holes bear better, and if the wood dries after assembly a snug fit loses much of its load because of [shrinkage](../glossary.md#warping--shrinkage) (USDA Wood Handbook 2021, ch. 8, pp. 8-16 to 8-17).
- Cutting: a cold-cutting saw blade leaves much less burr than an abrasive one; grind off the burr and [chamfer](../glossary.md#chamfer--fillet) the end so a nut starts easily (Albany County Fasteners 'How To Properly Cut, Deburr and Chamfer Threaded Stud').
- Rod-and-printed-part frames: a washer and nut on both sides of each printed part, set lengths with a jig, then tighten 'until they are firm, but do not crush the plastic' (RepRap 'Prusa Mendel Assembly').
- Lifting: lifting equipment 'must be of adequate strength' and 'clearly marked to indicate their safe working loads' (HSE LOLER, for equipment used at work); ordinary threaded rod has no such rating, so don't make lifting points from it.

## Common failures

Nut won't start on a cut end: burr from cutting; grind and chamfer the end (Albany County Fasteners). Printed part cracks: nuts over-tightened; tighten firm but 'do not crush the plastic', with washers (RepRap). Nuts work loose: vibration; add a jam nut, nylon-insert nut or threadlocker (Wikipedia 'Jam nut', 'Nut (hardware)'). Rod bends or sags: it is being used as a beam or is too thin; use a bigger rod or a tube (Wikipedia). Wobble when turned: bent rod or misaligned coupling (RepRap). Stainless threads seize: galling; lubricate before assembly (Wikipedia 'Galling'). Hanger bolt threads stripped: gripped with pliers instead of jammed nuts (The Handyman's Daughter); wood split or thread pulled out: pilot hole too small or too big ⚑ [uncertain]. Stud pulls out of aluminium: too little thread engagement (Nord-Lock). Rod stretches or snaps: a low grade overloaded (Wikipedia strength classes). Through-bolted timber goes slack: the wood dried and shrank (USDA Wood Handbook).

## Safety

Plain zinc-plated hardware-store rod is usually a low-strength grade, and unmarked metric rod is class 4.6, half as strong as class 8.8 (Wikipedia 'Threaded rod'). Don't use threaded rod to lift loads, hang swings or seats, or hold people or heavy things overhead unless every part, including the fixing it screws into, is rated and marked with a safe working load; UK law requires lifting equipment used at work to be strong enough and marked with its safe working load (HSE LOLER). Cut ends are sharp and burred: deburr and chamfer them; wear gloves, because rod gets hot when cut, and keep hands clear of the saw by holding the rod in a vice (Albany County Fasteners). Cover long sticking-out ends with cap nuts so they don't snag or cut ⚑ [uncertain]. Wear eye protection when cutting and grinding ⚑ [uncertain]. A turning rod (lead screw, mixer on a drill) can wind up hair, sleeves or cords ⚑ [uncertain]. When drilling walls or ceilings for anchors and hanger rods, check for hidden cables and pipes.

## Related

- Ordinary screw joint (bolt and nut, machine screw): a headed bolt with a plain shank, better in shear; threaded rod is the cut-to-length version
- Lead screw and nut (incl. anti-backlash nut): proper trapezoidal threads for turning rotation into straight-line motion; plain threaded rod is a crude substitute
- Turnbuckle: a body with left- and right-hand threads that tensions rods, wires and ropes
- Shims, spacers and standoffs: fixed spacers; threaded rod with nuts gives an adjustable one
- Locking adjustment screws (push-pull screws, spring-loaded levelling screws): fine adjustment and locking of a position
- Thread-locking hardware (nylon-insert nut, lock washer, split pin): stops nuts on rod loosening
- Heat-set threaded insert and Screw-in threaded insert: give printed or wooden parts a machine thread for a stud or rod
- Self-clinching fastener (press-in nut or stud): studs pressed permanently into sheet metal
- Wood screw (including coach screw): the wood-thread end of a hanger bolt grips like a coach screw
- Eye bolts, screw eyes and hooks: use rated eye bolts, not bent threaded rod, for hanging and lifting
- Tripod and stand screw mount: a short 1/4-20 stud is the camera-mount version
- Aluminium-extrusion connectors (T-slot nuts and brackets): the stiffer modern alternative to threaded-rod printer frames
