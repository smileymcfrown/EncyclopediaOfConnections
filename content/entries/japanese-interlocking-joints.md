Traditional Japanese carpentry joints in which two or three timbers are cut into interlocking shapes (hooks, dovetails, stepped seats and small tenons) that fit together only along one path and are then locked with a driven wedge or peg, joining beams end to end or at a corner without metal fasteners or glue.

**In plain words:** Wooden beams cut into matching hooks, steps and tongues that slide or drop together like a 3D puzzle and lock by their shape alone, then get tightened with a wooden wedge or peg: no nails, screws or glue. **Proper term:** Japanese interlocking wood joints: splice joints (tsugite), e.g. the gooseneck joint (kama-tsugi) and the wedged scarf joint (kanawa-tsugi), and multi-member corner joints (shiguchi), e.g. the three-way post joint (sanbou-zashi, often written sampo-zashi) and the three-way pinned corner mitre (kane-tsugi).

**Also called:** tsugite (end-to-end or splice joints) and shiguchi (angle or corner joints), the two basic families of Japanese joinery (JAANUS dictionary); kama-tsugi, gooseneck joint, gooseneck tenon and mortise joint (JAANUS; Big Sand Woodworking); koshikake kama-tsugi, lapped gooseneck joint, half-lap gooseneck tenon joint, also shikimen kama-tsugi (JAANUS; Wikimedia Commons file 'Lapped gooseneck joint'); kanawa-tsugi (金輪継ぎ), wedged scarf joint; in full 'an oblique, housed (dadoed), rabbeted, T-shaped, half-blind, tenoned scarf joint' (JAANUS); okkake daisen-tsugi, oblique housed and rabbeted scarf joint locked with two pins; okkake-tsugi when no pins are used (JAANUS); sanbou-zashi / sampo-zashi / sanpo-zashi, three-way joint where beams enter a post from three sides; shihou-zashi is the four-sided version (JAANUS; YouTube video titles 'Sampo zashi'); kane-tsugi, 'right-angle corner' joint, three-way pinned corner mitre, a mitre and pinned bridle joint combined (Fine Woodworking; Instructables); Kawai tsugite, '3-way joint', a modern puzzle-like splice that goes together in three directions (Core77; woodgears.ca); ne-tsugi ('splicing the roots'), repairing a rotted post base by splicing in new wood, often with kanawa-tsugi (Big Sand Woodworking); Japanese joinery, traditional timber joints, integral wood joints (MiGumi paper), nail-free joinery; scarf joint, splice joint, keyed-hook scarf: the Western equivalents (Wikipedia 'Scarf joint').

## How it works

Each timber is cut so that a shaped tongue on one fits a matching pocket in the other: a hooked gooseneck or dovetail head stops the pieces pulling apart, a stepped seat (koshikake) carries the weight, and small stub tenons stop twisting and sideways slipping (JAANUS koshikake kamatsugi; Big Sand Woodworking). Because of this shape the pieces can only be assembled along one path, for example dropped in from above for a gooseneck or slid lengthwise for kanawa-tsugi, and a wedge or offset draw pin driven into a gap between them closes that last path and pulls the shoulders tight (JAANUS kanawatsugi; Big Sand: 'impressive how tight these joints become with a few taps of the wedge'). In a three-way post joint (sanbou-zashi) beams are let into three sides of one post and their tenons cross and interlock inside it (JAANUS shihouzashi), while the furniture-scale kane-tsugi corner is a mitre and a pinned bridle joint fused together, locked by one pin (Fine Woodworking).

## Parts

A few parts: two timbers for a splice or three for a three-way corner, each cut with interlocking features (gooseneck or dovetail head and neck, sloping faces, stepped seat, stub tenons, mortises), plus usually one or two loose hardwood locking pieces: a tapered wedge (kusabi), a pair of long keys (shachi-sen) or a square draw pin (komisen) (JAANUS entries kanawatsugi, sen, kusabi; Big Sand Woodworking).

## Everyday examples

- Temple, shrine and old house posts with a new piece of wood spliced into the rotted base (ne-tsugi), e.g. at the Edo-Tokyo Open Air Museum (Big Sand Woodworking)
- Ground sills and roof purlins of traditional Japanese timber buildings (JAANUS)
- Wooden 'impossible joint' puzzles and viral joinery videos such as the Kawai tsugite three-way joint (Core77; woodgears.ca)
- Picture frames and table corners made with the kane-tsugi three-way pinned mitre (Fine Woodworking; Instructables)

## When to use it

Use these joints when you want solid-wood parts joined with no visible metal, glue or screws, when a joint must be taken apart later for repair, moving or replacing a damaged section (ne-tsugi splices exist for exactly this reason; Big Sand), or when a beam must be made longer than the available timber. Splices suit beams that rest on supports, such as sills and plates (JAANUS). Three-way corners suit frames where a post meets two rails. For makers they are also great as puzzles, teaching models and showpieces, and as printed demonstration pieces or joints between large printed parts.

## When not to use it

Avoid them when you need a quick or cheap joint: they take hours to fit by hand. Don't use across-grain puzzle joints like the Kawai tsugite where strength matters; Wandel found it 'breaks very easily' (woodgears.ca). Avoid splicing where the joint overhangs its support (JAANUS). Don't use them in plywood, MDF or particleboard; use biscuits, dowels, knock-down fittings or a mortise and tenon instead. For structural building work, have the design checked by an engineer ⚑ [uncertain]. If you only need a strong corner in furniture, a glued mortise and tenon or dovetail is simpler; for joining large 3D prints, simpler dovetail keys or alignment pins are easier.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: lengthens beams end to end (splices) or joins two or three members at a corner, with no nails, screws or glue
  - line parts up exactly: the shoulders, sloping faces and stub tenons put the pieces in one exact position and in line (Big Sand Woodworking)
  - lock, and release by hand: wedged or pinned versions can be knocked apart again by driving the wedge or pin back out with a mallet, which is how damaged post bases are cut out and spliced (ne-tsugi) (Big Sand Woodworking)
- **Movement:** none: once wedged or pinned nothing moves. The pieces only slide along a single assembly path while being put together.
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 when wedged or pinned. Without the wedge or pin most of these joints leave 1: the pieces can slide back out the way they went in (lengthwise for kanawa-tsugi, which 'must be inserted in a lengthwise direction'; upwards for a gooseneck that is dropped into its mortise) (JAANUS). The Kawai tsugite puzzle joint is unusual in having three possible assembly directions: 'They can be slid together in three different orientations' (woodgears.ca).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - self-aligning or self-centring: the stepped seats, sloping faces and stub tenons force the two pieces into line, and driving the wedge pulls the shoulders closed until 'the shoulder joints almost become invisible' (Big Sand Woodworking)
  - none of the other listed behaviours. The key trick is a wedge (kusabi) or offset draw pin (komisen) that turns a loose puzzle fit into a tight, pre-loaded joint (JAANUS sen and kusabi entries)
- **How it holds:**
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): hooked gooseneck and dovetail heads, stub tenons and stepped seats trap the pieces so they can only come apart along one path (JAANUS; Big Sand Woodworking)
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the driven wedge or draw pin presses the mating faces hard together, and the large contact area and snug fit are held by friction (ArchDaily on The Joinery animations; Big Sand Woodworking)
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Heavy at building scale: splices are used for ground sills (dodai), wall plates, door-track beams and eave purlins (JAANUS kanawatsugi). Strong in compression and bearing; kanawa-tsugi also resists pulling apart, bending and twisting thanks to its T-shaped stub tenons and wedge (JAANUS; Big Sand), and the half-blind tenon in lapped goosenecks 'prevents damage from twisting forces' (JAANUS). Weak points: the reduced neck (a gooseneck is only half the timber height; Big Sand), so splices are best placed directly over a support; a splice in an overhanging part 'results in a certain weakness' (JAANUS tsugite). Posts with beams let into three or four sides are hollowed out and 'structurally, the pillar is weak' (JAANUS shihouzashi). Joints whose thin parts run across the grain break easily: Wandel's Kawai tsugite 'breaks very easily' because 'Wood is strongest along the grain, and this joint doesn't take advantage of that' (woodgears.ca).
- **Play and precision:** None once wedged: the wedge takes up all clearance. Returns to the same position each time because the shoulders and tenons locate it. Very little tolerance for cutting errors: fitting is done by trial, rubbing ink (sumi) on one half to show where the other binds, and removing too much gives a 'sloppy fit' (Big Sand Woodworking). Designers leave deliberate small gaps where contact isn't needed, e.g. stub tenons cut slightly shorter than their recesses so the main shoulders close (Big Sand). Leaving no slack at all means extra chisel work to get the parts to slide (woodgears.ca).
- **Taking it apart:** removable: as long as no glue is used, knocking out the wedge or pin lets the pieces slide apart without damage; this is why kanawa-tsugi is used to splice new wood into rotted temple and house posts (ne-tsugi) and why teaching models are taken apart and put back together repeatedly (Big Sand Woodworking). Semi-permanent if the pegs are glued or the wood has swollen tight ⚑ [uncertain].
- **How often it can be reused:** many times for dry-fitted models and furniture (Big Sand ran repeated assembly and disassembly cycles on its models to ease the fit); full-size building joints are normally assembled once and opened only for repairs. Wedges and pins may crush and need replacing after repeated hard driving ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Sealing:** none: the joint is not meant to keep out water or air.
- **Adjustable:** Only by the wedge: tapping the wedge or draw pin further in tightens the joint; the position of the parts is fixed by the cuts. Re-driving wedges after the wood dries and shrinks is possible in principle ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Access needed:** both sides: the wedge or pin is driven in from one face and is usually knocked out from the other, and there must be room to slide or lift one timber along its assembly path; kanawa-tsugi 'cannot be slipped together from the side' and must go in lengthwise (JAANUS). Where access is limited, variants that slip in from the side (okkake daisen-tsugi) are used (JAANUS) ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Materials:** Solid, straight-grained wood. Traditional timbers include sugi (Japanese cedar), hinoki (Japanese cypress), akamatsu (red pine) and keyaki (zelkova) (Wikipedia 'Japanese carpentry'); pins and keys are hardwood, 'usually oak or zelkova' (JAANUS sen). Big Sand's models use cherry halves with oak wedges. Also made as 3D-printed plastic puzzle joints (Printables, Thingiverse). Poor in plywood, MDF or particleboard, which have no strong long grain for thin hooks and tenons ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Wood swells and shrinks with moisture, so these tight-fitting joints can bind when damp or loosen when dry; even the moisture in a water-based dye raised the grain enough to tighten Big Sand's model joints, fixed by waxing and working the joints (Big Sand Woodworking). Wet wood rots, especially at post bases on the ground, which is why ne-tsugi repairs are needed (Big Sand). Printed plastic versions creep under constant load and soften with heat (PLA especially) ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - woodwork: traditional hand-tool joinery (pull saws, chisels, planes), or power tools with jigs (router, table saw, bandsaw, drill press) followed by chisel clean-up
  - CNC: 3-axis milling of joints designed for round cutters (Tsugite system; MiGumi method)
  - 3D print (FDM): puzzle and demonstration versions (kanawa-tsugi, Kawai tsugite) and joints for joining printed parts
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the pieces are printed separately and slid together; several designs are made of identical pieces, e.g. print the kanawa-tsugi half twice, or four or eight identical Kawai tsugite blocks (Printables; Thingiverse).
- **Tools:** To make by hand: Japanese pull saws (ryoba, dozuki), chisels (nomi), planes (kanna), marking gauge (kebiki), ink line (sumitsubo) and square (sashigane) (Wikipedia 'Japanese carpentry'). With machines: table saw, router and jigs, bandsaw and drill press, then chisel clean-up (Big Sand Woodworking), a pantorouter (woodgears.ca) or a 3-axis CNC mill (Tsugite). To assemble: a mallet or hammer (genno) to drive wedges and pins; nothing for 3D-printed puzzle versions.
- **Difficulty:** hard by hand: needs sharp saws and chisels, accurate layout and a lot of test fitting (Big Sand found the gooseneck recess particularly tricky to fit because it has so many contact areas); the simpler kane-tsugi corner is described as 'within the capability of a modest woodworker with sharp tools' (Instructables). Moderate with a router jig, pantorouter or CNC; easy as a 3D-printed puzzle model.

### 3D printing tips

1) Leave clearance: Hubs gives 0.5 mm as the tolerance for interlocking joints in FDM (0.2 mm for SLA and SLS). The customizable Kawai tsugite model deliberately adds 'some play for a smooth assembly' (Thingiverse, aeropic), while Wandel's wooden version with no slack needed chiselling before it would slide (woodgears.ca). Print a small test pair first. 2) Treat layer lines like wood grain: thin hooks, necks and stub tenons are weakest where they cross layers, so lay the joint so these features run along the print bed ⚑ [uncertain]. Wandel's joint failed first at its thin tip, which crossed the grain (woodgears.ca). 3) Many designs need no supports: the Kawai tsugite puzzle box prints with 20% infill, no supports and no adhesion aid (Printables, Chickenface22). 4) Add a small radius to edges to help assembly and reduce stress concentrations (Hubs). 5) Choose a tougher plastic: Hubs prefers ABS to PLA for FDM joints because it is more ductile, and PETG is a common alternative ⚑ [uncertain]. 6) If you add a wedge, print it lying flat so its taper runs along the layers, and expect it to relax over time in plastic ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) The ideamanhandle Kawai tsugite notes that the joint is impractical in wood because of grain and effort but 'fun and useful' in 3D printing (Thingiverse).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Usually none: the joint is cut into the timber, and wedges (kusabi), keys (shachi-sen) and square draw pins (komisen) are shop-made from hardwood (JAANUS). Useful tools to search for: Japanese pull saws (ryoba, dozuki, kataba), Japanese chisels (nomi), genno hammer, marking gauge (kebiki), sumitsubo ink line, sashigane square (Wikipedia 'Japanese carpentry'). Software: Tsugite, a free research prototype for designing voxel-based wood joints and exporting 3-axis CNC milling paths (free for personal and non-commercial research use).

## Rules of thumb

- In a lapped gooseneck (koshikake kama-tsugi) or lapped dovetail splice, the mortise occupies about half the thickness of the timber and the other half forms the seat that the second timber rests on (JAANUS 'koshikake kamatsugi', 'koshikake aritsugi').
- The gooseneck head and neck are only half the overall stock height (Big Sand Woodworking 'Kama-tsugi / Gooseneck Joint').
- Put a splice directly over a support such as a post (shintsugi); a splice in a part that overhangs its support (mochidashitsugi) is weaker (JAANUS 'tsugite').
- Cut stub tenons slightly shorter than the depth of their recesses so they don't bottom out and leave gaps at the main shoulders; 'The more solid the contact between the main shoulders of these joints the stronger they will be' (Big Sand Woodworking 'Kanawa-tsugi Joinery Models Part 2').
- Kanawa-tsugi wedges taper along their length and are trapezoid-shaped in cross-section to match the sloping faces of the joint (Big Sand Woodworking 'Kanawa-tsugi Joinery Models Part 2').
- Plain scarf-joint tapers are generally cut between 1:8 and 1:10; interlocking scarves (hooked, keyed, nibbed) 'still depend on mechanical fastening to keep the joint closed', which is the job of the Japanese wedge or pin (Wikipedia 'Scarf joint').
- FDM interlocking joints need about 0.5 mm tolerance (0.2 mm for SLA and SLS) (Hubs 'How to design interlocking joints').
- When CNC milling, the round cutter leaves rounded inside corners that create gaps or overlaps and can block assembly, so joints must be redesigned for the cutter radius rather than copied directly (MiGumi, arXiv 2510.13168; Tsugite uses 'corner rounding' path planning).

## Common failures

Sloppy fit from paring away too much, or binding in hard-to-see places; use sumi ink or pencil to find contact points and trim a little at a time (Big Sand). Tear-out on the tiny sloped shoulders of the gooseneck rebate (Big Sand, reader comment). Stub tenons bottoming out before the shoulders close; cut them a little short (Big Sand). Joints tightening after finishing because moisture raised the grain; use alcohol-based dyes or leave a little extra clearance (Big Sand). No slack in the design, so the joint won't slide (woodgears.ca). Thin cross-grain tips breaking off, after which 'the rest of the joint doesn't hold together very well' (woodgears.ca). In CNC work, rounded inside corners making parts clash; design for the cutter radius (MiGumi; Tsugite). Posts weakened by too many beams let into them (JAANUS shihouzashi).

## Safety

Splicing a load-bearing post means temporarily supporting the building: Big Sand's crew uses 'a series of temporary beams, posts, and jacks to lift the structure around the post' before cutting out the rotten part. Sharp chisels and saws cause most hand-tool injuries; keep both hands behind the edge ⚑ [uncertain]. Driven wedges can split thin parts if forced ⚑ [uncertain]. Small printed puzzle pieces are a choking hazard for young children ⚑ [uncertain].

- Mortise and tenon (incl. wedged, drawbored and tusk tenons): the basic tongue-in-hole joint these combine; a tusk tenon is locked by a removable wedge in the same way
- Dovetail joint and sliding dovetail: flared shapes that resist pulling apart; Japanese splices often use dovetail (ari) heads instead of goosenecks
- Butterfly key (bow-tie key): a separate bow-tie insert across a joint, instead of shaping the timbers themselves
- Self-locking wedge (wedge clamp, taper key): the wedge principle on its own, used to lock these joints
- Split-part joints for large prints (dovetail keys, puzzle joints, alignment pins): the 3D-printing equivalents for joining printed pieces
- Plain scarf joint and keyed-hook scarf ('trait de jupiter'): Western splices that usually rely on bolts, plates or glue to stay closed (Wikipedia 'Scarf joint')
- Knock-down cam-lock and cross-dowel connectors: undoable joints using metal hardware instead of shaped wood
