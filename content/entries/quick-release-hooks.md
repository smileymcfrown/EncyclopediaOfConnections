A hinged hook, jaw or stack of rings that carries a big load through its strong parts while a small latch (a sliding ring, spring pin, trigger or cord loop) keeps it shut, so a small, deliberate pull on the latch releases the load instantly.

**In plain words:** A hook or clip that holds a heavy pull on a rope, chain or strap, but lets go the moment you pull a pin, knock a ring, tug a lanyard or yank a cable, even while the load is still pulling. **Proper term:** Quick-release hook (slip hook, release-under-load connector).

**Also called:** pelican hook (named for its beak-like look); gate hook or lifeline pelican hook (the small pin-locked kind on yacht lifeline gates); pelican hook chain stopper; Senhouse slip / slip hook (British names for the ring-held hinged hook); three-ring release / 3-ring release system / 3-ring riser release / cutaway system (skydiving); mini rings and large rings are its two sizes; snap shackle / quick release snap shackle / spinnaker shackle (spring-plunger shackle opened by a pull ring or lanyard); trigger shackle / trigger snap shackle; Triggersnap (Ronstan brand); panic snap / quick-release snap (horse tack); tow release / tow hook / Tost release (glider launching; Tost is a brand); cargo hook / release hook / payload release / drop hook (drones and helicopters); on-load release hook (lifeboats).

## How it works

The load is carried by the strong hinged part (tongue, jaw or ring) and the body, not by the latch; the latch only has to stop the hinged part swinging open. Because the hinge acts as a lever, the latch feels only a fraction of the load, so a small push or pull frees it. On a pelican hook the ring slides over the tip of the tongue; when the ring is knocked or slid off, the tongue flies open (Practical Boat Owner, 'Senhouse slip'; Wikipedia). On a snap shackle 'a piston and spring arrangement ... latches the hinged shackle closed' (Practical Sailor); pulling the lanyard draws the piston back and the jaw swings open. In a three-ring release each ring passes through the next larger one and folds back, so each ring acts as a lever on the next and 'multiplies the [mechanical advantage](../glossary.md#mechanical-advantage)'; the smallest ring is held by a cord loop, the loop by a thin cable, and pulling the cable out makes the whole stack 'cascade free' (Wikipedia). Well-designed releases make the release force nearly independent of the load: Pope's patent routes [axial](../glossary.md#axial--radial) loads through 'heavy axial jaws and not ... directly to the locking pin' (US 4,613,180), and Tylaska's shapes the latch contact as a logarithmic spiral so the release force stays the same whatever the tension (US 5,195,223).

## Parts

A few parts to a small assembly that acts as one unit. Pelican hook: a body with an eye, a hinged tongue (the 'beak'), a sliding ring (bail) that holds the tongue shut, and often a locking pin on a lanyard. Snap shackle: a body with a fixed or swivel eye, a hinged jaw, a spring-loaded plunger pin (piston) and a pull ring or lanyard; trigger types use a latch opened with a fid or release spike. Three-ring release: a large ring on the harness, a middle ring and a small ring sewn to the webbing riser, a cord loop, a grommet, and a semi-rigid release cable in a housing leading to a handle (Wikipedia).

## Everyday examples

- Yacht lifeline gates held shut by a small pelican (gate) hook with a pin on a lanyard (captnmike.com; Sea-Dog)
- Spinnaker sheets and halyards on sailing boats, clipped on with a quick-release snap shackle (Wichard, Practical Sailor)
- Horse trailer ties with a panic snap, which can be released even when a panicking horse is pulling back (Wikipedia, 'Panic snap')
- A skydiver's harness, where a three-ring release lets a malfunctioning main parachute be cut away with one pull (Wikipedia)

## When to use it

When a load must be let go fast, on purpose, while it is still pulling: cutting away a tangled parachute, dropping a tow line from a glider or a tug, releasing a sail or spinnaker, freeing an anchor chain, letting a panicking animal go, dropping a payload from a drone or model aircraft, or opening a lifeline gate. Also when the release has to happen from a distance, through a lanyard, cable or servo (Tylaska's patent lists gliders, barges and sails as typical uses).

## When not to use it

Avoid it where an accidental release would be dangerous and a person could bump, snag or vibrate the latch: use a screw-pin shackle, locking carabiner or a knot instead, or add a second, independent attachment (helicopter rescue systems use a belly band or dual hook, Wikipedia). Don't use an ordinary spring-gate carabiner or snap hook when you need to open it under load; they cannot be opened while loaded. Don't use a plain snap shackle for releasing big loads; use a trigger shackle with a release spike (Ronstan, Practical Sailor). Don't use a printed release for people, heavy or falling loads.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - lock, and release by hand: holds a heavy load and lets it go when a person pulls a pin, ring, lanyard or cable
  - join and hold: connects a rope, chain or strap to an eye, ring or harness
  - hold or release using power: some versions are released by a servo or [actuator](../glossary.md#actuator), e.g. drone payload drops and RC glider tow hooks (Printables models)
- **Movement:** rotate: once the latch is freed, the hinged tongue or jaw swings open on its pivot (or the rings unfold) and the load pulls itself out; while latched nothing moves ⚑ [uncertain: opening angles are not published]
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 while latched (a swivel eye, where fitted, adds free turning about the pull line); after release the jaw or tongue has 1 (it swings on its hinge) and the load is free.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - quick-release: one pull on a pin, ring, lanyard or cable frees the load instantly, even under full tension (pelican hooks and trigger shackles are 'capable of being released under load', Sea-Dog, Ronstan)
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): it cannot open by itself under a straight pull; someone must operate the latch, sometimes from a distance through a lanyard or cable
  - [fail-safe](../glossary.md#fail-safe--fail-secure) or breakaway: some variants release automatically, e.g. Tost glider centre-of-gravity releases open at 'a defined cable angle' during winch launches, and Tylaska's patent describes a version that works as a reusable 'weak link' at a set tension (US 5,195,223)
- **How it holds:**
  - shape (the parts interlock; [form-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): a closed hook, jaw or interlocked rings surround the ring, chain link or strap
  - spring force (a spring or springy part presses it in place): snap shackles and panic snaps keep their latch closed with a spring
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): in the three-ring release the webbing wrapped round each ring adds friction that helps the rings share the load ⚑ [uncertain: not stated by the sources]
  - powered hold: servo or [solenoid](../glossary.md#solenoid) releases hold the latch with an actuator
- **Input and output:** not applicable: it holds and releases a load rather than passing motion on (the latch is a lever that reduces the load it sees: 'Each ring in the series multiplies the mechanical advantage', Wikipedia on the 3-ring release)
- **Power:** passive (no power needed) for pelican hooks, snap shackles, three-ring releases and panic snaps. Powered versions exist (servo-actuated drone payload releases and RC glider tow releases, electrically released helicopter cargo hooks); a servo release generally keeps its last state if power is lost ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Load:** Medium to very heavy, and strongest as a straight pull. Examples: a 70 mm Wichard HR snap shackle has a working load of 880 kg and a breaking load of 1,500 kg; the Tylaska T12 trigger shackle is rated for a '6,000-pound safe working load' (Practical Sailor); pelican hook chain stoppers range from 10 to 100 tonnes safe working load (Beaver Engineering); Tost glider releases take 14.1 kN of cable force. Weak points: side and twisting loads on snap shackles (Wikipedia 'Shackle': 'not as secure as any other form of shackle'), and anything that knocks or depresses the latch.
- **Play and precision:** not applicable: it doesn't locate parts. Snap shackles and panic snaps usually have a swivel eye so the load can turn freely; a three-ring release depends on close [tolerances](../glossary.md#tolerance) in the riser webbing, and poorly made ones lose their mechanical advantage (Wikipedia)
- **Taking it apart:** tool-free quick release: a pull on a ring, pin, lanyard or handle; large chain-stopper pelican hooks are knocked open with a maul (sledgehammer), and loaded trigger shackles are opened with a fid or release spike
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: it is reset by closing the jaw and refitting the ring, pin or loop. Three-ring releases need the rings, loop and cable re-rigged and checked each time (Wikipedia); lifeboat hooks need careful resetting (Safety4sea); a printed PLA cargo hook cracked from the shock of one heavily loaded release (firstgizmo, Printables).
- **Sealing:** none: nothing makes it seal
- **Adjustable:** no: it is either latched or released; length and tension are set by the chain, strap or rigging it is fitted to
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind): the latch is worked from the outside, often from a distance by a lanyard, trip line, handle and cable, or servo
- **Materials:** Investment-cast 316 stainless steel (Sea-Dog pelican hooks); forged 17-4 PH stainless steel (Wichard HR snap shackles); brass for small snap shackles (Wikimedia Commons photo); steel for large chain-stopper pelican hooks ⚑ [uncertain: grade not given]; three-ring releases use metal rings on Type 17 nylon webbing risers and a Lolon-F or Teflon-coated steel cable (Wikipedia). Printed light-duty versions: PLA (firstgizmo's cargo hook), usually with a steel pin and a compression spring (Doormat_1's snap shackle). It connects to rope (spliced eyes), chain links, webbing loops, rings and eyebolts.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Salt water: precipitation-hardened stainless steels used in snap shackles can suffer 'pitting, rust, and crevice corrosion' where imperfections break the passive layer (Practical Sailor); 'Rust should be polished clean and inspected with a magnifying lens' (Practical Sailor). Wear and poor adjustment: lifeboat on-load release hooks 'can become extremely unstable due to minor wear tolerances or poor adjustment of release cables which could trigger unexpected releases' (Safety4sea). Three-ring releases: stiff or twisted riser webbing and hard cable housings can jam a cutaway, and replacing Lolon-F cable coatings with Teflon compounds 'caused both cracking and cable stripping problems' (Wikipedia). Printed releases: PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in sunlight; PETG is fine outdoors below about 80 °C; ASA resists UV up to 93 °C (Prusa).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: the normal route for anything carrying real load (pelican hooks, snap shackles, trigger shackles, three-ring riser sets, glider tow releases)
  - 3D print (FDM): light-duty snap shackles, pelican hooks and remote-release cargo hooks, assembled with metal pins, springs and screws (Printables models)
  - 3D print (resin): possible for small parts ⚑ [uncertain: no tested example found]
  - CNC: machined latch and jaw parts for strong custom releases ⚑ [uncertain: no maker example found]
  - sewing: three-ring releases need the rings sewn into webbing risers; parachute risers are made by certified riggers ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: printed versions are separate parts assembled with a spring, a metal hinge pin or bolt, and a split ring or wire (Jackson's and Doormat_1's snap shackles; firstgizmo's cargo hook uses glue or M3 bolts)
- **Tools:** none for small hooks and shackles; a maul or sledgehammer for large pelican hooks (Wikipedia; US Navy Seaman training manual); a fid or release spike to open a loaded trigger shackle (Ronstan: 'always use a release spike when opening a loaded shackle'). Fitting may need a splice, shackle or sewing (three-ring risers).
- **Difficulty:** moderate: light-duty printed snap shackles and release hooks are simple to print and assemble with a spring, pin and screws, but anything that holds people or heavy, falling loads must be a rated, bought part

### 3D printing tips

Design so the load runs through the body and hinge, not through the latch pin: that is what keeps the release force small and constant (Pope, US 4,613,180; Tylaska, US 5,195,223). Print loaded parts on their side so the layers run along the direction of pull: Jackson's printable snap shackle says 'Print all components on their side for strongest layer orientation' and uses 100% [infill](../glossary.md#infill) for the hinge pin and gate pin; Doormat_1 warns that a pin printed standing up 'can break apart along the [layer lines](../glossary.md#layer-lines)' and suggests replacing it with M6 threaded rod. Use metal where you can: a steel hinge bolt, a compression spring on the gate pin (Doormat_1 used an 11 mm OD, 8 mm ID, 25 mm spring) and a split ring for the pull. Expect the release itself to be the hardest moment: firstgizmo's PLA cargo hook held about 50 kg of bricks for 15 minutes, but 'the violent release of tension cracked the hooks main body', so use more [perimeters](../glossary.md#perimeters) or a tougher material (PETG, nylon) and keep people clear of the load path. Supports are usually needed inside tubes and pockets (Doormat_1). PLA softens above about 60 °C and degrades in UV; use PETG or ASA outdoors (Prusa).

## Rules of thumb

- Keep the load off the latch: in a good design axial loads 'are supported by the heavy axial jaws and not transferred directly to the locking pin so that minimal release force is required even under heavy loads' (Pope, US 4,613,180); Tylaska's latch surface is a logarithmic spiral so 'the force necessary to release the mechanism is substantially independent of the tension' (US 5,195,223).
- Plain pelican hooks are hard to release under strong tension: the simple 'pelican' fitting 'is particularly difficult to release once under strong tension' (US 5,195,223); big ones are knocked open with a sledgehammer or maul (Wikipedia; US Navy manual).
- Take the load off the pin before pulling it on a lifeline pelican (gate) hook: 'Squeeze the Pelican Hook/ Gate Hook ... FIRST', because pulling the pin hard under load 'can cause the ring to break and the pin to come shooting out' (captnmike.com).
- Ordinary snap shackles are not meant for big loaded releases: used that way a snap shackle 'will work rather poorly (hard to release) and is likely to have the pin assembly or the split ring fail' (Wikipedia, 'Shackle'). Use a trigger shackle opened with a fid or release spike: Practical Sailor needed 'just 5 pounds of push to insert a fid' to open a Tylaska T12 'with 1,000 pounds of tension in the line'.
- Rated working load is roughly 60% of breaking load for marine snap shackles: Wichard HR 70 mm swivel snap shackle, working load 880 kg, breaking load 1,500 kg (Wichard product page).
- Bigger rings mean an easier pull in a three-ring release: mini rings 'reduce the mechanical advantage inherent in the system thereby increasing the pull force a jumper must apply', and the tolerance in making the risers and their connection to the rings 'is critical in maintaining the mechanical advantage' (Wikipedia). A Wikimedia Commons animation description gives the mechanical advantage as 200 to 1 ⚑ [uncertain: not from a primary source].
- Maintain three-ring releases regularly: disconnect and flex the risers, check the rings for cracks or corrosion, and remove, clean and lubricate the cable, 'typically with silicone based lubricant' (Wikipedia).
- Where people's lives depend on it, add redundancy against accidental release: helicopter rescue systems use a belly band or dual hook so that 'two distinct actions [are] required to release the load' (Wikipedia, 'Helicopter flight rescue system').

## Common failures

Won't release under load: plain pelican hooks and ordinary snap shackles jam when heavily loaded (US 5,195,223; Wikipedia 'Shackle'); choose a release designed to open under load and keep it lubricated. Pin or split ring breaks when forced (Wikipedia 'Shackle'; captnmike.com): take the load off first, or use a lanyard or spike. Opens by accident: ring or pin not fully seated (captnmike.com), wear or poor cable adjustment (Safety4sea), or a latch knocked or snagged; seat the latch fully and add a keeper or backup. Three-ring cutaway is hard or fails: mis-rigged rings, stiff or twisted risers, dirty or cracked cable (Wikipedia); flex, inspect and lubricate regularly. Corrosion and pitting in marine stainless (Practical Sailor). Printed release cracks from the shock of a heavy release (firstgizmo).

## Safety

Stored energy: a line or chain under load leaps free when released, and 'leads or ropes under load can flail when freed' (Wikipedia, 'Panic snap'); stand clear of the load path, as in the US Navy anchor drill where the seaman with the maul 'knocks the bail off the tongue of the pelican hook and steps clear'. A pelican hook pin pulled under load can fly out, and a half-seated pin can let a lifeline gate 'pop open with no warning', risking someone going overboard (captnmike.com). Unexpected release of lifeboat hooks has caused deaths (Safety4sea). Opening a loaded trigger shackle with a finger is dangerous; use a fid or spike to keep fingers clear (Practical Sailor). Never trust a printed or unrated release with a person's weight or with anything that could fall on people.

## Related

- Spring-gate hook (carabiner, snap hook): the same hook-and-gate idea, but the gate cannot be opened while loaded; for joining rather than releasing
- Shackle and quick link: screw-pin and bolt shackles (D-shackles) and screw-closed quick links; stronger and more secure, but need a pin or sleeve unscrewed with the load off
- Quick-release pin (ball-lock pin): a push-button pin through a clevis; releases a pinned joint rather than a hook
- Rope splices: include the soft shackle, a rope loop closed by a knot; it and the toggle and becket are rope versions that can be undone by hand, usually without load
- Cam cleat and clam cleat: let a rope run free with one flick, but only for loads a person can hold
- Shear-pin coupling (breakaway link): lets go automatically at a set overload instead of on command
- Draw latch ([over-centre](../glossary.md#over-centre) latch): a lever that snaps shut and flips open, for lids and panels rather than rope loads
- Electropermanent magnet and solenoid latches: powered releases with no moving hook
- Capewell release: the older parachute riser release that Bill Booth's three-ring system largely replaced (Wikipedia, 'Cut-away' and 'Bill Booth')
