Rope wrapped round poles where they cross or touch and then tightened with turns between them, to make rigid frames, braced crossings, scissor legs, tripods and lengthened poles with no hardware or cut joints.

**In plain words:** Rope or string wound tightly round two or three poles where they cross or lie side by side, then cinched with extra turns between the poles, so sticks, canes or logs can be built into frames, tripods, A-frames and trellises without nails, screws or bolts. **Proper term:** Pole lashings (spar lashings): square, diagonal, shear, round and tripod lashings, each made of wrapping turns (wraps) pulled tight by frapping turns (fraps).

**Also called:** lashing (ropework): 'an arrangement of rope, wire, or webbing ... used to secure and fasten two or more items together in a somewhat rigid manner' (Wikipedia 'Lashing (ropework)'); square lashing (ABOK #2114), including the traditional, modified and Japanese (Mark II) square lashings (Animated Knots; Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION'); diagonal lashing (ABOK #2115), including the Filipino diagonal lashing (Animated Knots; Scout Pioneering); shear lashing, sheer lashing, two-spar shear lashing, for shear legs and A-frames (ABOK #2108-2110) (Animated Knots; Wikipedia); round lashing, for joining two parallel poles to make a longer one (similar to ABOK #2103); West Country round lashing, strop lashing, half-hitch round lashing; gammon is the nautical round lashing of a mast (Animated Knots; Scout Pioneering 'Four Different Lashings to Extend the Length of a Pole'; Wikipedia); tripod lashing, gyn lashing, figure-of-eight lashing, three-spar shear lashing (ABOK #2111); with four poles, a quadpod (Animated Knots; Wikipedia 'Lashing (ropework)', 'Pioneering (scouting)'); floor lashing: lashes a row of poles side by side into a deck or table top (Wikipedia 'Pioneering (scouting)'); wraps, fraps or frapping turns, and racking turns: the turns a lashing is built from (Wikipedia 'List of knot terminology'; Animated Knots 'Terminology'); pioneering (scouting): building structures from spars and lashings (Wikipedia 'Pioneering (scouting)'); bamboo scaffolding ties: nylon or bamboo strips used to lash scaffold poles (Hong Kong Labour Department code); cane wigwam, tepee or bean teepee: garden canes tied together at the top (RHS; Fine Gardening).

## How it works

The rope is first tied to a pole, then wound round both poles several times (wraps). Next, frapping turns go round the wraps in the gap between the poles, at right angles to them, which pulls every wrap tighter, much like pinching in the middle of a loose belt (Wikipedia 'Lashing (ropework)'; Animated Knots 'Square Lashing'). The tight rope presses the poles hard together, so friction between rope and poles, and between the poles themselves, stops them sliding or turning; the end is tied off with a hitch or half hitches (Animated Knots). A diagonal lashing starts with a timber hitch round both poles, which pulls a gap between them closed (Animated Knots 'Diagonal Lashing'); a tripod lashing weaves 'racking turns' in and out between three poles like extended figure eights (Animated Knots 'Tripod Lashing'); a round lashing for lengthening a pole has no frapping turns, because the poles already touch, and is tightened with wedges instead (Scout Pioneering; Animated Knots 'Round Lashing').

## Parts

A few parts: one length of rope, twine or string and the two or three poles (spars, canes, dowels or logs) it joins. It starts and finishes with simple hitches (usually a clove hitch, or a timber hitch for a diagonal lashing), and a round lashing may add two wooden wedges.

## Everyday examples

- A wigwam or row of A-frames of bamboo canes tied at the top for runner beans or sweet peas (RHS)
- Bamboo scaffolding lashed together on buildings in Hong Kong (Wikipedia 'Bamboo scaffolding')
- Scout camp gateways, tables, towers and bridges built from spars and lashings (Wikipedia 'Pioneering (scouting)')
- A garden trellis of bamboo canes lashed with twine (NPS 'Building with Bamboo'; Fine Gardening)

## When to use it

When you want to build a frame from round poles, canes or sticks without drilling, cutting joints or buying hardware: garden trellises, bean wigwams and cane cages, camp furniture and gadgets, temporary stands, display frames, rafts and play structures (Animated Knots 'Square Lashing'; Wikipedia 'Pioneering (scouting)'; Fine Gardening). It suits poles of uneven or varying diameter, like bamboo or saplings, that would be hard to fit with standard fittings, and structures that must come apart again with the poles and rope re-used. Use a square lashing for crossings, a diagonal lashing to pull together and brace an X, a shear lashing for A-frames, a tripod lashing for three legs, and a round lashing to lengthen a pole.

## When not to use it

Not for anything people climb, sit or stand on unless it is built and checked under experienced supervision and tested under load first; for anything that holds a person, use a rated, designed system. Not for permanent outdoor structures, where rope rots or loosens: use bolted or cut joints (Cross-halving slot joint, Japanese interlocking joints) or Clamp-on tube fittings. Not for square-section timber, where the starting clove hitch 'will not work on rectangular shapes' (Wikipedia 'Knot'): screw or bolt it instead. Not for parts that must line up precisely. For quick light ties on thin canes, cable ties (Linear ratchet), hose clamps or a printed connector are faster; for a self-supporting set of crossing sticks, see Reciprocal frame. Don't build in rain or wet conditions (Scout Pioneering).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: joins poles where they cross (square and diagonal), at their tips so the legs can spread (shear and tripod), or side by side to make one longer pole (round)
  - tighten and hold tension: frapping turns pull the wraps tight and keep them tight
  - lock, and release by hand: unties by hand so the rope and poles can be used again
- **Movement:** none once frapped: the joint is meant to be rigid. A shear lashing is deliberately left as a scissor pivot while the legs are spread, then frapped (Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION'); a loose strop lashing 'allows movement or acts as a hinge' (Scout Pioneering).
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 when finished and frapped; 1 (the legs pivot about the lashing) while shear legs or a tripod are being spread, or in a deliberately loose strop lashing.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - tensioning (takes up slack and holds): frapping turns, wound round the wraps between the poles, take up the slack in the wraps and keep them tight (Wikipedia 'List of knot terminology', Fraps; Animated Knots 'Square Lashing')
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the tightened wraps clamp the poles together, and friction between rope and poles stops them moving (Wikipedia 'Lashing (ropework)'; Animated Knots)
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the wraps encircle both poles, and in a tripod the crossed legs 'bind against each other for greater stability' (Animated Knots 'Tripod Lashing')
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Light (garden canes) to medium (scout-size spar structures); no laboratory strength figures for rope lashings were found ⚑ [uncertain]. The square lashing 'is designed to be load bearing and can be used to create scaffolding' (Animated Knots), and Hong Kong bamboo scaffolds are fastened with nylon strips (Wikipedia 'Bamboo scaffolding'), but those follow an official code with trained workers. Strongest where the poles press against each other (crossways shear and compression); weakest against the frame twisting out of square (racking), which is why frames are cross-braced with diagonal lashings 'to prevent racking' (Wikipedia 'Lashing (ropework)'), and starting with a clove hitch 'helps keep the structure from racking' (Scout Pioneering). Grip falls if the rope gets wet or the poles shrink (see environment_limits).
- **Play and precision:** noticeable: a friction joint on rough, irregular poles, so it can settle and creep and is not used to locate parts precisely ⚑ [uncertain]. 'Good lashings are not made in a hurry. Each wrap must be made with a strain on the rope', and frapping turns are 'pulled up as tightly as possible' to keep it rigid (Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION'). It copes well with poles of uneven size and with crossings from 45 to 90 degrees (Animated Knots 'Square Lashing').
- **Taking it apart:** removable: unties by hand and the ropes are re-used ('When the day's work is complete, untie all knots, coil all ropes, check all hardware', Scout Pioneering '22 Pioneering Safety Points'). A West Country round lashing 'can be a little difficult to untie' (Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION').
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: pioneering kits keep lashing ropes cut to standard lengths with whipped ends for repeated use, and ropes and poles are checked before and after each use (Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION', '22 Pioneering Safety Points').
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** partly: tension can be topped up by adding frapping turns or re-tying, and the spread of shear legs and tripods is set before the fraps are pulled tight (Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION'; Animated Knots 'Tripod Lashing'). Moving a pole means untying the lashing.
- **Access needed:** both sides: the rope must pass right round the poles, so you need to reach all the way round the crossing.
- **Materials:** Rope: pure manila is the traditional choice for scout pioneering, 1/4 in for most projects and 3/8 in when the two spars together exceed 6 in diameter; beware sisal sold as manila, which is 'not anywhere near as good' (Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION', 'Lashing Ropes and Spars'); binder twine or cheaper rope for small temporary gadgets (Scout Pioneering). Garden work: 'cotton or nylon string works fine' for finger-thick bamboo on a stake, thin clothesline for three stout saplings in a bean tepee, and baling twine worked poorly for one gardener (Fine Gardening); polypropylene twine for a bamboo trellis (NPS); twine for cane A-frames (RHS). Poles: straight wooden spars with little taper (pine is widely used, hardwoods allow thinner spars), typically 2-4 in at the butt and 6-12 ft long for scout projects (Scout Pioneering); bamboo canes; dowels and staves. Wikipedia notes the clove hitch, the usual starting knot, 'will not work on rectangular shapes' (Wikipedia 'Knot'), so lashings suit round poles best.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Wet: 'Rope and spars become slippery, as does your footing. Knots can slip when wet and become unsafe' (Scout Pioneering '22 Pioneering Safety Points'); nylon is '10-15% weaker when wet' (Wikipedia 'Rope'). Rot and weather: natural-fibre rope rots more readily than synthetic, and some synthetics are damaged more easily by UV light (Wikipedia 'Rope'); manila 'withstands repeated wetting and drying cycles' (Peschke, quoted by Scout Pioneering). Shrinkage: green bamboo or wood dries and shrinks, loosening lashings and ties (one trellis designer notes bamboo 'shrinks a bit over time') (Printables 104360; a maker's observation, not a measured rate) ⚑ [uncertain]; Hong Kong's code requires scaffold bamboo to be air-dried at least 90 days before use (Wikipedia 'Bamboo scaffolding'). Bamboo 'deteriorates over time' and is combustible (Wikipedia 'Bamboo scaffolding'). Printed helper parts in PLA go brittle outdoors (Printables 1732400, designer's note).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: rope, twine or string, tied by hand
  - woodwork: cutting and trimming poles, spars, canes and dowels, and cutting wedges for round lashings
  - 3D print (FDM): printed alternatives to lashing (cane wigwam holders, trellis joints, tripod connectors) and miniature practice models
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: lashings are tied with rope round poles; only alternative connectors or practice parts are printed.
- **Tools:** none to tie. Helpful extras: a stick to lever frapping turns tight using your legs (Animated Knots 'Square Lashing'); two wooden wedges and a mallet to tighten a round lashing (Animated Knots 'Round Lashing'); loppers or a saw to cut canes (NPS 'Building with Bamboo'); a mallet for pioneering stakes (Scout Pioneering).
- **Difficulty:** easy for garden canes and light frames; moderate for tight, load-bearing lashings on thick spars, which need technique and strength to frap tight; anything people climb or stand on needs experienced supervision.

### 3D printing tips

Lashings aren't printed, but printed parts can replace or help them. 1) For a cane wigwam, a printed disc with holes round the edge can replace tying the tops; its designer recommends PETG or ASA because 'PLA will go brittle after a season outdoors' (Printables 1732400, designer's note). 2) Printed bamboo trellis joints (an OpenSCAD customiser) work best in TPU or another flexible filament after two seasons' use, because flexible joints forgive mismatched cane diameters; secure canes with UV-resistant cable ties of at least 40 lb strength pulled tight with a tie gun, since bamboo 'shrinks a bit over time' and slips; printed ridges keep the ties in place (Printables 104360, designer's note). 3) A printed teepee connector for three 25-30 mm wooden rods, with an M6 clamping screw, makes a tripod without lashing (Printables 1275716). 4) If you lash over printed tubes or brackets, add a groove or ridges so the wraps can't slide off, and expect a printed part under constant lashing tension to creep, especially in PLA ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) To practise, make miniature pioneering models with small sticks and white thread, as scouts do (Wikipedia 'Pioneering (scouting)').

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Manila rope in 1/4 in and 3/8 in, binder (baler) twine, polypropylene twine, jute or cotton garden twine, nylon string and thin clothesline; bamboo canes (sold in bundles at garden centres, RHS), wooden spars, dowels and staves; wooden wedges. Hardware alternatives: cable ties (Linear ratchet), hose clamps, U-bolt clamps, clamp-on tube fittings, and printed or bought cane connectors and wigwam toppers.

## Rules of thumb

- Rope length, side by side: add the two pole diameters in inches and allow 3 ft of rope per inch, e.g. 2.5 in + 3.5 in = 6 in, so 18 ft (Peschke's Pioneering merit badge pamphlet, quoted by Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION'); the scouting Fieldbook says '1 yard of rope for each inch of the combined diameters' (quoted by Fine Gardening), the same rule; for a 1/4 in bamboo spar on a 1 x 2 in stake one gardener cuts about 5 ft of string and still often runs short (Fine Gardening).
- Rope size: 1/4 in manila when the two spars together are 6 in diameter or less, 3/8 in above that (Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION').
- Turns, side by side: square lashing with three or four wrapping turns and three or four frapping turns (Animated Knots 'Square Lashing'); three wraps and two fraps (Scout Pioneering); 'two or three or even four really tight turns, known as frapping turns' (Fine Gardening). Shear lashing: eight or ten wraps, then fraps (Scout Pioneering). Tripod: about six racking turns, then two or three fraps in each gap (Animated Knots).
- Pick the lashing by how the poles meet: square lashing for poles crossing at about 45-90 degrees (Animated Knots; Wikipedia 'Pioneering (scouting)'); diagonal lashing, started with a timber hitch, where crossing poles don't touch, as at the middle of an X-brace (Scout Pioneering; Animated Knots); shear lashing for two poles whose far ends spread into an A-frame; tripod lashing for three legs; round lashing to make a longer pole (Wikipedia 'Lashing (ropework)').
- Shear legs and A-frames: tie the wraps somewhat loose, spread the legs to the required distance, then frap and finish (Scout Pioneering 'Lashing INFORMATION').
- Tripod: don't over-tighten; unlike other lashings, 'it is possible to make it too tight!', which can stop the legs spreading or overload the rope (Animated Knots 'Tripod Lashing').
- Lengthening a pole (round lashing): overlap the poles well and use two lashings near the ends of the overlap, each of 6-8 turns (Animated Knots) or 8-10 turns (Scout Pioneering) with no frapping turns; drive wedges between the poles to tighten (Animated Knots); on smooth poles add half hitches to the clove hitches (Scout Pioneering). For comparison, Hong Kong's code for professional bamboo scaffolds asks for overlaps of 1.5-2 m (standards) or at least 2 m (ledgers), ties no more than 300 mm apart, and each tie made with at least 5 rounds of nylon strip (Labour Department, Code of Practice for Bamboo Scaffolding Safety, 5th ed. 2024, printed pp. 30-31).
- Tighten frapping turns with your legs: wrap the rope round a stick, stand on the poles with knees bent, hold the stick across your thighs and straighten up (Animated Knots, crediting Dana Holgate).
- Garden canes: push uprights in about 20 cm apart, tie the tops of two rows together into A-frames and lay a horizontal cane along the crossing for strength; wigwams use three or four canes (RHS). A freestanding trellis needs its support stakes buried about 2 ft, or about 6 in if it leans on something (NPS 'Building with Bamboo').

## Common failures

Joint slides or rotates because the wraps were loose or the frapping turns too few or too slack; make every wrap under strain and frap as tightly as possible (Scout Pioneering; Animated Knots). Frame racks (leans out of square) because it has no diagonal bracing; add an X-brace with diagonal lashings (Wikipedia 'Lashing (ropework)'). Starting clove hitch slips on smooth poles; add half hitches, or start with a timber hitch (Scout Pioneering; Animated Knots). Gap left between crossing poles that don't touch; use a diagonal lashing whose timber hitch springs them together, because 'A clove hitch around one pole could not be used to pull the poles together and might come untied' (Animated Knots 'Diagonal Lashing'). Round lashing slips because it is hard to get tight; use two lashings far apart plus wedges (Animated Knots). Tripod lashed too tight to spread, or the rope overloaded when spread (Animated Knots). Rope too short to finish the lashing; join on more rope rather than leaving it short (Scout Pioneering). Lashings loosen as poles dry or rope gets wet, or rope rots outdoors; re-tighten or replace (Scout Pioneering; Wikipedia 'Rope').

## Safety

Check any lashed structure under load before people climb or stand on it, and never rely on a home-made lashing to hold a person without experienced supervision. Scouting practice: 'Before allowing general use, run a complete test to see everything is working correctly', keep checking anchors as strain is applied, limit how many people are on a platform, and climb on only 'after all lashings are tight, and the structure has been completely inspected'; under US camp accreditation rules, a project lifting participants more than 6 ft must be formally reviewed, or the climber top-rope belayed (Scout Pioneering '22 Pioneering Safety Points'). Professional bamboo scaffolds may only be built by trained workers under a competent person, and those over 15 m must be designed by a professional engineer (Hong Kong Labour Department code); Hong Kong began phasing bamboo scaffolding out in 2025, citing 23 deaths since 2018 (Wikipedia 'Bamboo scaffolding'). Keep fingers clear when lifting a spar to frap a tripod or shear lashing, don't stand on spars lying on the ground because they roll, and take care with mallets (Scout Pioneering). Over-tightening a tripod lashing can break the rope (Animated Knots).

- Reciprocal frame (self-supporting stick bridge): crossing sticks that hold each other up; its crossings can be lashed
- Clamp-on tube fittings (slip-on structural fittings): hardware that joins tubes at the same kinds of crossings and corners
- Cross-halving slot joint (egg-crate joint): a cut joint for two members crossing, for flat stock rather than round poles
- Japanese interlocking joints (splices and three-way corners): cut joints that lengthen beams or join three members without rope
- Hitches to a post, rail, ring or eye (clove hitch, round turn and two half hitches): the clove and timber hitches that start and finish lashings
- Binding knots and seizings (constrictor knot, seizing, cable lacing): tight wraps round a bundle; a quick tie for light canes
- Basic knots (overhand knot, half hitch, reef knot): the reef knot finishes West Country and strop lashings
- Linear ratchet (cable-tie ratchet): cable ties are the quick hardware alternative for light canes
- Hose clamp (worm-drive clamp, ear clamp, spring hose clamp): a metal band alternative for clamping poles together
- Clamp-on accessory mount for bars, poles and edges (handlebar clamp, desk clamp, U-bolt): fixes a part to a pole with hardware
- Rope-end finishing (whipping, heat-sealing, back splice, aglet): whip lashing-rope ends so they last
