A Chicago screw is a hollow post with a thread inside and a matching screw that threads into it from the other side, clamping leather, straps, paper, plastic or printed parts between two smooth, low heads like a rivet, but one that can be unscrewed to swap a buckle or used as a pivot pin.

**In plain words:** A two-piece stud: a short tube with a thread inside it, and a screw that goes in from the other side, leaving a smooth flat head on each face, so it looks and works like a rivet that you can unscrew. **Proper term:** Chicago screw (sex bolt, screw and post, binding screw and barrel), a type of mating fastener.

**Also called:** sex bolt, mating fastener (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'); Chicago screw, Chicago binding screw and post (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; Weaver; Tandy); screw post, post and screw, screw and post, screw-in-post fastener (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; Pet Hardware blog); binding barrel and screw, binding screw, book screw, bookbinding screw (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; Printables KeyCarry); barrel nut, barrel bolt, sleeve nut, female screw, connector bolt, splint screw (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'); architectural bolt: the version with a knurled barrel for one-sided assembly (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'); paper rivet, binding post: cosplay names (Adam Savage's Favorite Tools, 'Chicago Screws' episode summary on TheTVDB); not the electrical binding post.

## How it works

The post is pushed through the holes in all the layers from one side, and the screw goes in from the other; tightening the screw pulls the two heads together and clamps the material between them, like a nut and bolt whose nut is hidden inside a smooth head (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'). The post is chosen a little shorter than the total thickness, so the heads press on the material before the screw head reaches the end of the post (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; Pet Hardware blog). Undoing the screw takes the joint apart again, for example to change a belt buckle; a drop of [threadlocker](../glossary.md#threadlocker) on the thread makes it semi-permanent but still removable 'with sufficient force' (Pet Hardware blog; Weaver). Left with a little free play, or through a stack of keys and washers, it works as a pivot pin, as in printed key organisers (Printables KeyCarry).

## Parts

A few parts: a barrel (post) with a flat or domed head and an internally threaded [boss](../glossary.md#boss), and a matching machine screw with a slotted head; sometimes washers or spacers between the layers (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; Pet Hardware blog; Printables KeyCarry).

## Everyday examples

- Leather belts with a buckle that unscrews so it can be swapped (Weaver; Soldier Systems Daily)
- Bound books and binders held together by screw posts, which is why they are also called 'book screws' and 'binding barrels'; uses run 'from book binding to installing bathroom partitions' (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt')
- Knife sheaths, holsters, bags and dog collars (Tandy; Pet Hardware blog)
- Pocket key organisers that hold a stack of keys on two screw posts (Printables KeyCarry)

## When to use it

Use a Chicago screw when you want the neat, flat look of a rivet but need to take the joint apart later: interchangeable belt buckles, replaceable snap hooks on leashes, repairs, adjustable or customisable goods (Pet Hardware blog; Weaver). It suits thick leather stacks, webbing, book binding, and printed or laser-cut parts that must come apart, and it makes a low, smooth pivot for key organisers, folding parts and hinged cosplay armour where nuts and bolt ends would scratch (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; Printables KeyCarry; Adam Savage's Favorite Tools). It also needs no hammering, so there is 'no risk of unintentionally deforming or damaging the material' as can happen with rivets (Pet Hardware blog).

## When not to use it

For a cheap, permanent, tamper-proof joint use a rivet (Leather and textile rivets). Where only one side can be reached, use a blind threaded fixing, a [heat-set insert](../glossary.md#heat-set-insert) or a [captive nut](../glossary.md#captive-nut) in a printed part. For a precise, low-play pivot under load, use a shoulder bolt or bearing (Plain pivot and rolling bearing). Where it will be flexed, shaken or vibrated a lot, add threadlocker or choose a riveted or sewn joint, because Chicago screws can back out (Soldier Systems Daily). Never use it in anything that holds a person, such as a climbing harness or safety strap. For thin fabric or soft webbing, add washers on both sides so the heads can't pull through (leatherworker.net 'Strong rivet reinforcement for webbing').

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: fixes buckles, straps, loops, holsters, sheaths, binders and armour plates together like a rivet, but removable (Weaver; Tandy; Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; Adam Savage's Favorite Tools)
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): the pivot of a key organiser or of hinged cosplay armour plates that 'can move without nuts and bolts sticking out' (Printables KeyCarry; Adam Savage's Favorite Tools)
- **Movement:** none when clamped tight; rotate (any angle) when used as a pivot, e.g. keys swinging out of a key organiser or armour plates hinging (Printables KeyCarry; Adam Savage's Favorite Tools).
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 when clamped; 1 (turning about the screw) when used as a pivot.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it clamps like a small nut and bolt; its point is the flat, smooth head on both faces and that it can be undone
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping ([force-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): screw tension clamps the layers between the two heads, and thread friction, or threadlocker, keeps the screw from turning (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; Pet Hardware blog)
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the heads are larger than the hole, and the barrel passing through the hole locates the parts and carries sideways ([shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear)) load (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt')
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Light to medium; no strength ratings for Chicago screws in leather or plastic were found ⚑ [uncertain]. The barrel carries sideways (shear) load through the hole, and the clamping force is limited by the small thread and by soft material compressing. They are used on belts, holsters, sheaths, collars, leashes and harnesses (Tandy; Pet Hardware blog), but can loosen and back out with repeated flexing and movement unless threadlocked or checked: one belt maker recalled customers after 'a few reports of the Chicago screws loosening and backing out' (Soldier Systems Daily, 2015). Never for anything holding a person.
- **Play and precision:** As a pivot it has small play, because the barrel diameter 'should be slightly less than the installation hole' (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'); it is not a precision bearing. As a clamp, a post that is too long leaves the joint loose (Weaver; Pet Hardware blog).
- **Taking it apart:** removable: unscrew it with a flat-blade screwdriver; Chicago screws 'can be opened and reattached as needed on things like belts, purses, bags, and valet trays' (Weaver). With threadlocker it becomes semi-permanent but can still be removed 'with sufficient force if repairs become necessary' (Pet Hardware blog).
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: it is meant to be undone and refitted whenever a buckle or part is changed (Weaver; Pet Hardware blog); small threads in soft brass or aluminium, and screws set with threadlocker, will wear or seize sooner ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** partly: the joint can be tightened, loosened or taken apart and parts swapped, but the clamping length is set by the post; key organisers take more or fewer keys by changing the screw length and spacers (Printables KeyCarry; Pet Hardware blog).
- **Access needed:** both sides: the post goes in from one face and the screw from the other, and the post usually has to be held still while the screw is turned (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; leatherworker.net). Some architectural sex bolts have knurled barrels that grip the hole to allow 'one-sided assembly' (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt').
- **Materials:** Made in 'aluminium, steel, brass, copper, stainless steel and plastic', finished anodised, zinc, brass, chrome, bronze or nickel plated or black oxide (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'). For leather and pet goods: brass is naturally corrosion-resistant and patinas; stainless steel has 'Exceptional resistance to water and heavy loads'; plated steel is 'Affordable, strong'; zinc alloy takes detailed decorative finishes (Pet Hardware blog). Joins leather (which compresses and takes a permanent dent under the head), coated waterproof webbing (which compresses very little, so measurements are accurate) (Pet Hardware blog), paper and card (book binding), wood, plastic and thin metal: uses range 'from book binding to installing bathroom partitions', fitness apparatus, playground equipment, athletic helmets and knives (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'), plus 3D-printed key organisers and cosplay armour (Printables; specops501st forum).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Corrosion: brass is naturally corrosion-resistant, stainless steel has 'Exceptional resistance to water', and plated steel is for general use (Pet Hardware blog). Loosening: repeated flexing and movement can unscrew them; belt makers have seen screws 'loosening and backing out' (Soldier Systems Daily). Leather compresses under the head and keeps the dent (Pet Hardware blog), so the joint can slacken over time ⚑ [uncertain]. Threadlocker: the low-strength (222) and medium-strength (243) grades are rated to about 150 °C (300 °F) and 180 °C (360 °F) in service, and [cure](../glossary.md#cure) slowly on inactive metals such as stainless steel and zinc plating (Henkel guide pp. 6 and 8); it can stress-crack some plastics (Loctite Threadlocker Manual).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: metal screw-and-post sets, fitted by hand
  - 3D print (FDM): printed parts with holes for Chicago screws (key organisers, cases, cosplay armour), printed washers and spacers, and drilling jigs
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the screw and post are bought metal parts fitted through holes in the printed or leather parts.
- **Tools:** A hole punch (or a drill for hard materials), a flat-blade screwdriver that fits the slot closely in width and thickness (Pet Hardware blog), and something to stop the smooth post spinning while you tighten: a piece of rubber or an eraser under it, a dab of hot glue, or special pliers such as Weaver's Malan Chicago screw tool (leatherworker.net 'Tightening and loosing chicago screws'). Optional threadlocker (Weaver; Pet Hardware blog).
- **Difficulty:** easy: punch or drill a hole and screw it together; the skills are choosing the post length and stopping the smooth post spinning while tightening (Pet Hardware blog; leatherworker.net).

### 3D printing tips

1) Key organisers: the KeyCarry design uses 5 mm Chicago screws ('also known as Book or Bookbinding screws'), or 4 mm ones for keys with narrow holes, stacked 'Keyholder half - washer - key - washer - key - washer - Keyholder half', with washers added on the second screw to match; printed spacers should be printed 'with a brim', and there is a version for ordinary M3 screws, nuts and washers (Printables 837519); another design uses printed 0.5 mm spacers between keys (Printables 1246908), and a third simply uses M3 screws cut to length (Printables 125291). 2) Holes: make the hole so the barrel just slides in; the barrel should be 'slightly less than the installation hole' (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'); FDM holes often print a little undersize, so test-print one first ⚑ [uncertain]. 3) Stop the post spinning in plastic: one cosplayer 'embedded 1/2 of a Chicago stud into the knee plate with j weld' (J-B Weld, brand) and screwed the other plate to it (specops501st forum); a hex or knurled pocket that grips the post's head is another way ⚑ [uncertain]. 4) Threadlocker near prints: anaerobic threadlockers 'can attack certain plastics and lead to stress cracking', and Henkel rates ABS, ASA, acrylic, polycarbonate, polystyrene and PVC as 'Often not suitable' (Loctite Threadlocker Manual, PDF pp. 71 and 122); put a tiny drop on the metal threads only and keep it off the print; PLA and PETG aren't in the table ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) Drilling jigs: a printed jig drills straight, evenly spaced 13/64 in holes in belt links for Chicago screws (Printables 1113451). 6) Tighten firmly but not hard: 'With steel variants, excessive force can damage the thread' (Pet Hardware blog), and printed parts can [creep](../glossary.md#creep) under a constant clamp, so recheck tightness ⚑ [uncertain].

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Chicago screws / screw posts / binding barrels and screws / sex bolts, sold by post (barrel) diameter, post length and head diameter, with standard slotted, double-slotted and decorative heads (Pet Hardware blog; Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'; Weaver; Tandy); architectural sex bolts with knurled barrels (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'); washers and printed spacers; low-strength threadlocker (Loctite 222, purple) or medium (Loctite 243, blue) (Henkel threadlocking guide); Chicago screw pliers (leatherworker.net). Search terms: 'Chicago screw', 'screw post', 'binding barrel', 'sex bolt', 'book screw'.

## Rules of thumb

- Post length: shorter than the material, side by side: 'Normally the length of the barrel is shorter than the thickness of the material being clamped' (Wikipedia 'Sex bolt'); choose a post '1-1.5 mm shorter than the total measured material thickness', e.g. two 2.5 mm layers (5 mm) take a 4 mm post; too long and 'the connection will remain loose', too short and 'the thread won't engage at all' (Pet Hardware blog); 'The screw post might look too small, but that is normal. You don't want the post to be too long because then it will be loose' (Weaver).
- Measure the total thickness of every layer at the fastening point; leather compresses when tightened, coated webbing hardly at all (Pet Hardware blog).
- Hole: 'should match the post diameter exactly' so the post fits snugly; if it drops in too easily it may spin during tightening (Pet Hardware blog).
- Threadlocker or not: if it is never meant to come apart, add 'a drop of Loctite threadlocker'; if you plan to swap buckles, 'skip the adhesive' and just tighten firmly (Pet Hardware blog; Weaver).
- Threadlocker strength: low strength (purple, e.g. Loctite 222) is 'Ideal for fasteners <1/4" (6 mm)', with 'Easy disassembly using hand tools', and 222 is also recommended for 'low-strength metals, such as aluminum or brass'; medium (blue) is meant for 1/4-3/4 in fasteners (Henkel Threadlocking User's Guide pp. 6 and 8). Leatherworkers commonly use blue on Chicago screws (leatherworker.net). Typical cure: about 10 minutes to handle, 24 hours to full strength (Henkel guide p. 8).
- Metal matters for threadlocker: brass, copper and plain steel are 'active' and cure readily; stainless steel, zinc plating and anodised aluminium are 'inactive', so use a [primer](../glossary.md#primer) or a primerless grade (Henkel guide p. 6).
- Community stand-ins for threadlocker: nail polish or superglue 'in a pinch' (leatherworker.net), or candle wax or crayon rubbed into the threads to resist loosening while staying removable (Soldier Systems Daily, quoting a belt maker).
- Without threadlocker, check and retighten belt screws every so often (Soldier Systems Daily, reader comment).
- When replacing a screw post in leather, keep the same head diameter so it sits in the old dent (Pet Hardware blog).

## Common failures

The post spins while you tighten or loosen the screw, because its smooth head has no grip: hold it on rubber or an eraser, glue it temporarily, or use Chicago screw pliers (leatherworker.net 'Tightening and loosing chicago screws'). The screw works loose and backs out over time: use threadlocker, wax or regular retightening (Soldier Systems Daily; leatherworker.net). The joint is loose or rotates because the post is too long, or the thread won't catch because it is too short (Pet Hardware blog; Weaver). Stripped threads from overtightening, especially steel versions (Pet Hardware blog). A scratched head from a badly fitting screwdriver (Pet Hardware blog). A screw that won't come out after strong threadlocker, or cracking of a plastic part from threadlocker (Henkel guide; Loctite Threadlocker Manual).

## Safety

A flat screwdriver 'can slip more easily on smooth metal surfaces', scratching the work and your hand (Pet Hardware blog). A loose Chicago screw on a belt, holster, sheath or leash can let go and drop what it holds, so check it (Soldier Systems Daily). Never use it in anything that holds a person. Threadlocker: follow the label and avoid skin contact ⚑ [uncertain]. The small screws and posts can be swallowed by young children ⚑ [uncertain].
