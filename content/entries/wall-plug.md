A wall plug is a plastic (once fibre or wood) sleeve that you push into a hole drilled in brick, block, stone or concrete; driving a screw into it spreads the sleeve against the hole so the screw can hold a shelf, bracket or cabinet, and bigger metal or resin anchors do the same job for heavy loads.

**In plain words:** A small plastic sleeve you tap into a hole drilled in a brick or concrete wall; when you drive a screw into it, the sleeve swells and grips the sides of the hole, so the screw holds in a wall too hard and crumbly to take a thread. **Proper term:** Wall plug (plastic expansion anchor, screw anchor); its heavy-duty relatives are masonry anchors: wedge anchor, sleeve anchor, drop-in anchor, chemical (bonded) anchor and concrete screw.

**Also called:** wall plug (UK); anchor or plastic anchor (US); rawlplug or 'rawl plug' (UK everyday name taken from the Rawlplug brand; the plug was invented in 1911) (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'); Rawlplug (brand) and Fischer plug (brand, the plastic plug Artur Fischer designed in the late 1950s) (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'); expansion plug; plastic expansion anchor; nylon plug; screw anchor; masonry plug; 'dowel' (a common translation of the German 'Dübel', used on Printables model pages); split-ribbed plastic anchor; conical screw anchor (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'); universal plug; all-purpose plug: a plug that expands in solid walls and folds or knots in hollow ones (fischer UX and DuoPower pages); masonry anchor; concrete anchor; post-installed anchor (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'); wedge anchor; stud anchor; expansion bolt; torque-controlled expansion anchor (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Eugene Fasteners); sleeve anchor; Rawlbolt (brand; the first mechanical anchor, made by Rawlplug in the 1930s) (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'; Eugene Fasteners) ⚑ [uncertain: Wikipedia does not say which modern anchor type the original Rawlbolt was]; drop-in anchor; internally threaded expansion anchor; displacement-controlled anchor (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Simpson Strong-Tie setting-tool page); chemical anchor; bonded anchor; adhesive anchor; resin anchor; injection anchor (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; fischer); concrete screw; masonry screw; screw anchor; self-tapping concrete screw; Tapcon (brand) (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Confast).

## How it works

You drill a hole the same size as the plug, tap the plug in until its collar is flush with the wall, then drive a screw through your bracket into it. The screw is fatter than the hole in the middle of the plug, so as it goes in it forces the two halves of the plug apart; the soft plastic squeezes into every bump of the hole and presses hard on its walls, and ribs and fins stop it spinning or sliding out (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'; Rawlplug). In hollow bricks and blocks, universal plugs instead fold or tie themselves into a knot behind the thin brick walls, so they hold by shape rather than by squeezing (fischer UX: 'expands in solid building materials and knots in hollow building materials'). Heavy-duty metal anchors work the same way with a steel cone pulled into a split sleeve or clip; chemical anchors glue a threaded rod into the hole with resin, and concrete screws cut their own thread into the concrete (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Eugene Fasteners; Confast).

## Parts

A few parts. Plain wall plug: one moulded plastic sleeve (split along its length, with ribs, fins or teeth on the outside and a small collar at the front), used with your own wood or chipboard screw (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'; Rawlplug R-OLD data sheet). Universal plugs such as the fischer DuoPower are moulded from two plastics in one piece (fischer). Heavy-duty variants are small assemblies that act as one unit: wedge anchor = threaded stud with a cone on the end, a wedge clip (expansion clip), washer and nut; sleeve anchor = bolt or stud, cone, full-length split sleeve, washer and nut; drop-in anchor = internally threaded shell with an expander plug inside, set with a setting tool; chemical anchor = two-part resin cartridge, threaded rod and, in hollow masonry, a mesh sleeve; concrete screw = a single hardened screw with a sharp, cutting thread (Eugene Fasteners; Simpson Strong-Tie setting-tool page; fischer FIS H N; Confast).

## Everyday examples

- The plugs behind almost every shelf bracket, curtain rail, bathroom cabinet, mirror and picture hook on a brick or concrete wall (Rawlplug R-OLD and fischer DuoPower application lists)
- Radiators, TV mount brackets, kitchen cabinets, gutter brackets and house numbers on brick (Homebuilding)
- A furniture or TV anti-tip strap screwed to a brick wall with concrete screws (Anchor It)
- Bolts holding a handrail, machine or steel post to a concrete floor, which use wedge, sleeve or chemical anchors (Eugene Fasteners; Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')

## When to use it

Use a wall plug whenever you need to screw something to brick, block, stone or concrete: shelves, brackets, rails, hooks, cabinets and printed holders. Plastic plugs and universal plugs cover light and medium loads in most solid walls, and universal plugs also cope with perforated brick and unknown walls (fischer UX and DuoPower). Use concrete screws for quick, strong fixings in concrete, brick or block that can be unscrewed later, such as anti-tip kits and battens (Anchor It; Confast). Use wedge anchors for heavy and structural loads in solid concrete, sleeve anchors for medium loads in mixed or weaker masonry, drop-in anchors where you want a flush threaded socket for a bolt, including overhead, and chemical anchors for hollow or crumbly masonry, near edges, or for the heaviest loads (Eugene Fasteners; Simpson Strong-Tie setting-tool page; fischer FIS H N; Wikipedia 'Wall plug').

## When not to use it

Not for plasterboard (drywall), hollow doors or thin panels: a plain plug just pulls out; use a hollow-wall anchor or screw into a stud (Homebuilding: 'if fixing into plasterboard, Rawlplugs will not work'). Not for wood: screw straight into timber (see 'Wood screw'). Don't put a wedge anchor in hollow block, brick or a mortar joint: 'the clip will simply break out the base material rather than expand against it' (Eugene Fasteners). Don't put an expanding plug in a crumbling wall, where 'the force of the expanding plug may be enough to cause cracking'; use a resin fix instead (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'). For structural, safety-critical or code-required fixings (balustrades, heavy overhead loads, anything people stand on or under), use anchors with a published, approved rating installed exactly to the maker's instructions; 'most sleeve anchors are not code-listed for structural applications' (Eugene Fasteners). For light items on smooth walls where you can't drill, consider stretch-release adhesive strips or double-sided foam tape instead.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: fixes shelves, brackets, cabinets, rails, hooks and machines to brick, block, stone and concrete walls, floors and ceilings (Rawlplug R-OLD applications; fischer DuoPower applications; Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
  - glue or bond: chemical (bonded) anchors glue a threaded rod or bar into the hole with epoxy, polyester or vinylester resin (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
- **Movement:** none: once the screw is tight the plug and fixture can't move
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: the plug is locked in the hole and the screw clamps the fixture to the wall. Before the screw goes in, a loose plug can still turn or slide in the hole, which is why plugs have anti-rotation fins and why the hole size matters.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none of the listed behaviours: a wall plug is simply a permanent anchor point for a screw
  - Its own trick: universal plugs change how they hold to suit the wall: they expand in solid material, and fold or knot behind the webs of hollow bricks and blocks, so one plug can be used when you don't know what the wall is made of (fischer UX; fischer DuoPower)
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping ([force-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): the expanded plug, or the steel clip or sleeve of a wedge or sleeve anchor, presses outward on the walls of the hole and holds by friction (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt': 'friction mechanical interlock guaranteed by expansion forces'; 'Plastic anchors... similar to mechanical expansion anchors')
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): ribs and fins key into the hole; universal plugs knot behind the webs of hollow bricks; resin pushed through a mesh sleeve forms a key in perforated brick; [undercut](../glossary.md#undercut) anchors bear on an undercut in the hole; and a concrete screw's thread bears on the concrete it has cut (fischer UX; fischer FIS H N; Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
  - material bond (glue, solvent or melting): chemical anchors are held by resin bonded to the hole wall and the rod (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt')
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed). A drill, often with hammer action, is only used to make the hole (Confast; Anchor It).
- **Load:** Light to heavy, and it depends far more on the wall than on the plug. fischer's stated maximum loads for a single DuoPower 6 x 50 mm universal plug are 165 kg in concrete, 55 kg in solid brick, 17 kg in vertically perforated brick, 15 kg in aerated concrete and 15 kg in 12.5 mm plasterboard (fischer DuoPower 6 x 50 page). Strongest against a downward ([shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear)) load close to the wall, weaker in straight pull-out (tension), as on a ceiling or a shelf bracket's top screw: Wikipedia's table gives typical figures of 10-20 lb (4.5-9 kg) tension against 20-30 lb (9-14 kg) shear for plastic expansion anchors, and 300-600 lb (140-270 kg) tension for concrete screws (Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor') ⚑ [uncertain: the table cites no test source and doesn't say whether these are safe or breaking loads]. Wedge anchors carry the highest, structural-rated loads but only in solid concrete (Eugene Fasteners). Loads held away from the wall (deep shelves, swing-arm TV mounts) lever on the top fixings and greatly reduce what each anchor can take (TOGGLER FAQ, written for its hollow-wall anchors).
- **Play and precision:** not applicable as a moving joint: once tight there is no play. Position [accuracy](../glossary.md#repeatability--accuracy) depends on marking and drilling; a hole drilled oversize lets the plug spin and holds less (Rawlplug: 'Anti-rotation fins prevent spinning'; Simpson Strong-Tie: 'Oversized holes will make it difficult to set the anchor and will reduce the anchor's load capacity').
- **Taking it apart:** semi-permanent: the screw can be undone and the fixture taken down, but the plug normally stays in the wall (it can be pulled or drilled out, leaving a hole to fill). Wedge and sleeve anchors are 'permanent installations': the nut or bolt can be removed and refitted but the anchor body stays in the hole (Eugene Fasteners). Chemical anchors are permanent. Concrete screws unscrew; a hole that is too shallow can leave the screw 'bound in the hole and unable to be removed' (Confast).
- **How often it can be reused:** a few times: the screw can usually be taken out and put back into the same plug a few times, but each plug is set once; a plug that has been pulled out is normally replaced ⚑ [uncertain: no source gives a number of re-uses].
- **Sealing:** none: a wall plug doesn't seal anything
- **Adjustable:** no: the position is fixed once the hole is drilled. Slotted holes in the bracket, or a French cleat, give some adjustment (see 'Hang-on mounts').
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind): everything is done from the face of the wall; the back of the wall is never reached.
- **Materials:** Plugs: polypropylene or polyethylene (Rawlplug R-OLD: 'impact resistant co-polymer of polypropylene'), nylon (fischer UX and DuoPower), once jute fibre bonded with glue, lead, wood or white bronze (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'). Metal anchors: zinc-plated or stainless carbon steel; chemical anchors: polyester, vinylester or epoxy resin with steel threaded rod (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'; Eugene Fasteners). Walls they suit: concrete, solid brick, sand-lime brick, natural stone and solid block; universal plugs and chemical anchors with mesh sleeves also suit perforated brick, hollow blocks and aerated concrete (fischer DuoPower; fischer FIS H N). Wedge anchors suit solid concrete only, not hollow block, brick or mortar joints; sleeve anchors suit concrete, brick, hollow block and (at low load) mortar joints (Eugene Fasteners). Not for plasterboard on its own: 'if fixing into plasterboard, Rawlplugs will not work' (Homebuilding); see 'Hollow-wall anchors'.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Plastic plugs are used indoors and out, but a steel screw left wet outdoors will rust; use stainless or coated screws outside ⚑ [uncertain: general advice, not from an opened source]. Published plastic-anchor loads assume normal room temperature: for example fischer's nylon plasterboard anchor GK is rated only 'for temperatures in the substrate up to +24 °C (resp. short term up to +40 °C)' (fischer GK load table). Bonded (chemical) anchors lose capacity in wet holes (20% less in wet concrete with polyester resin) and have known issues with 'high temperature behavior and [creep](../glossary.md#creep) response' (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'). Crumbly or damp masonry holds much less, and an expanding plug may crack it (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'). Anchors in cracked concrete and under vibration or earthquake loads need specially rated types (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt': seismic and crack-cycling loads). Plastic plugs may creep or loosen under constant heavy load or vibration ⚑ [uncertain: no opened source gives data].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: wall plugs and masonry anchors are bought; the maker's part is the bracket, holder or shelf fixed with them
  - 3D print (FDM): printed brackets and holders are screwed to walls through [clearance](../glossary.md#clearance) holes into plugs; emergency printed plugs exist, printed in PETG at 100% [infill](../glossary.md#infill), for solid masonry and light loads only (Printables models by Petr Vacek, Skovik, Mape and Marc Herter, read through the Printables API)
  - woodwork: battens, cleats and cabinets fixed to masonry walls with plugs and screws or concrete screws (Anchor It)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: a commercial plug is moulded, and the printed versions on Printables are single plugs that need a screw; nothing is assembled when printed.
- **Tools:** Drill with a masonry bit of the size marked on the plug; hammer action for concrete, hard brick and concrete screws ('The hole must be drilled using a hammer drill with a carbide tipped bit' for Tapcon screws, Confast; 'a masonry bit, a heavy-duty hammer drill, concrete screw anchors, and an impact driver' for brick, Anchor It). A hammer to tap the plug flush, a screwdriver or drill-driver, a pencil, tape measure and level, and a cable, pipe and stud detector before drilling (Electrical Safety First). Heavy anchors add a blow-out pump or compressed air to clean the hole, a spanner or [torque](../glossary.md#torque) wrench for wedge anchors, a setting tool for drop-in anchors and a dispensing gun and brush for chemical anchors (Eugene Fasteners; Simpson Strong-Tie setting-tool page).
- **Difficulty:** easy for plastic plugs and concrete screws: you need the right drill, bit and screw, and a detector to check the wall first. Moderate for wedge, drop-in and chemical anchors, which need exact hole sizes, hole cleaning, a torque wrench or setting tool, and time for resin to [cure](../glossary.md#cure) (Eugene Fasteners; Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt').

### 3D printing tips

1) The plug goes into the wall, not into the print: give the printed bracket a plain clearance hole for the screw (a little bigger than the screw's outer diameter) and let the screw thread grip only the plug ⚑ [uncertain: general practice; not stated by a cited source]. 2) Count the print as the 'fixture' when choosing screw length: screw length = plug length + fixture thickness + 1 x screw diameter (fischer DuoPower and UX), so a 50 mm plug and a 10 mm thick printed bracket with a 5 mm screw needs at least a 65 mm screw. 'The threadless part of the screw must not be longer than the fixture' (fischer), or the plain shank ends up inside the plug and doesn't expand it. 3) Make the area round each screw hole solid (extra [perimeters](../glossary.md#perimeters) or 100% infill) and use a pan or washer-head screw, or a washer, so the clamping force bears on solid plastic; a countersunk head acts as a wedge and can split printed layers ⚑ [uncertain: general printing advice]. 4) Orient the bracket so the load presses layers together rather than peeling them apart, and print hangers for heavy or permanent loads in PETG, ABS or ASA rather than PLA, which creeps under constant load ⚑ [uncertain: general printing advice]. 5) Printed plugs are for emergencies: Petr Vacek's online HmoždinkApp generator suggests PETG (also PLA), 0.3 mm layers or lower, 100% infill, and says the plug 'is best fitting to solid materials, such as solid bricks or concrete' and 'has no official certificates. Experiment with low loads first'; Skovik says for his 10 x 45 mm plug 'PETG is appropriate material', ABS also, 'PLA není vhodný' (PLA is not suitable); Marc Herter printed his 6 x 30 mm plug in PETG at 100% infill; Mape found that thinner walls and a larger gap between the two halves 'works much better', and recommends a large brim (Printables API). Don't use printed plugs for anything overhead, heavy or that could fall on someone. 6) If you want a thread inside the print itself, use a [heat-set insert](../glossary.md#heat-set-insert), printed thread or [captive nut](../glossary.md#captive-nut) instead (see those entries); wall plugs are made for masonry.

## Rules of thumb

- Drill bit = plug diameter: 'a 6mm Rawlplug commonly fits a hole drilled with a 6mm bit', and the plug 'should fit snugly into the hole without being forced in, and it should sit flush with the wall surface' (Homebuilding). Rawlplug's R-OLD plugs: yellow = 5 mm hole for 3.0-5.0 mm screws, red = 6 mm hole for 3.5-5.5 mm screws, brown = 7 mm hole for 5.0-6.0 mm screws (Rawlplug R-OLD data sheet), but 'colour coding can vary between brands' (Homebuilding).
- Match the screw to the plug: use a screw diameter inside the range printed on the pack, e.g. 4.0-5.0 mm wood or chipboard screws for a 6 mm fischer DuoPower (fischer). In UK screw gauges: 5 mm plugs take No. 6-8, 6 mm take No. 8-10, 7-8 mm take No. 10-14 (Homebuilding).
- Screw length = plug length + fixture thickness + 1 x screw diameter (fischer DuoPower and UX).
- Drill deeper than the plug: fischer gives a 60 mm minimum hole depth for a 50 mm DuoPower; Homebuilding's tip is to line the plug up against the bit, add 5 mm and mark the depth with masking tape.
- Get through the plaster: the plug must grip the masonry, not the plaster on top; fischer's longer DuoPower plugs are 'especially suited to... bridge plaster' (fischer DuoPower).
- Keep away from edges: 'The edge distance must be at least one plug length' (fischer UX). Wedge anchors need even more edge distance and concrete thickness because 'the high local pressure at the wedge can crack thin or close-to-edge concrete' (Eugene Fasteners).
- Choose the anchor by the wall: wedge anchors in solid concrete only; sleeve anchors in concrete, brick, hollow block and (low load only) mortar joints (Eugene Fasteners); chemical anchors with a mesh sleeve in perforated brick, where 'the mortar is pressed through the grid structure and creates a form fit' (fischer FIS H N); a resin or gypsum-pad fix in crumbling walls where an expanding plug could crack the wall (Wikipedia 'Wall plug').
- Concrete screws need their own bit size and depth: a 3/16 in screw needs a 5/32 in hole and a 1/4 in screw a 3/16 in hole; embed at least 1 in and at most 1-3/4 in; drill the hole 1/2 in deeper than the screw goes so the dust has somewhere to go (Confast, for Tapcon screws).
- Wedge anchors: drill exactly the anchor diameter and 'at least 1/4" deeper than the published minimum embedment', blow the dust out, and tighten to the published torque with a torque wrench; sleeve anchors are tightened 'snug plus a defined turn count (commonly 3 to 5 turns past snug, per brand spec)' (Eugene Fasteners).
- Chemical anchors: clean the hole: tests showed up to 60% less capacity in a dirty hole, and 20% less in wet concrete with polyester resin (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt').
- Anchor furniture that can tip: 'Anchoring all furniture with drawers, doors, and shelves to the wall prevents tip-over injuries and deaths' (US CPSC, Anchor It); on brick, use concrete screw anchors set with a masonry bit and hammer drill (Anchor It).

## Common failures

1) Plug spins in the hole and the screw won't tighten: hole too big (wrong or worn bit, or a wobbly drill) or soft, crumbly wall; drill the size on the pack and use plugs with anti-rotation fins (Rawlplug; fischer UX). 2) Plug pulls out with the screw: screw too thin or short to expand it, plug sitting in plaster instead of masonry, or overloaded; use the screw range and length rule on the pack and a long enough plug (fischer). 3) Plug won't go in flush, or the screw bottoms out: hole too shallow or full of dust; drill deeper than the plug and clear the dust (fischer; Homebuilding; Confast). 4) Wall cracks or a corner breaks off: fixing too close to an edge or wall too weak; keep at least one plug length from edges (fischer UX) and use resin anchors in crumbly walls (Wikipedia 'Wall plug'). 5) Plug disappears into a void in hollow brick or block: use a universal plug that knots, or a chemical anchor with a mesh sleeve (fischer). 6) Concrete screw jams or snaps: hole too shallow so dust packs the bottom, or wrong bit size (Confast). 7) Chemical anchor pulls out: hole not cleaned (up to 60% loss) or resin not cured (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'). 8) Masonry anchor failure modes in tension: pull-out, concrete cone break-out, splitting near an edge, or the steel itself breaking (Wikipedia 'Anchor bolt'). 9) Drilled into a cable or pipe: check with a detector first (Electrical Safety First).

## Safety

Check for hidden cables and pipes before drilling: 'A common error is accidentally drilling, nailing, or screwing into cables hidden inside your walls. A quality cable detector can help track buried cables before you start work', and cables buried in walls are required to run 'either horizontally or vertically from electrical accessories like socket-outlets or switches', so avoid those lines (Electrical Safety First, UK). A damaged gas pipe 'can result in a gas leak, fire or explosion'; take care when drilling (Cadent Gas). Drilling concrete, brick and mortar makes fine silica dust (respirable crystalline silica), which causes lung disease; HSE says FFP3 is 'the most advisable type' of mask for work that 'does or could create high dust levels or involves silica' (HSE construction dust FAQ). Wear eye protection when drilling masonry ⚑ [uncertain: general advice]. Heavy, overhead and tip-prone loads need anchors rated for the load and the wall, fitted to the maker's instructions; furniture with drawers, doors and shelves should be anchored to the wall to prevent tip-overs (US CPSC, Anchor It). Ceiling fixings carry the full load in pull-out, the direction anchors are weakest (Wikipedia 'Drywall anchor' table), and a failure drops the load on whoever is below.

## Related

- Hollow-wall anchors (toggle, molly, self-drilling plasterboard anchor): for plasterboard, hollow doors and panels, where a plain plug would pull straight out
- Wood screw (incl. coach screw): what usually goes into a wall plug, and what you screw directly into a timber stud or batten
- Internal tube anchors (star nut, expansion plug): the same expanding-sleeve idea used inside the end of a tube
- Press-fit and expanding inserts (tap-in insert): expanding threaded inserts for holes in parts rather than walls
- Blind threaded fixings (rivet nut, well nut): blind threads for thin sheet; a rubber well nut can also anchor in a blind hole by squashing outward, like a plug
- Hang-on mounts (keyhole slot, French cleat): the hanging hardware usually screwed to the wall with plugs
- Modular grid mounting systems (pegboard, Gridfinity-style grids): boards fixed to walls with plugs and screws
- Metal brackets and plates (incl. furniture anti-tip ties): steel brackets screwed to walls through plugs
- Threaded rod and studs: the rod set in resin in a chemical anchor
- Eye bolts, screw eyes and hooks: often screwed straight into a wall plug
- Stretch-release adhesive strips and double-sided foam tape: no-drill alternatives for light items on smooth walls
- Two-part epoxy and epoxy putty: related resins; chemical anchors are a purpose-made version for holes in masonry
- Heat-set threaded insert: the way to put a strong thread into a 3D print, not a wall
