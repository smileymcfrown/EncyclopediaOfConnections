A panel standoff is a two-part decorative fixing, a threaded barrel fixed to the wall and a cap screwed into its front, that clamps a drilled sheet of acrylic, glass, aluminium composite or metal between them so the panel is held a set distance off the wall with no frame or glue.

**In plain words:** A shiny metal post screwed to the wall with a cap screwed into its front, which pinches a drilled sheet of acrylic, glass or metal so the sign floats a little way off the wall. **Proper term:** Panel standoff (sign standoff, standoff screw, stand-off mount).

**Also called:** standoff, standoff screw, sign standoff, standoff mount (4over4); stand-off, sign stand-off, wall mount (Sign Trade Supplies); glass standoff, standoff cap assembly (CRL 'Standoff Cap Assembly'); through-grip standoff, edge-grip standoff (4over4); male-female, male-male and female-female standoffs (4over4); barrel and cap (the two visible parts; 4over4); mirror screw with domed cap, mirror cap, dome screw cover (a face-fixing screw with a cap, no barrel; LION; vxb); glass-mount standoff (adhesive-pad or clamp style for glass walls; 4over4); printed sign standoff (Printables).

## How it works

The barrel is screwed to the wall (through a wall plug or into a stud) and its length sets the gap behind the panel; the panel's drilled hole goes over the cap's threaded stud and the cap is screwed into the front of the barrel, so the panel is sandwiched between the barrel's end and the cap (4over4: 'The barrel anchors to the wall and sets how far the panel floats, and the cap clamps the sign panel in place'). Edge-grip standoffs clamp the panel's edge instead, so the face needs no holes; through-grip ones pass through a hole and hold heavier panels more securely (4over4). Unlike a plain spacer (an unthreaded sleeve a bolt passes through), a standoff is threaded, so it fastens as well as spaces (4over4). Mirror screws do the same job with no gap: the screw goes through the drilled mirror into the wall and a domed cap winds into the screw head to hide it (LION).

## Parts

A few parts per fixing: wall screw (and wall anchor), barrel (the spacer that sets the gap), cap with a threaded stud that passes through the panel hole, and for glass a clear vinyl washer (4over4; CRL). Edge-grip standoffs replace the cap with a clamp that grips the panel edge (4over4). A mirror screw is two parts: a screw with a tapped head and a domed cap wound into it, plus a plastic washer (LION; vxb).

## Everyday examples

- the clear acrylic logo sign behind a reception desk, held off the wall by four shiny posts (4over4)
- a glass or acrylic door name plate or room number plaque
- a bathroom mirror or splashback held by screws with chrome dome caps (RealHomes; LION)
- laser-cut acrylic house numbers or menu boards in cafes (4over4)

## When to use it

When you want a sheet panel (acrylic, glass, aluminium composite, metal, plywood) to look like it floats off the wall with visible, tidy hardware, can be swapped later without a frame, and is light enough for a few fixings into the wall. Good for laser-cut and printed acrylic signs, name plates, wall art, glass splashbacks and display panels, and for holding a panel clear of an uneven or wet wall.

## When not to use it

Not for heavy panels on weak walls (use fixings into studs or masonry, a French cleat, or a framed mount). Not for undrilled toughened glass: use edge-grip standoffs, mirror clips or glass clamps instead. Not where you don't want holes through the panel face: use edge-grip standoffs, rail or cable systems, or double-sided tape for very light signs. Not for holding a circuit board inside a case: that is a PCB standoff (see Shims, spacers and standoffs). Not for outdoor signs in strong wind on long barrels: use short barrels and more fixings (4over4).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: fixes a sheet panel to a wall, post or other panel
  - line parts up exactly: sets the panel a fixed, even distance off the wall
- **Movement:** none
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: the panel is clamped between barrel and cap at every fixing point
- **Special behaviour:**
  - quick-release ⚑ [uncertain: no opened source says caps are meant to be taken off often]: the cap unscrews by hand, so the panel can be swapped without taking the barrels off the wall ('a panel can be swapped without tearing out a frame', 4over4)
  - none of the other listed behaviours
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping ([force-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): the cap squeezes the panel against the barrel
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the cap's stud passes through the panel hole, so the panel can't slide off even if the clamp is light; the barrel and cap hold by their screw threads
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Light to medium: for signs and glass name plates, not for anything that hangs heavy loads or people. The wall fixing is usually the limit: 'four standoffs in plain drywall anchors are good for a lightweight acrylic panel under about 10 pounds'; into studs, masonry or toggle bolts they carry far more (4over4). Adhesive glass-mount standoffs hold lighter panels, 'under about five pounds' (4over4). The panel is weakest at its holes (cracking from the edge or from over-tightening).
- **Play and precision:** Small: the panel hole is drilled slightly larger than the stud (e.g. a 9 mm hole for a 7.5 mm thread; Sign Trade Supplies), so the panel can shift a little before the cap is tight; acrylic needs extra [clearance](../glossary.md#clearance) for expansion (ACRYLITE: hole at least 2 times the bolt diameter). Even small tilts show on a floating sign, so level the barrels carefully ('Even an eighth of an inch of tilt shows', 4over4).
- **Taking it apart:** removable: the cap unscrews by hand (no tools for most caps) and the barrels unscrew from the wall; the wall holes remain (4over4)
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: the metal hardware lasts; the panel's holes may wear or crack if over-tightened repeatedly
- **Sealing:** none; outdoors, seal the wall anchor holes with silicone to keep water out (4over4)
- **Adjustable:** no once fitted: the gap is set by the barrel length (change barrels to change it); slots or oversized panel holes allow small sideways adjustment and expansion (ACRYLITE)
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind): everything is fitted from the front, the barrel screws to the wall and the cap screws in from the face of the panel
- **Materials:** Hardware: stainless steel (most common, and best outdoors), brushed or anodised aluminium, brass, chrome-plated steel, nylon or plastic (4over4; Sign Trade Supplies). Panels it suits: acrylic (PMMA, the usual choice), aluminium composite panel (Dibond (brand)), PVC foam board, metal sheet, and glass with holes made before toughening (4over4; Wikipedia 'Tempered glass'). Most hardware takes panels from about 1/8 to 1/2 in (3-12 mm) thick (4over4; Sign Trade Supplies). Not suited to thin flexible sheet that sags between fixings, or toughened glass that has not been drilled by the maker.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Outdoors use stainless hardware, seal anchor holes with silicone, allow room for acrylic to expand in heat and use short barrels against wind (4over4). Acrylic's thermal and moisture movement means tight holes crack (ACRYLITE). Ammonia cleaners cloud acrylic (4over4). Printed standoffs [creep](../glossary.md#creep) under load, PLA worst (Printables 954045). Glass and metal hardware are unaffected by normal indoor temperatures.

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part (sign standoffs, glass standoff cap assemblies, mirror screws)
  - laser cut (acrylic or wood panels with the holes cut in)
  - CNC (routed or drilled panels)
  - 3D print (FDM): printed screw-on standoffs and hidden standoffs for light signs (Printables)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: barrel and cap print as separate parts that screw or press together; one printed design uses bicycle spokes as the pins through the sign (Printables 1658818)
- **Tools:** drill, level, screwdriver and wall anchors to fit the barrels; a drill bit for plastics (acrylic) or the glass supplier to make holes in glass; caps are usually hand-tightened ('You do not need special tools beyond a drill and a level', 4over4). Some barrels have flats or a hex for a spanner (4over4).
- **Difficulty:** easy: buy the hardware and drill the panel; moderate for glass (holes must be specified to the glass maker before toughening, and a soft washer must keep metal off the glass)

### 3D printing tips

Printed standoffs suit light indoor signs only. Material: one designer prints in PETG at 0.2 mm layers with a 0.4 mm nozzle and warns 'Avoid PLA since it deforms over time under load'; nylon 'would be ideal'; tune for strong layer bonding (Printables 954045). Use metal for the part that carries the sign: one design uses old bicycle spokes as the pins, 'so you don't have to worry about whether the filament is going to be strong enough', with files for 1/8 and 1/4 in signs and 9 mm holes (Printables 1658818). A hidden two-part design screws the base to the wall with #8 flat-head screws (with anchors as needed) and fixes the top part to the sign with #6 screws or glue, then presses the sign on (Printables 954045). Threads: print barrel and cap standing upright so the threads are round, and use a coarse thread; printed fine threads strip ⚑ [uncertain: general FDM practice, no opened source]. A parametric Fusion 360 sign-plus-standoff exists for customising (Printables 1300840).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Search 'sign standoff' or 'stand-off wall mount' (barrel diameter x length, e.g. 13 x 13 mm to 25 x 25 mm or 1/2 to 1 in barrels; Sign Trade Supplies; 4over4), 'glass standoff' or 'standoff cap assembly' with 'clear vinyl washer' (CRL), 'edge grip standoff', 'female-female standoff', 'mirror screws' with 'mirror caps' or 'dome screw covers' and 'mirror washers' (LION; vxb), plus wall anchors suited to the wall (plasterboard toggles, masonry plugs). Follow the hardware maker's table for hole size and panel thickness instead of copying sizes.

## Rules of thumb

- Hole size comes from the hardware maker's table, e.g. 13 x 13 mm stand-off with 7.5 mm thread: panel hole 9 mm, max panel 6 mm; 19 x 19 mm or 25 x 25 mm with 9 mm thread: panel hole 11 mm, max panel 10-12 mm (Sign Trade Supplies). 4over4 instead says holes should sit about 1/16 in wider than the barrel diameter; follow the table for the hardware you bought.
- Edge distance, figures that disagree: 4over4 says keep every hole at least 1 in (25 mm) from every edge ('Acrylic cracks when holes sit less than one inch from the edge'); ACRYLITE says hole centre at least 1.5 times the hole diameter from the edge; Cut My says at least twice the sheet thickness (and drills its own holes at least 20 mm from the edge). The 1 in rule is the safest for signs.
- Acrylic expands with heat and moisture: make bolt holes at least 2 times the bolt diameter, and use a rubber grommet to stop cracks at the hole (ACRYLITE); outdoors 'leave a hair of room rather than over tightening' (4over4).
- Glass (architectural toughened glass): hole rim to nearest edge at least 6 mm (1/4 in) or 2 times the glass thickness, whichever is greater; between holes 10 mm or 2 times the thickness; at least 6.5 times the thickness from a corner tip; hole diameter at least the glass thickness plus 1/16 in, never under 1/4 in (6 mm) (Viracon).
- Toughened (tempered) glass: 'Polishing the edges or drilling holes in the glass is carried out before the tempering process starts'; cutting or grinding after tempering makes it shatter (Wikipedia 'Tempered glass').
- Barrel length is the gap: 1/2 to 2 in; the most common lobby pairing is a 1 in barrel under a 1-1/4 in cap; use 3/4 in barrels for menu boards up to about 18 x 24 in, and shorter barrels outdoors to cut wind load (4over4).
- Four corner standoffs for a rectangle; for panels over 36 in add one every 18-24 in along the long edge to stop bowing (4over4).
- Tighten caps 'hand tight plus a quarter turn'; over-tightening acrylic 'causes stress fractures that spread over time' (4over4).
- Wall fixings: plasterboard anchors for light acrylic under about 10 lb; heavier panels into studs or with toggle bolts; masonry anchors for brick and concrete (4over4).
- Keep text and graphics about 2 in clear of each hole, because the cap covers about 1/2 in round it (4over4).

## Common failures

1) Acrylic cracks at a hole drilled too close to the edge ('the number one cause of ruined panels', 4over4): keep at least 1 in from edges. 2) Stress cracks round holes from over-tightened caps (4over4) or from holes too tight for expansion (ACRYLITE): hand tight plus a quarter turn, oversize holes, use grommets. 3) Wall anchors pull out or plasterboard anchors used in plaster: match the anchor to the wall (4over4). 4) Sign visibly tilted or barrels out of line: mark holes using the panel as a template and use a level (4over4). 5) Large panel bows between corners: add fixings every 18-24 in (4over4). 6) Glass chips or cracks at a metal-to-glass contact: always use the vinyl washer (CRL). 7) Mixed hardware finishes look unfinished: buy from one batch (4over4).

## Safety

Toughened (tempered) glass can't be cut, ground or drilled after toughening, so its holes must be ordered from the glass maker before it is toughened; drilling it later makes it shatter (Wikipedia 'Tempered glass'). Never let metal touch glass directly: fit the clear vinyl washer that protects 'the glass edges around the drilled hole against chipping and scratching' (CRL), and the plastic washer under mirror screws (vxb). Heavy panels need fixings into studs, masonry anchors or toggle bolts; plain plasterboard plugs only suit light panels (4over4). Glass panels and big mirrors are heavy and sharp-edged: lift and hold them with a helper while the caps go on ⚑ [uncertain: no opened source states this for standoffs]. Check for hidden pipes and cables before drilling the wall ⚑ [uncertain: general practice].
