A sharp, coarse-threaded screw that forms its own thread in wood or board and clamps the parts together under its head; with the right pilot and [clearance](../glossary.md#clearance) holes it is the everyday way to fix wood to wood, and the coach screw (big, hex-headed) and Confirmat screw (fat, for chipboard furniture) are its heavy and flat-pack versions.

**In plain words:** A pointed metal screw with a coarse, sharp thread that you drive into wood with a screwdriver or drill; it cuts its own groove in the wood as it goes in, and its head pulls the top piece down tight. **Proper term:** Wood screw (screwed wood joint), including the coach screw (lag screw) and the Confirmat screw.

**Also called:** wood screw; flat-head (countersunk), oval-head (raised countersunk) and round-head wood screws (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8; Wikipedia 'Screw'); coach screw (UK, Australia, New Zealand), lag screw or lag bolt (US), French wood screw (Scandinavia): a large hex-head wood screw turned with a spanner; metric hex-head ones follow DIN 571 (Wikipedia 'Screw', Lag screw section); Confirmat screw, cabinet-connecting screw: a fat, blunt-tipped screw for chipboard and MDF furniture (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'); chipboard screw, particle board screw (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'); drywall screw (bugle head; the fine-thread kind often has a twin-start 'Twinfast' thread) (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'); deck screw, decking screw: corrosion-resistant, bugle head, auger-type cutting tip (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'); tapping screw, self-drilling screw (Tek screw) for fixing wood to steel (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8; Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'); pocket screw (screwed at an angle from a drilled pocket; see Butt and lap wood joints); hanger bolt, dowel screw, double-ended screw (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'); standards: DIN 95, 96 and 97 (slotted oval, round and countersunk wood screws), ANSI B18.6.1 for US inch wood screws, DIN 571 for coach screws (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'; 'Screw').

## How it works

As the screw turns, its sharp, coarse thread cuts or presses a spiral groove into the wood fibres, so the wood itself becomes the nut (Wikipedia 'Screw'). The top piece has a clearance hole, or the screw has a plain shank there, so the thread grips only the bottom piece; the head then bears on the top piece and the screw pulls the two together and clamps them (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types', wood screw). How hard it holds depends on the fibres trapped between the threads: withdrawal strength rises with the square of the wood's density (specific gravity), with the screw diameter and with the length of thread in the wood (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). A correctly sized [pilot hole](../glossary.md#pilot-hole) lets the thread form without the screw acting as a wedge that splits the wood or binds and snaps (Wikipedia 'Pilot hole').

## Parts

Single piece: one screw with a point, a threaded part, often a plain (unthreaded) shank, and a head with a drive recess or hex. It is usually driven into a pre-drilled pilot hole, with a clearance hole and sometimes a [countersink](../glossary.md#counterbore--countersink) in the top piece (Wikipedia 'Screw', 'Pilot hole'; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).

## Everyday examples

- Flat-pack (ready-to-assemble) chipboard furniture held together with Confirmat screws turned with a hex key (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw', 'Furniture screw')
- Decking boards fixed with coated deck screws (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
- Plasterboard (drywall) screwed to wooden or metal studs with drywall screws (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
- Heavy timber framing, and machinery feet fixed to wooden floors, with coach screws (Wikipedia 'Screw', Lag screw section)

## When to use it

Use wood screws whenever you fix wood to wood, or fix brackets, hinges and printed or metal parts onto wood: they are fast with a drill-driver, need access from one side only, pull the joint tight as they go in, and can be undone. Choose a part-threaded wood screw to pull two boards together, a chipboard screw for chipboard and MDF, a deck screw outdoors, a coach screw for heavy timber and big loads where a through-bolt is awkward (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8), and a Confirmat screw for chipboard cabinet corners that may be taken apart and rebuilt (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). Screws add strength and clamp the work while glue sets in glued joints ⚑ [uncertain].

## When not to use it

Avoid screws alone in [end grain](../glossary.md#end-grain), which holds poorly (use dowels, a pocket screw into side grain, or a bracket) (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8; Butt and lap wood joints). Don't use ordinary wood or drywall screws in chipboard or MDF that must be taken apart (use Confirmats, Knock-down cam-lock and cross-dowel connectors, or threaded inserts) (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). Don't use [brittle](../glossary.md#brittle--ductile) drywall screws in hardwood or where they bend or carry a load (woodgears.ca). For structural metal connectors such as joist hangers use only the fasteners the connector maker specifies (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog). For parts taken apart often, use machine screws into threaded inserts or nuts (Ordinary screw joint, Screw-in threaded insert). For masonry and hollow walls use a Wall plug or Hollow-wall anchors. For fine furniture where looks matter, hidden joinery or glue is often preferred ⚑ [uncertain].

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: fixes wood to wood, or metal, plastic and 3D-printed parts to wood, quickly and strongly
- **Movement:** none: a driven screw holds the parts still.
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 once tightened.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it simply holds by its thread in the wood and clamps under its head.
- **How it holds:**
  - shape (the parts interlock; [form-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): the thread sits in the spiral groove it has cut or pressed into the wood (Wikipedia 'Screw')
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the head clamps the top piece onto the bottom piece as the screw is tightened (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types')
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Medium; heavy for coach screws. Strong sideways ([shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear)) and in straight withdrawal from side grain: the Wood Handbook's withdrawal formula p = 108.25 G² D L (N, mm) gives about 2.8 kN ultimate for a #8 (4.17 mm) screw with 25 mm of thread in wood of specific gravity 0.5 (calculated here; safe design loads are far lower) (USDA Wood Handbook eq. 8-10a, Table 8-9). Weak points: pulling out of end grain (averages about 75% of the side-grain value, and erratic), stripping in chipboard and MDF, the head pulling through the top piece, which often limits screwed joints (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8 'Fastener Head Embedment'; woodgears.ca), and brittle hardened screws snapping: 'in hardwoods, a drywall screw will usually fail before the wood fails' (woodgears.ca). Coach screws threaded into the wood about 7 x their shank diameter in dense wood and 10-12 x in light wood develop about the full tensile strength of the screw (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
- **Play and precision:** Before tightening, the clearance hole lets the top piece shift a little; once tight there is no play. Screws are not locating parts: holes drilled by hand wander and a screw driven without a pilot hole drifts, so add dowels or use a jig where parts must line up exactly ⚑ [uncertain]. A Confirmat screw's plain shoulder 'helps hold the screw in position' (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw').
- **Taking it apart:** removable: it can be unscrewed, but each removal wears the thread it cut. In chipboard and MDF, drywall screws 'cannot be removed and re-inserted', while a Confirmat screw presses rather than cuts its thread and 'can therefore be removed and replaced dozens of times' (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw').
- **How often it can be reused:** a few times: in solid wood a screw can usually be refitted into its own hole a few times before the thread strips ⚑ [uncertain]; a Confirmat in chipboard dozens of times, provided the same thread is used (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'); a drywall screw in particleboard not at all (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw').
- **Sealing:** none: the screw does not seal the joint; add glue or sealant if needed.
- **Adjustable:** no: the position is fixed once the screw is driven; to move a part, unscrew it and drill new holes (slotted brackets give adjustment, see Slotted adjustment with a jacking screw).
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind): the screw is driven from the face of the top piece into the hidden bottom piece; coach screws are used 'where it would be difficult to fasten a bolt or where a nut on the surface would be objectionable' (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
- **Materials:** Screws: usually steel, also brass, stainless steel and other alloys, with finishes such as nickel, blued, chromium or cadmium (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8); coach screws are usually zinc-galvanised carbon steel (Wikipedia 'Screw'); Confirmats are steel, galvanised steel, nickel or aluminium-zinc alloys (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). Joins: solid softwood and hardwood, plywood, chipboard (particleboard) and MDF (needing chipboard or Confirmat screws and gentle tightening), and fixes thin metal, plastic and 3D-printed parts onto wood (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8; Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). 'Ordinary wood screws do not hold well in particleboard ... They tend to tear out' (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'). Not for: brick or concrete (use a Wall plug or a concrete screw), hollow plasterboard on its own (use Hollow-wall anchors), thick metal (use machine screws or self-drilling screws). Outdoors and in preservative-treated wood use stainless, hot-dip galvanised or coated screws: treated wood corrodes plain steel fasteners in the presence of moisture, and steel stains some woods (USDA Wood Handbook 'Corrosion and Staining', written for nails); deck screws have 'improved corrosion resistance' (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types').
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Moisture: in damp conditions plain steel corrodes, especially in preservative-treated wood, and steel reacts with some wood extractives to leave dark stains; use stainless, hot-dip galvanised or coated screws outdoors (USDA Wood Handbook 'Corrosion and Staining', written for nails). Mixed metals: a fastener and a metal connector of different metals corrode each other faster, so match them (USDA Wood Handbook; Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog). Wood moving: wetting and drying cycles cut nail withdrawal resistance to as little as 25% of its initial value (USDA Wood Handbook, for nails; screws are probably affected less ⚑ [uncertain]). Chipboard and MDF swell and lose grip when wet ⚑ [uncertain]. Plastic or 3D-printed parts under a screw head [creep](../glossary.md#creep) and loosen in heat ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: wood screws, coach screws and Confirmat screws are bought, not made
  - woodwork: the main use, fixing boards, frames and furniture parts together
  - 3D print (FDM): printed brackets, feet, holders and jigs fixed to wood with wood screws through printed clearance holes
  - laser cut: plywood and MDF parts joined at butt joints with screws or Confirmats ⚑ [uncertain]
  - CNC: routed sheet-material furniture assembled with screws or Confirmats ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the screw is a bought metal part; only the parts it holds, and jigs for drilling, are printed.
- **Tools:** Screwdriver, or drill-driver or impact driver, with the bit that matches the drive (Phillips, Pozidriv, Torx or star, square, hex or slotted) (Wikipedia 'Furniture screw'; 'List of screw drives'); a spanner, socket or special bit for the hex head of a coach screw (Wikipedia 'Screw'); a drill with pilot, clearance and countersink bits, or a combined bit that drills the whole profile in one go (Wikipedia 'Pilot hole', 'Countersink'); a stepped drill bit and hex key for Confirmat screws (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw'); soap or wax on the thread for dense wood and long coach screws (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
- **Difficulty:** easy: buy the screws and drive them with a drill-driver; the skill is choosing the right screw and drilling the right pilot and clearance holes.

### 3D printing tips

1) To fix a print onto wood, give the print a clearance hole the screw passes through freely, so the thread grips only the wood and the head pulls the print down, just as with a top board; this is what the plain shank of a wood screw is for (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'). 2) Countersunk heads wedge outward as they seat ⚑ [uncertain] and can split thin printed walls; on prints prefer pan, round or washer heads, or add a washer, and keep a wall at least one screw diameter thick round any hole (Hubs 'How to assemble 3D printed parts with threaded fasteners'). 3) If you do model a countersink, match the screw: 82° for US inch screws, 90° for ISO metric (Wikipedia 'Countersink'); Engineering ToolBox lists countersink diameters by screw gauge. 4) Don't overtighten into or onto plastic: the plastic under the head can creep and the joint loosens over time ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) Driving a wood screw into a print is really a Self-tapping screw into plastic (see that entry for hole sizes); Hubs suggests a pilot hole giving 75-80% thread engagement if the screw maker gives none. 6) Printable workshop aids help with wood screws: drill guides that keep pilot holes square, countersink depth stops, and screw-size gauges (see make_it_yourself).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Wood screws (countersunk/flat, raised/oval, round and pan heads; Phillips, Pozidriv, Torx/star, square or slotted drives; part-threaded or fully threaded); chipboard (particle board) screws; drywall screws (coarse or fine, twin-start); deck screws; coach (lag) screws with hex heads (DIN 571); Confirmat screws with their stepped drill bits and cosmetic cover caps; pocket-hole screws; tapping and self-drilling (Tek) screws; hanger bolts and dowel screws; combined pilot-and-countersink drill bits (Wikipedia 'Screw', 'List of screw and bolt types', 'Confirmat screw', 'Pilot hole'). For hole sizes by screw gauge, use a table rather than guessing: Engineering ToolBox 'US Wood Screws - Pilot Holes' gives hardwood and softwood pilot holes, shank clearance holes and 82° countersink diameters.

## Rules of thumb

- Pilot (lead) hole for the threaded part of a wood screw: about 70% of the thread's root diameter in softwoods and about 90% in hardwoods; the root diameter of most wood screws is about two-thirds of the shank diameter (USDA Wood Handbook 2021, ch. 8, pp. 8-10 to 8-11).
- For the best sideways strength, drill the shank part of the hole the same diameter as the shank or slightly smaller, and the threaded part the same as the thread root in dense woods or slightly smaller in light woods (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-12).
- Coach (lag) screws: shank hole = shank diameter; thread hole 40-70% of the shank diameter in low-density softwoods (cedars, white pines), 60-75% in Douglas-fir and Southern Pine, 65-85% in dense hardwoods such as oak; use the larger figure for larger screws, slightly larger holes for long screws, and soap on the thread (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-12).
- Coach screw thread penetration of about 7 x the shank diameter in dense woods (specific gravity over 0.61) and 10-12 x in light woods (under 0.42) develops about the screw's full tensile strength (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-12).
- Sideways (lateral) loads: screw into the piece holding the point at least 7 x the shank diameter; make the top piece about half that thickness; keep end distance at least the top piece's thickness and edge distance at least half of it; screwing a metal plate to wood raises the load about 25% (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, pp. 8-11 to 8-12).
- End grain: wood screws and coach screws withdraw at about 75% of their side-grain load, and erratically, if the wood doesn't split (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8).
- Long screws are wasted in dense hardwood: the screw breaks in tension before the wood lets go, at a shorter length the denser the wood (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-10).
- 'Screws should always be turned in. They should never be started or driven with a hammer', which tears the fibres and damages the thread (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-12).
- Chipboard: tighten modestly; withdrawal hardly changes for lead holes of 50-90% of the root diameter, and the stripping [torque](../glossary.md#torque) for an 8-gauge screw 15.9 mm deep is T = 3.16 + 0.0096 x board density (N·m, kg/m³), about 9 N·m for a 650 kg/m³ board (calculated here) (USDA Wood Handbook eq. 8-11a, p. 8-11).
- In MDF furniture joints the screw's depth mattered most, then the hole diameter, and the screw type least (2019 study cited in Wikipedia 'Furniture screw').
- Type A tapping (sheet-metal) screws hold about 10% more in wood than wood screws of the same size (ratio 1.05 in redwood to 1.16 in oak) (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-10).
- Confirmat screws have about twice the shank diameter of ordinary wood screws, go into stepped pre-drilled holes, and must always be refitted with a screw of the same thread or the hole is ruined (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw').

## Common failures

Wood splits: no pilot hole, too small a pilot, or a screw too close to an end or edge, so the screw acts as a wedge; drill a pilot hole and keep end and edge distances (Wikipedia 'Pilot hole'; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Screw snaps while driving: too small a pilot hole lets the core bind (Wikipedia 'Pilot hole'), or a hardened drywall screw in hardwood (woodgears.ca). Hole strips and the screw spins: pilot hole too big, which gives a 'rough, shallow' thread (USDA Wood Handbook Fig. 8-8), or overtightening in chipboard (USDA Wood Handbook eq. 8-11). Joint won't pull tight: the thread is gripping the top piece too; drill a clearance hole or use a part-threaded screw (Wikipedia 'List of screw and bolt types'). Head pulls into or through the wood: soft or thin top piece, countersunk head; use a bigger or flat head or a washer (USDA Wood Handbook 'Fastener Head Embedment'; woodgears.ca). Weak joint in end grain (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Rust streaks and stains outdoors from uncoated steel (USDA Wood Handbook). Drive recess stripped by the wrong bit or too little pressure ⚑ [uncertain]. A refitted Confirmat with the wrong thread ruins the hole; plug it with a dowel and re-screw (Wikipedia 'Confirmat screw').

## Safety

Before screwing into a wall, check for hidden cables and pipes: an electrician quoted by Homebuilding says that in newer homes cables usually run in 'cable zones', e.g. horizontally about 150 mm below the ceiling or vertically to and beside switches, and recommends a detector with voltage sensing (Homebuilding 'How to avoid drilling into wires'). Hardened screws, especially drywall screws, can snap in hardwood and leave a stub buried in the wood (woodgears.ca). Structural and load-bearing joints (decks, joist hangers, things people stand on or under) need the fastener type and number the maker specifies to reach their design load (Simpson Strong-Tie SE blog). Screw points that come through the far side are sharp ⚑ [uncertain]. Wear eye protection when drilling ⚑ [uncertain].

## Related

- Nails, brads and tacks: faster to drive and cheaper, but hold less in withdrawal and can't be undone neatly
- Butt and lap wood joints: the joints wood screws usually hold, including pocket-hole screws
- Ordinary screw joint (bolt and nut, machine screw): a machine thread into a nut or tapped hole, for joints taken apart often
- Self-tapping screw into plastic: the same self-threading idea for plastic and 3D prints
- Knock-down cam-lock and cross-dowel connectors: flat-pack alternatives to the Confirmat that can be swapped and rebuilt
- Screw-in threaded insert and Threaded rod and studs (hanger bolt): give wood a machine thread
- Wall plug and Hollow-wall anchors: what a screw needs to hold in masonry or plasterboard
- Metal brackets and plates: steel angles and plates screwed or nailed on to strengthen wood joints
- Dowel and biscuit joints: wooden pins that align and glue parts with no metal showing
- PVA wood glue: often used together with screws, which clamp the joint while the glue sets
