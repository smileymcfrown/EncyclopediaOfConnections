A bell crank is a lever that rocks on a pivot at its corner, so a rod or cable pulling one arm makes the other arm push or pull in a different direction (usually at 90 degrees), with the arm lengths setting how much the movement and force are scaled up or down.

**In plain words:** An L-shaped (or straight) lever on a pin that takes a push or pull coming from one direction and sends it off in another direction, and can make the movement bigger or smaller on the way. **Proper term:** Bell crank (bellcrank, bell-crank lever).

**Also called:** bellcrank / bell-crank lever (Wikipedia; Sheldon Brown); angle lever; elbow lever ('the bell-crank or elbow-lever', 507 Mechanical Movements no. 156); right-angled or rectangular lever ('a rectangular lever in the form of a crank', Chambers's 1908 dictionary); 90-degree bellcrank and 180-degree bellcrank (the two common forms; Wikipedia); rocker / suspension rocker (race-car pushrod and pullrod suspensions; Formula 1 Dictionary); control horn (on a light-aircraft rudder, where the bellcrank pivots on the rudder hinge; Wikipedia); steering bellcrank (RC cars, e.g. Traxxas and Tamiya TT02 models on Printables); 'clickbox' (the bell-crank gear-change unit on a Shimano Nexus 3-speed hub (brand); Wikimedia Commons).

## How it works

The lever can only turn about its fixed pin. When a rod or cable pulls or pushes one arm, the lever rotates a little and its other arm moves its own rod along a new line set by the angle between the arms: 90 degrees in the classic L-shape, anything from a small angle up to a straight 180-degree bar that simply reverses the direction (Wikipedia; Rob Ives). Both arms turn through the same angle, so the arm ends move in proportion to their distance from the pivot: if the arms are equal the output moves as far as the input, otherwise the movement and force are traded against each other like any lever (load x load arm = effort x effort arm) (DT Online; Government Polytechnic Vaishali lever notes). This ratio holds only near the middle of the swing: as the lever turns further, each rod pulls less squarely on its arm, so the motion becomes non-linear; designers either keep the swing small with the arms square to the rods at neutral, or use the effect on purpose, for example to make ailerons move further up than down (Wikipedia; RC Airplane World).

## Parts

A few parts: the lever itself (an L-, T- or straight plate with a pivot hole at the corner and a hole or several holes near the end of each arm), a pivot pin or shoulder bolt with a bushing or small ball bearing, and the two rods or cables with their end fittings (clevises, ball links, Z-bends or cable eyes). In plain words: 'lever, pivot bolt, bushing, two pushrods or cables, two clevises'. Steel control-line bellcranks come with a brass bushing, lock washer, screw and nut (Brodak); a Tamiya TT02 printed version uses an 11x5x4 mm ball bearing (Printables 1110254).

## Everyday examples

- Bicycle brakes: in a caliper brake the pull from the handlebar lever is turned through 90 degrees to squeeze the brake block against the rim (DT Online)
- The gear-change unit on a Shimano 3-speed hub bicycle, which turns a pull on the gear cable into a push on a rod inside the axle (Sheldon Brown)
- Car throttle and brake-pedal linkages (Wikipedia), and the steering linkage of many RC cars
- Old servants' bell pulls, and the levers at the top of the stairs in old San Francisco houses that open the front door remotely (Wikipedia)

## When to use it

Use a bell crank when a push or pull has to go round a corner or change direction inside a tight space, such as a cable from a pedal turned to move a rudder, a servo in a wing driving an aileron, a lever at the top of a staircase opening a door below, or a wheel's up-and-down movement turned sideways to a spring and damper lying flat in a car. It is also the simplest way to scale a movement up or down (longer arm = more travel, less force), which is why hobby servos usually drive their loads through a bellcrank and pushrod rather than a linear actuator (Wikipedia, 'Servo (radio control)'). It is cheap, light, precise, easy to adjust with extra holes and threaded rods, and works in both directions with rigid rods (or with cables when two cables pull against each other, as in control-line models).

## When not to use it

Avoid it when the path has many bends or has to flex: a push-pull (Bowden) cable follows curves without levers. Avoid it when the output must turn a lot or keep turning: it only rocks through a limited angle, so use bevel gears, a belt or chain, or a rack and pinion for continuous or large rotations. Avoid it when the output must stay exactly proportional over a large swing: the motion becomes non-linear, so use a pulley and cable (pull-pull) or gear sector instead. Don't rely on it to hold a position against a load: it back-drives, so add a lock, a non-back-drivable drive (worm gear or lead screw) or a toggle clamp that goes over centre. For sharing one pull evenly between two points use a whippletree (floating lever) rather than a fixed-pivot crank.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - change the kind of motion: it turns a push or pull round a corner (usually 90 degrees) or reverses it (a straight 180-degree bellcrank), and turns the swing of a servo arm, pedal or hand lever into a push or pull somewhere else
  - change speed or turning force: unequal arm lengths make the output move further with less force, or less far with more force (mechanical advantage)
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): it is the pivot point that lets a linkage of rods or cables swing
- **Movement:** rotate: the lever rocks about its pivot through a limited angle, while the rods or cables on its arms move back and forth (push-pull). The useful swing is kept to a fraction of a turn so the rods stay roughly square to the arms; the rods should stay above a 40-degree angle to the arm they drive (transmission angle rule, MechSimulator)
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 1: the lever can only turn about its pivot pin. Together with its rods and the frame it forms a one-freedom linkage (a four-bar linkage when both rods are rigid), so one input position gives exactly one output position
- **Special behaviour:**
  - motion conversion (e.g. rotary to linear): it changes the direction of a push or pull (507 Mechanical Movements no. 126: 'used for changing the direction of any force') and can scale it up or down; with an offset (off-centre) pick-up point it deliberately gives unequal travel in the two directions, which RC modellers use for aileron differential (RC Airplane World)
  - none of the holding behaviours on its own: it does not lock, click or slip. If it is swung until an arm lines up with its rod it reaches a dead point where it can jam or flip over; this is avoided in control linkages but used on purpose when a bell crank is part of a toggle clamp (DT Online, Thang's 'Toggle clamp 2b' animation)
- **How it holds:**
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): it passes a push or pull on and redirects it
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): pins, bolts, Z-bends and clevis pins through its holes keep the rods attached
- **Input and output:** Push or pull (or the swing of a lever or servo arm) -> push or pull along another line, usually at 90 degrees; any angle from about 0 to 180 degrees is possible, and a straight 180-degree bar reverses the direction (Wikipedia). Ratio: output travel / input travel = output arm length / input arm length, and the force ratio is the inverse (W x l_w = P x l_e, Polytechnic Vaishali notes). Typical sizes: Du-Bro's nylon micro bellcrank has 0.625 in (15.9 mm) from the centre hole to the outer holes (Du-Bro); control-line stunt planes use 3 to 4 in bellcranks with the pushrod hole 0.6 to 1.25 in from the pivot (Model Aviation); race-car rockers are often set so 1 cm of wheel travel gives about 2.5 cm of damper travel (Formula 1 Dictionary). Efficiency: high, since the only losses are pivot friction; no measured figure was found ⚑ [uncertain]. Shaft arrangement: not shafts; the input and output lines lie in one plane at the arm angle, and the pivot axis is square to that plane. Back-drivable: yes; pushing on the output rod moves the input, because nothing in a plain bellcrank locks.
- **Power:** passive (no power needed); it passes on whatever push or pull it is given by a hand, foot, cable, servo or actuator
- **Load:** Light to heavy depending on how it is built: injection-moulded nylon micro bellcranks for indoor RC planes (Du-Bro), steel control-line bellcranks with a brass bushing (Brodak), machined aluminium rockers in racing-car suspensions (Formula 1 Dictionary; the FSAE-based Printables 708756), cast or forged levers in machinery such as railway signalling and governors (Polytechnic Vaishali notes). Strong for forces in its own plane that turn it about the pivot. The pivot pin carries the combined (vector) sum of both rod forces, which for a 90-degree bellcrank is sqrt(W^2 + P^2), more than either rod force alone (Polytechnic Vaishali notes), so the pin and its mounting must be sized for that. Weak against sideways (out-of-plane) forces and twisting, which bend the pin and wear the hole; Model Airplane News warns that when an arm's travel is not in line with its pushrod 'you have extra stress'.
- **Play and precision:** Small but cumulative play: each pivot and each rod end adds a little, and linkages with many levers always have some slop and drag; ball bearings at the pivots reduce it, but even bearings have some radial play and it adds up through the system (RCHelicopterFun on mechanical mixing linkages). Full-size aircraft cable systems are adjusted so there is no backlash, both for precise control and to help prevent control-surface flutter (EAA). Repeatability is good when the pivots are tight. It tolerates very little misalignment out of its plane: the rods should lie in the plane the lever swings in, or the pin binds and wears.
- **Taking it apart:** removable: the pivot bolt or screw undoes and the rods come off their clevises, ball links or cable ends; a Z-bend can only come off once the arm is removed from its pivot (FlyRC)
- **How often it can be reused:** effectively unlimited: nothing is deformed when it is taken apart; the limit is wear of the pivot hole and pins
- **Sealing:** none: it is an open lever on a pin; nothing about it seals
- **Adjustable:** yes: several holes along each arm change the ratio and how quickly the output responds (Model Aviation: pushrod throw of about 0.6 in for slow response to 1.25 in for very fast response); adjustable clevises, threaded rod ends or turnbuckles set each rod's length so the lever sits at neutral (FlyRC; EAA); moving the pick-up hole ahead of or behind the centre line gives more travel one way than the other (RC Airplane World). In a race car the ratio can only be changed by fitting a different rocker (Formula 1 Dictionary).
- **Access needed:** both sides: the pivot is usually a bolt with a nut, so you need to reach both faces of the mount; one side only when the lever turns on a shoulder screw threaded into its mount, or in a moulded bracket such as Du-Bro's in-wing mount
- **Materials:** Injection-moulded nylon (Du-Bro micro bellcrank); steel plate with a brass bushing (control-line bellcranks, Brodak); aluminium, often CNC-machined (race-car rockers; Printables 708756 generative-design bellcrank based on FSAE dimensions); cast iron or steel with a 2-3 mm brass bush in the pivot boss in heavy machinery (Polytechnic Vaishali notes); 3D-printed ABS or PETG for RC-car steering bellcranks (Printables 1110254); thin card for teaching models (Rob Ives); wood, plywood or acrylic for hobby linkages.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Printed PLA levers soften at about 60 C (CNC Kitchen), so keep them out of hot cars and away from motors; PETG copes to about 80 C and ASA to about 110 C. Dirt, rust and dried grease at the pivot cause stiffness and wear; bronze bushings or sealed bearings last longer. Vibration loosens small screws and nuts, which is why RC builders add thread-locker and lock nuts (FlyRC). In aircraft, any play that develops in the control linkage can allow control-surface flutter (EAA). Plastic levers under a constant spring load may creep over time ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - 3D print (FDM): flat printed plates, e.g. RC-car steering bellcranks in ABS or PETG (Printables 1110254)
  - 3D print (resin): small levers for models, though resin is brittle at the pivot boss ⚑ [uncertain]
  - CNC: aluminium or steel rockers and bellcranks (race cars, FSAE)
  - laser cut: plywood, acrylic or thin sheet levers
  - sheet metal: steel bellcranks cut and drilled from plate (control-line bellcranks)
  - woodwork: wooden levers for toys and automata; card for paper mechanisms (Rob Ives)
  - off-the-shelf part: RC aircraft bellcranks (Du-Bro), control-line bellcranks (Brodak), RC-car steering bellcranks
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: a bellcrank is printed as a flat part and fitted onto a metal pivot bolt, bushing or bearing; no print-in-place bellcrank model was found in this search
- **Tools:** Screwdriver or hex key and a spanner for the pivot bolt and nut; pliers or Z-bend pliers to bend and fit wire pushrods (FlyRC); small screwdriver for adjustable clevises or E/Z connectors; in full-size aircraft cable systems, turnbuckles with fork ends that fit over the bellcrank (AN161) and the tools to rig them (EAA). Blue thread-locker helps keep small screws from vibrating loose (FlyRC).
- **Difficulty:** easy: it is a flat plate with three holes that can be printed, laser cut or cut from sheet; the tricky parts are making the pivot run freely without play and getting the geometry right (arms square to the rods at neutral, enough swing without reaching a dead point)

### 3D printing tips

1) Print the lever lying flat with the pivot hole vertical, so the bending in the plane of the lever runs along the printed lines; FFF prints, like wood, are stronger in one direction than another (Prusa). 2) Don't use the printed hole as the bearing for anything that matters: put a metal bolt or shoulder screw through a brass bushing (as in control-line bellcrank kits, Brodak) or press in a small ball bearing (the Printables TT02 bellcrank uses an 11x5x4 mm bearing and is printed in ABS or PETG with all walls and no sparse infill). 3) For moving parts start with at least 0.3 mm clearance and adjust after a test print (Prusa); holes usually print slightly small, so ream or drill them to size. 4) Geometry: set each arm square to its rod at mid-travel; ArduPilot's setup guide sets the servo arm perpendicular to the link at the midpoint of its travel, and Model Airplane News notes that an arm whose travel is not in line with the pushrod adds stress. Keep the angle between rod and arm above about 40 degrees over the whole swing (MechSimulator transmission-angle rule) so it cannot reach a dead point. 5) Put two or three holes along each arm so you can change the ratio later (Model Aviation's throw choices). 6) Make the pivot boss beefy: the pin carries the vector sum of both rod forces (sqrt(W^2 + P^2) for a 90-degree crank), and machine-design notes size a metal lever's boss at about twice the pin diameter (Polytechnic Vaishali notes); treat this as a starting point for plastic ⚑ [uncertain]. 7) Material: PETG, ABS/ASA or nylon rather than PLA for anything warm or under steady load: in CNC Kitchen's oven test PLA gave at 60 C, PETG at 80 C and ASA at 110 C. 8) Keep the rods in the lever's plane, add a washer each side of the lever, and use a locknut tightened only until the lever swings without wobble.

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

RC aircraft bellcranks, e.g. Du-Bro 'Micro 90° Bellcrank System' (brand; nylon, with mounting hardware); control-line bellcranks with brass bushing, e.g. 'Perfect Bellcrank Large' 3 3/8 in sold by Brodak (brand); RC-car steering bellcranks and bellcrank bearing kits; clevises (e.g. Kwik-Link (brand)), ball links, E/Z connectors, pushrods and Z-bend pliers (FlyRC; Du-Bro catalogue categories); shoulder bolts, bronze flanged bushings and small ball bearings for pivots; turnbuckles with AN161 fork ends that fit over a bellcrank, for full-size aircraft control cables (EAA); rod ends and clevis forks for bigger linkages; bicycle hub gear bell-crank units ('clickbox', Shimano (brand)).

## Rules of thumb

- Lever balance: load x load-arm length = effort x effort-arm length (W x l_w = P x l_e), so travel scales with arm length and force with its inverse (Government Polytechnic Vaishali, 'Levers and its design' notes; DT Online: equal arms give equal movement).
- Pivot load for a right-angled bell crank: the fulcrum reaction is R_F = sqrt(W^2 + P^2), bigger than either arm force (Government Polytechnic Vaishali notes).
- Metal bell-crank lever proportions from machine-design notes: fulcrum pin length about 1.25 x pin diameter, pivot boss outside diameter about 2 x pin diameter, and a 2-3 mm brass bush in the boss so wear can be renewed (Government Polytechnic Vaishali notes).
- Set the arm at 90 degrees to its link at mid-travel (ArduPilot traditional-helicopter swashplate setup: servo arm perpendicular to the pitch link at the midpoint of servo travel) and keep the link-to-arm (transmission) angle above about 40 degrees over the whole swing (MechSimulator).
- Control-line stunt models (.35-.60 engines): use at least a 3 in bellcrank, 3.5-4 in strongly recommended; pushrod hole about 0.6 in from the pivot for slow response, about 0.9 in, up to about 1.25 in for very fast response; elevator horn 0.6-1.0 in and flap horn about 1.0 in from its pivot (Ted Fancher, 'Control Line: Aerobatics', Model Aviation, May 1985).
- Aileron differential with one servo: pick-up holes set ahead of or behind the centre line give less travel on the pulling side; typically about half as much down as up, or start a radio differential mix at 30% (RC Airplane World) or 30-40% (Model Airplane News).
- Race-car rocker example: 1 cm of wheel movement giving about 2.5 cm of damper movement, the damper arm being the longer one (Formula 1 Dictionary).

## Common failures

1) Worn pivot hole or rod ends give play (slop) that makes the control vague and, in aircraft, can allow flutter: use bushings or bearings and replace worn parts (EAA; RCHelicopterFun). 2) Binding: rods not in the lever's plane, a pivot bolt over-tightened, or a hole printed undersize; check that it swings freely under finger force. 3) Over-travel past a dead point: the arm lines up with its rod and the linkage jams or flips; limit the swing and keep the transmission angle above about 40 degrees (MechSimulator). 4) Unequal or wrong throws because the arm was not square to the rod at neutral (ArduPilot; RC Airplane World). 5) Cracks at the pivot boss or where an arm joins it in printed or thin parts, since that is where bending is greatest ⚑ [uncertain]. 6) Loose screws, pushrods pulling out of connectors or clevises opening under vibration; use thread-locker, Z-bends or keepers (FlyRC).

## Safety

Pinch points between the arms, rods and frame when the linkage moves, especially if it is driven by a motor, servo or spring. Bellcranks in brake, throttle, steering or flight-control linkages are safety-critical: a cracked arm, a lost pivot nut or a clevis that pops open disconnects the control, so use locknuts or safety pins and sleeve clevises (FlyRC recommends silicone tubing on clevises to stop them opening). Parts for full-size aircraft must be the specified certified hardware (AN/MS parts; EAA), not home-printed replacements.

## Related

- Servo horn and spline hub: the arm on a servo output that usually drives the pushrod to a bellcrank; a bellcrank is a separate free-pivoting lever placed elsewhere in the linkage
- Four-bar linkage: a bellcrank with two rigid rods is one link of a four-bar linkage, and the same transmission-angle and dead-point rules apply
- Whippletree (load-equalising lever): a floating lever that shares one pull between two points instead of redirecting it round a fixed pivot
- Bowden (push-pull) cable: carries a push or pull round curves without levers, at the cost of friction and some lost motion
- Toggle clamp and over-centre linkage: a bellcrank and link driven past the dead point on purpose so they lock
- Slider-crank and Scotch yoke: turn continuous rotation into straight-line motion, whereas a bellcrank only rocks through a limited angle
- Bevel gears or a rack and pinion: change direction for continuous or large rotations, with more parts and some backlash
- Rod end and ball link: the joints usually used at each arm of a bellcrank to allow small misalignments
