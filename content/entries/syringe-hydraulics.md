A sealed tube of liquid with a piston at each end, so that pushing one piston pushes the other one wherever the tube goes; because the pressure is the same everywhere in the liquid, a small piston driving a large one multiplies the force and divides the distance by the ratio of their areas, just as a lever does.

**In plain words:** Two syringes (or cylinders) joined by a tube full of water: push one plunger in and the other plunger slides out. If the second syringe is fatter than the first, it pushes much harder but moves a shorter distance, so a small push can lift something heavy, like a lever made of water. **Proper term:** Hydraulic lever (syringe hydraulics; Pascal's principle; master and slave cylinders).

**Also called:** syringe hydraulics, syringe hydraulic arm, hydraulic arm (school and museum activity, Exploratorium); hydraulic lever: the hydraulic press 'uses the hydraulic equivalent of a mechanical lever' (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic press'); Pascal's law, Pascal's principle, principle of transmission of fluid-pressure (Wikipedia); master cylinder and slave cylinder (car brakes and hydraulic clutches); wheel cylinder; caliper piston (Wikipedia 'Master cylinder', 'Hydraulic brake'); hydraulic jack, bottle jack (whiskey jack), floor jack, trolley jack, air hydraulic jack (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)'); hydraulic press, Bramah press (Wikipedia); hydraulic lift (car lift, dentist chair, wheelchair lift) (Science Buddies); hydraulic disc brake and hydraulic rim brake on bicycles (Wikipedia 'Bicycle brake'); hydrostatic transmission, passive fluid transmission, rolling-diaphragm transmission (robotics; Disney Research); miniature hydraulics, mini hydraulics, micro hydraulics (models; Wikipedia); closed hydraulic system (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake').

## How it works

Liquid hardly squashes at all, so when you push one plunger the liquid has to go somewhere, and the only part of its container that can give way is the other plunger, which moves out (Exploratorium; Science Buddies). Pascal's law says the extra pressure is passed on equally to every part of the trapped liquid (Wikipedia), and force equals pressure times area, so a piston with 50 times the area pushes 50 times harder but moves only one-fiftieth as far: the work in equals the work out, exactly as with a lever (Wikipedia 'Pascal's law'). Pulling the first plunger back sucks the second one back too, so a plain pair works both ways (Exploratorium). A jack or press adds two one-way valves (check valves) so that many short strokes of a small pump piston add up to one long, strong lift, and the load stays up until a release valve lets the oil flow back to the reservoir (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)'; Potter USA).

## Parts

A few parts acting as one unit. Syringe version: two syringes (each a barrel and a plunger with a rubber tip), a length of flexible tube and water (Exploratorium; Science Buddies). Brake version: lever or pedal, pushrod, master cylinder (piston, return spring, seals and a fluid reservoir), reinforced hoses, and one or more caliper or wheel cylinders (slave cylinders) (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake'). Jack or press version: oil reservoir, small pump piston and pump cylinder, two check valves, big main cylinder and ram (main piston), and a release valve (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)'; Potter USA bottle jack guide). Robot version: pairs of rolling-diaphragm cylinders joined by tubing, with timing belts or gears turning the push into rotation (Disney Research).

## Everyday examples

- The brake pedal of a car and the brake lever of a mountain bike with hydraulic disc brakes: a small master cylinder pushes fluid to the brake pistons (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake', 'Bicycle brake')
- A car jack or bottle jack, and the hydraulic lifts at a garage or in a dentist's chair (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)'; Science Buddies)
- The syringe-powered 'hydraulic arm' or digger built in school science and technology lessons (Exploratorium; Printables models)
- The arms of garbage trucks and the blades of bulldozers (Exploratorium)

## When to use it

Use it when you want to move something from a distance or round corners with a simple push, especially where a cable would rub: the tube can go anywhere and has far less friction than a Bowden cable round tight bends (Disney Research 2014). Use a small-to-large pair to multiply your force, for lifting, pressing or clamping something heavy with a small effort (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic press'), or a large-to-small pair to make a small movement bigger. It is ideal for teaching and models (syringe arms, diggers, lifts, cranes; Exploratorium; Printables), and for low-friction, back-drivable robot joints where the motor is kept in the body and the arm stays light (Disney Research). Buy a jack or press when you need tons of force for pressing bearings, forming metal or lifting (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic press'; Potter USA). A small-to-large pair also scales a movement down for fine positioning: micromanipulators reduce a hand movement 'hydraulically using pistons of different diameters connected by tubing containing non-compressible fluid' (Wikipedia 'Micromanipulator'), so a maker can use the same idea to nudge a probe onto a circuit board under a microscope from a distance (the micromanipulator itself is a whole instrument and outside this entry).

## When not to use it

Not for long strokes from a small input: volume in equals volume out, so you'd need a huge input syringe or a pump with check valves (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)'). Not for holding a position for hours: sliding seals leak and the two ends drift out of step (Disney Research 2014), so use a lead screw or a self-locking worm drive instead. Not for strong pulling: suction is limited to about one atmosphere (Disney Research 2014). For a simple pull over a short, gentle route a Bowden cable is simpler and dry; for a compact, rigid force multiplier use a lever, bell crank or gears; for powered, repeated motion use an air or hydraulic cylinder with a valve, or an electric linear actuator. Don't use a home-made system where failure could hurt someone, such as brakes or anything holding a person or a car up: buy rated parts, and never rely on a jack alone (HSE).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - change speed or turning force: a small piston driving a big one multiplies force and divides distance by the area ratio, the fluid version of a lever (Wikipedia 'Pascal's law', 'Hydraulic press')
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): carries a push or pull along a bendy tube to a part somewhere else, like a Bowden cable but with much less friction round bends (Disney Research)
  - lock, and release by hand: in a hydraulic jack or press, check valves trap the oil so the load stays up until you open the release valve (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)'; Potter USA)
- **Movement:** slide: each plunger or piston slides in a straight line in its cylinder; what it pushes can swing, e.g. the Exploratorium arm pivots on a bolt as the syringe extends (Exploratorium). Stroke is limited by the syringe length; a jack gets a long lift from many short pump strokes (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)')
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 1 per liquid line: each sealed tube links one input movement to one output movement (several outputs on one line, such as a car's brake calipers, all move together). A robot arm needs one line (or one pair of lines) per joint (Disney Research: N water lines plus 1 shared air line for N joints)
- **Special behaviour:**
  - motion conversion: changes force and distance by the ratio of piston areas and sends the push round corners and along any route the tube takes; for example a master piston half the diameter of the slave piston moves 4 times as far with a quarter of the force (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake')
  - hand-release only (jacks and presses): check valves stop the oil flowing back, so the ram holds its load until a person opens the release valve (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)'; Potter USA)
  - none of the locking behaviours for a plain syringe pair: it is fully back-drivable, so pushing on the output plunger pushes the input back (Exploratorium; Disney Research: 'complete backdrivability')
- **How it holds:**
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): it passes a push or pull from one place to another. In a jack the trapped, almost incompressible oil behind the check valves is what holds the load up (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)')
- **Input and output:** Straight push or pull -> straight push or pull somewhere else (and, through a lever arm, a swing; Exploratorium). Force ratio = output piston area / input piston area = (output bore / input bore) squared; the distance ratio is the inverse, e.g. 1 N on the small piston of a pair with a 50:1 area ratio gives 50 N, and a 100 cm stroke gives 2 cm (Wikipedia 'Pascal's law'); identical syringes give the same force and the same distance (Exploratorium). Efficiency: no measured figure found for syringe pairs; rubber plunger seals add friction ⚑ [uncertain]. Rolling-diaphragm cylinders, which have no sliding seal, had static friction of 1% of full-range torque or less (Disney Research 2014). Arrangement: input and output can be in line, at any angle or far apart, because the tube can be routed anywhere; fluid transmissions 'offer greater routing flexibility than cable drives and Bowden cable transmissions' (Disney Research 2014). Back-drivable: yes for a plain closed pair; no for a jack or press until its release valve is opened (Potter USA).
- **Power:** passive (no power needed): your hand or foot supplies the push, as in syringe arms, brake levers and hand-pumped jacks (Exploratorium; Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake'). Powered versions exist: an air hydraulic jack is pumped by compressed air, and car brakes add a vacuum booster to multiply the driver's force (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)', 'Hydraulic brake'). Robot transmissions are driven by an electric motor at the input end (Disney Research)
- **Load:** light for syringe models: a 10 mL syringe arm lifts small toys or a set of keys (Exploratorium) and 25 mL and 60 mL syringes lift jars of water (Science Buddies). Heavy for bought jacks and presses: bottle jacks go up to 50 tons (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)'). Strong when pushing along the cylinder's axis; weak against sideways loads on the plungers and against pulling the tube off its tips. Pulling only works by suction, which is limited to about one atmosphere before the liquid cavitates (Disney Research 2014), so a syringe pulls far more weakly than it pushes
- **Play and precision:** Small play when the system is full of liquid with no bubbles; any air bubbles compress and make it feel soft and spongy, with lost motion (Park Tool). Soft or balloon-like tubing also adds springiness, so Disney Research used rigid nylon tubing for water to keep it stiff (Disney Research 2014). Sliding-seal systems slowly leak, so the input and output drift out of step over time (Disney Research 2014). It tolerates misalignment very well because the tube bends anywhere. For fine positioning the movement-reducing mechanism usually has to be free of backlash (Wikipedia 'Micromanipulator'), so bubbles and soft tubing matter even more there.
- **Taking it apart:** removable: syringe tubes push onto the tips and pull off (Exploratorium); car and bike brake systems are sealed, and opening them lets air in, so they must be bled afterwards (Park Tool; Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake')
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: syringes and tubes can be emptied, refilled and reconnected over and over, though push-on tube ends loosen and may need tape or putty (Science Buddies)
- **Sealing:** watertight at low pressure for syringes (the rubber plunger tip and the push fit of the tube on the tip); pressure-tight for brakes, jacks and presses, which are closed systems sealed with O-rings and piston seals (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake'). Rolling-diaphragm cylinders seal with a rolling rubber sleeve instead of a sliding seal, so they don't leak or stick (Disney Research 2014)
- **Adjustable:** yes: swap one syringe for a different size to change the force and distance ratio (Exploratorium swaps a 10 mL for a 1 mL syringe); add or remove liquid to change where the output sits when the input is at rest; robot transmissions are pre-pressurised and their preload can be adjusted (Disney Research)
- **Access needed:** not applicable: the two cylinders are mounted wherever needed and joined by a tube; you only need to reach the tube ends (or the fill and bleed ports) to fill and connect it
- **Materials:** Cylinders: plastic syringes (Exploratorium; Science Buddies); metal master and caliper cylinders with O-ring-sealed pistons (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake'); fabric-reinforced nitrile rubber rolling diaphragms (Disney Research 2014). Tube: clear plastic tubing (Exploratorium), silicone fuel tubing (Science Buddies), nylon tubing (Disney Research), reinforced brake hoses (Wikipedia). Liquid: water for models (Exploratorium; Printables 1037944 recommends water); oil in jacks because it is 'self-lubricating and stable' (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)'); brake fluids are glycol-ether (DOT 3, 4, 5.1), silicone (DOT 5) or mineral oil (Shimano and Magura bike brakes) (Wikipedia 'Brake fluid'). Frames: pine and screws (Exploratorium), PLA prints (Printables models), steel wall plates glued to the plungers (Science Buddies)
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Water freezes and expands, which can split syringes or tubes left in the cold ⚑ [uncertain: not checked against a source]. Glycol-ether brake fluids (DOT 3, 4, 5.1) absorb water from the air, which lowers their boiling point; mineral-oil and silicone fluids don't (Wikipedia 'Brake fluid'). Hot brakes on long descents can boil the fluid, which turns to compressible vapour and the brake fails (Wikipedia 'Bicycle brake', 'Brake fluid'). DOT fluid is corrosive to frame paint (Wikipedia 'Bicycle brake'). Silicone fluid is more compressible than glycol fluid, so brakes feel spongier (Wikipedia 'Brake fluid'). Mixing fluids or contaminating them with water, alcohol or antifreeze can crack seals and cause leaks (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake'). Rolling diaphragms are limited to low pressures, typically 10 bar (Disney Research 2014).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: plastic syringes (catheter-tip, Luer-slip or Luer-lock), flexible tubing, bottle jacks and shop presses, brake master and slave cylinders, rolling-diaphragm cylinders (Science Buddies; Wikipedia; Disney Research)
  - 3D print (FDM): frames, syringe holders, clamps, arms and linkages around bought syringes (Printables models 1037944, 632407, 632421, 74121, 550862, 772606)
  - woodwork: the Exploratorium hydraulic arm is pine boards, a post and an arm held with screws, washers and wing nuts (Exploratorium)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the syringes, tube and liquid are bought, and the printed frame, holders and arms are separate parts assembled around them with glue, rods or screws (Printables 1037944; Printables 772606 needs 12 M3 screws)
- **Tools:** none for a basic syringe pair: fill the syringe and tube under water (Science Buddies); scissors for tubing and a drill, screwdriver and pliers for the Exploratorium wooden arm (Exploratorium); tape, plumber's putty or hot glue to keep tubes on (Science Buddies; Printables 632407). Bike brakes need a bleed kit of syringes and hoses plus safety glasses and gloves (Park Tool). Bottle jacks need a screwdriver for the oil-fill plug and jack oil (Potter USA)
- **Difficulty:** easy: syringes, tube and water, with a printed or wooden frame; the main skill is getting all the air bubbles out (Exploratorium; Science Buddies). Hard to build a high-pressure system such as a jack, press or brake yourself, so buy those

### 3D printing tips

1) Don't print the cylinder: use bought syringes as the cylinders and print only the frame, clamps and arms (all the Printables syringe models do this). Printed hollow parts leak at their corners under pressure, and a printed PLA air tank burst at 5.5 bar (CVRA), so never hold stored pressure in a printed part. 2) Let both ends pivot: the syringe swings as the arm moves, so mount the barrel in a loop that can turn on a bolt and pin the plunger end with a pin or nail through a hole drilled in the plunger (Exploratorium uses a cable-tie loop on a machine screw, and a nail through a 1/8 in hole in the plunger). 3) Stop the plunger pulling out: Exploratorium drives a small screw into each barrel just below the flange as a stop. 4) Fill without bubbles: fill one syringe and the tube completely, flick the tube to bring bubbles up and top it off, push the other syringe's plunger fully in so it holds no air, then join them (Exploratorium); or hold the syringe tip up, push the air out, and draw water through the tube several times (Science Buddies). 5) Keep the tube on: tubes pop off when pushed hard, so seal joints with plumber's putty, tape or hot glue (Science Buddies; Printables 632407), or use Luer-lock syringes whose threaded collar stops the cone being pulled apart (see Taper fit). 6) Pick the ratio for the job: equal syringes give equal force and movement; a small syringe driving a large one gives more force and less movement (Exploratorium; Science Buddies: 25 mL and 60 mL syringes work well together). 7) Avoid dead points in the linkage: the printed shovel's four-bar linkage has a 'singularity' when the syringe is fully retracted (Printables 1037944), so lay out the arm so the syringe never lines up with the link it drives. 8) Make legs and thin arms strong: a one-piece printed press stand broke its legs on the first drop because the layers ran across the legs; the redesign prints separate parts joined with M3 screws (Printables 772606). Turn parts so layers run along thin legs and arms ⚑ [uncertain: general FDM practice, not tested here]. 9) Rods and pins: the printed shovel uses 5.6 mm by 46 mm printed rods, or wooden sticks of the same size (Printables 1037944). 10) If the arm tips over when lifting, weight the base (Exploratorium).

Syringes from 1 mL to 60 mL with catheter tips, Luer-slip or Luer-lock tips (Exploratorium; Science Buddies; Printables 632407 uses 20 mL and 10 mL); clear vinyl, aquarium air-line or silicone fuel tubing, e.g. 5/32 in (about 4 mm) inside diameter (Science Buddies); plumber's putty. ## Rules of thumb

- Force multiplies by the area ratio and distance divides by it: 1 N on a small piston lifts 50 N on a piston of 50 times the area, but a 100 cm push moves the big piston only 2 cm; force times distance stays the same (Wikipedia 'Pascal's law'; Exploratorium)
- Area goes with diameter squared: a master piston of half the slave's diameter has a quarter of the area, so pushing it 40 mm with 10 N moves the slave 10 mm with 40 N (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic brake')
- Two identical syringes pass on the same force and the same distance; swapping the hand syringe from 10 mL to 1 mL makes the push much easier but the arm moves much less (Exploratorium)
- Volume in = volume out: the big piston can only move as far as the small syringe's volume allows, so for a long, strong stroke add two check valves and pump repeatedly, as a jack does (Wikipedia 'Jack (device)')
- Get every bubble out: 'Air bubbles will compress and cause the brake to feel soft' (Park Tool); in brakes, boiled fluid turns to vapour, which 'is highly compressible relative to liquid' and stops the brake working (Wikipedia 'Brake fluid')
- Pushing is strong but pulling is weak: liquid can only pull by suction, limited to about one atmosphere of reverse pressure before it cavitates (Disney Research 2014). For strong two-way motion, push one way and return with a spring or weight, use one line per direction, or pre-pressurise both sides as robot transmissions do (to half the maximum pressure; Disney Research 2014)
- Keep the line stiff: use rigid or reinforced tubing and as little trapped air as possible; Disney Research used rigid nylon tubing (4.3 mm inside diameter) for water, and found that air-filled stiffness falls as the volume of air in the tubing rises (Disney Research 2014)
- A separate pump means only small-diameter tubing is needed, because narrow tubing resists pressure more easily (Wikipedia 'Hydraulic press')
- Rolling-diaphragm cylinders are low-pressure parts, typically limited to 10 bar (off-the-shelf up to 17 bar), with a stroke of only about one bore diameter, and they need positive pressure at all times or the diaphragm folds and jams (Disney Research 2014)
- Bottle jacks: open the release valve only about a quarter turn; store and carry them upright, because laying them down lets air in and 'air-locks' them; purge air by opening the release valve and pumping about 20 times (Potter USA)
- Bike brakes: never put DOT fluid in a mineral-oil brake or the other way round, and never share bleed kits between them (Park Tool)

## Common failures

1) Spongy, weak or jerky movement from air bubbles: refill under water and push the bubbles out (Exploratorium; Science Buddies; Park Tool). 2) Tube pops off the syringe tip when pushed hard: refill and refit it, then seal with plumber's putty or tape (Science Buddies), or use Luer-lock syringes. 3) Plunger pulled right out of the barrel: add a stop screw (Exploratorium). 4) Slow leaks past plunger seals, so the two ends drift out of step (Disney Research 2014). 5) Arm tips over under load: add weight to the base (Exploratorium). 6) Linkage jams at a dead point when the syringe is fully in (Printables 1037944). 7) Jack won't lift or won't build pressure: low oil, or air trapped after it was laid on its side; jack creeps or leaks oil when the release valve is opened too far (Potter USA). 8) Brakes: soft lever from air or the wrong fluid swelling the seals, pistons that won't retract and a scraping disc (Wikipedia 'Bicycle brake'); fade from boiling fluid on long descents (Wikipedia).

## Safety

1) Syringe models are low risk (a school activity), but use syringes without needles and keep water away from electronics (own advice). 2) Jacks: vehicles falling or rolling off badly placed jacks or stands are 'one of the main causes of fatal accidents in motor vehicle repair'; never work under anything held up by a jack alone, always add axle stands and chock the wheels (HSE 'Working under vehicles'). 3) Brakes are safety-critical: 'Hydraulic brakes rarely fail, but failure tends to be complete' (Wikipedia 'Bicycle brake'); the wrong fluid makes seals fail and can lead to brake failure (Park Tool; Wikipedia); wear safety glasses and gloves when bleeding brakes (Park Tool). 4) High pressure: a small piston driven hard makes very high pressure, and a jet of oil from a pin-hole leak can inject fluid under the skin; serious cases have happened above 100 bar and possibly as low as 7 bar, and need immediate hospital treatment (HSE hydraulic injection bulletin). 5) Pinch points: keep fingers out of the arm and linkage as it moves. 6) Never hold stored pressure in a 3D-printed part: a printed PLA air tank exploded at 5.5 bar (CVRA).

## Related

- Bowden cable: carries a push or pull through a sleeve, but can't change the force, and its friction grows rapidly with every bend (Disney Research 2014)
- Air or hydraulic cylinder: the powered version, where a pump or compressor and a valve drive the piston instead of a second syringe
- Syringe pump: a motor and lead screw push a single syringe slowly and precisely to dispense liquid, rather than to move a part
- Check valve: the one-way valves that turn a hydraulic pair into a jack that pumps up and holds
- Bell crank, whippletree and other levers: change force and direction with rigid bars instead of liquid
- Pulley system (block and tackle): trades distance for force with rope instead of liquid
- Lead screw and nut: a screw jack lifts slowly and holds by itself without leaking
- Bellows and inflatable actuators: push with a flexible chamber and no sliding seal, like the rolling diaphragm
- Capstan cable drive (tendon drive): the low-friction cable alternative for robot arms (Disney Research 2014)
- Taper fit (Luer taper), Hose barb and Quick couplers: the fittings that join syringes and tubes
