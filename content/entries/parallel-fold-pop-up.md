Pieces of card that cross the centre fold with all their creases running parallel to it, so that opening the card pulls them up into a step, box, tent or floating stage, and closing it folds them flat again, like a paper parallelogram [linkage](../glossary.md#linkage).

**In plain words:** A step, box or little stage cut or glued across the middle fold of a card, that stands up by itself when you open the card and lies flat again when you shut it. **Proper term:** Parallel-fold pop-up (parallelogram pop-up).

**Also called:** parallel fold (Instructables 'Pop-Up Card'; Georgia Tech Paper Museum); box fold, double box and tent (Warwick WMG paper-engineering lessons); layer pop-up / single sheet layer (Robert Sabuda); step fold or step pop-up (common card-making name; no opened source uses it) ⚑ [uncertain]; parallelogram, parallelogram pop-up (Wikipedia 'Pop-up book'; Duncan Birmingham, via Cut Out + Keep); symmetrical two-cut pop-up and asymmetrical pop-up (Carol Barton, 1998); floating layer, floating plane (Mark Hiner; Carol Barton; Chibitronics, after Duncan Birmingham); platform: movable parallel, fixed parallel, concave, convex, tilted, multi-layer (Superplexus, Michael McGinnis); scenery flat and tent fold (Superplexus); standing structure (Instructables 'Pop-Up Card'); slot and joint (an arm slotted through a parallelogram; Chibitronics); origamic architecture, paper architecture, 90-degree kirigami card (single-sheet cut-and-fold cards; Masahiro Chatani); multiple layers (Mark Hiner); lattice pop-up (criss-cross slotted strips) ⚑ [uncertain: needs confirming].

## How it works

All the creases of the piece run parallel to the card's centre fold, so the base card, the piece and the creases form parallelograms that flex at the folds as the card opens; Wikipedia's 'Pop-up book' calls the parallelogram 'the basic principle of a pop-up book'. Opening the card pulls the two ends of the piece apart and the middle stands up; closing it folds everything flat, and the springiness of the creases helps: 'The "energy" which opens the shapes comes from the folds' (Instructables 'Pop-Up Card'). The piece only closes flat if it balances: on each side of the centre fold, the length of the piece plus its distance from the fold must be the same, 'even if the element is asymmetric' (Georgia Tech Paper Museum; Instructables writes it a1+b1=a2+b2; Carol Barton: the tab joining the image to the back page 'must equal the distance from the page centerfold to the base of the image'). Each new fold can carry more pop-ups, and 'each successive pop-up will move in the opposite direction as the fold it crosses' (Barton). Which opening angle shows it best depends on the design: a step or box made with two cuts is meant for a card opened to about 90 degrees and has no depth when fully open or fully shut (Barton; Superplexus 'Scenery Flats'; Georgia Tech), while tents, fixed platforms and floating planes are made to stand up with the card opened flat to 180 degrees (Warwick; Superplexus 'Tent Folds'; Barton). Variants covered here: the single-sheet layer (two equal parallel cuts from the fold, the strip pushed through; Sabuda), several layers cut one inside another (Barton: 'Multi-layered symmetrical pop-ups'; Hiner's 'multiple layers'), glued-on boxes, tents and arches (Warwick 'Box Fold' and 'Tent'; Georgia Tech: 'boxes, tents, cylinders, cones, pyramids, boats, and arcs'), floating layers (a flat plane on three parallel supports, folded parallel to the crease or diagonally; Chibitronics; Barton; Superplexus 'platforms'), slot-and-joint arms that jut out through a slot in a parallelogram (Chibitronics), and 90-degree origamic architecture, usually cut from one sheet of thin card and opened to a right angle, while other styles open to 180 or 360 degrees (Wikipedia 'Origamic architecture'; Li et al. 2010 call these 'parallel' paper architectures, whose pieces stay parallel to the two pages). Lattice pop-ups (criss-cross slotted strips) are only mentioned, as 'lattice-type folds' among V-fold variants, in Li et al.'s 2011 paper; no how-to source was found ⚑ [uncertain: needs confirming].

## Parts

Single piece, or a few parts glued together. Single-sheet version: one folded card with two cuts and a pushed-through strip (Sabuda). Glued-on version: a base card (the 'spread') plus a folded strip or box with glue tabs; a floating layer adds a flat top plane on three folded 'feet' or support arms (Barton; Chibitronics; Superplexus). Parts in plain words: base card, centre fold (gutter or spine), pop-up piece, creases (mountain and valley folds), glue tabs, support arms.

## Everyday examples

- pop-up greeting cards with a step or box that stands up inside
- children's pop-up books with layered scenery, such as Robert Sabuda's books
- white cut-paper 'origamic architecture' building cards (Masahiro Chatani's style)
- tunnel books (peepshows) sold as souvenirs, whose folded side strips hold picture layers apart

## When to use it

Use it when you want something to stand up off a page by itself when the card or book is opened: steps and stages that hold figures, boxes, buildings, tents, arches and layered scenery. Use the single-sheet two-cut version for quick cards with no glue (Sabuda; Barton), glued-on boxes and tents when the piece needs its own colour or shape, and a floating layer (a flat plane on three parallel feet) for a large picture that seems to hover above the page (Barton; Chibitronics). Combine it with V-folds for richer scenes: the V-fold gives height and the parallel fold a 'stage' (Judit Orosz). Parallel folds also work as platforms for paper circuits and LEDs (Chibitronics). Mentioned here as relatives: tunnel (peepshow) books and carousel (star) books use accordion-folded side strips to hold cut-out layers apart (Wikipedia; Utah tunnel book lesson; Utah OAB 'carousel book'; Superplexus 'Tunnel Books').

## When not to use it

Not for anything that must carry weight, get wet, or be handled roughly by small children for long: card tears at tabs and corners. Not for shapes that must lean towards or away from the centre fold at an angle or swing up out of the page: use a V-fold pop-up (angle fold), whose creases meet at one point on the spine. Not for moving a picture across the page or flipping a flap on demand: use a pull-tab and slider mechanism. For a rigid version made from wood, plastic or 3D prints, use a parallelogram four-bar linkage with real pins. For uncut fold patterns that collapse (Miura, Kresling), see Origami mechanisms.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - change the kind of motion: the turning of the card as it opens becomes a piece rising up off the page and folding back down
  - let it move (turn, slide or bend): every crease acts as a paper hinge so the whole shape can fold flat and open again
- **Movement:** multiple: every crease turns like a hinge (from flat to fully folded, up to 180 degrees), so the pieces swing up and out while staying parallel to the card's pages; the whole shape moves as one, tied to how far the card is opened
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 1: like a parallelogram linkage, the card's opening angle sets the position of every piece. A 'movable' floating platform can also be pushed a little to the left or right when open (Superplexus 'Basic Platforms'); a 'fixed' platform braced with a tent fold has none (held rigid by triangulation).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - motion conversion: opening the card (turning about the spine) lifts and moves the pop-up pieces, which collapse again when it closes
  - none of the other listed behaviours apply; note that fold springiness pushes a symmetrical floating platform off-centre unless the support arms are built as a 'Z' on one side and a 'reverse Z' on the other (Superplexus 'Basic Platforms')
- **How it holds:**
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): it is a folding mechanism
  - [material bond](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond) (glue, solvent or melting): glued-on pieces are held to the base card by glue tabs; single-sheet versions need no glue
- **Input and output:** Input: the card's two pages turning about the centre fold (0 to 90 or 180 degrees, depending on the design). Output: the pop-up pieces rising off the page and translating while staying parallel to the pages (step, box, tent or floating plane). Ratio: set by geometry; an 'acute' scenery flat moves less than the page, an 'obtuse' one moves faster than the page (Superplexus 'Scenery Flats'). Efficiency: not meaningful (paper folds, light springiness). Arrangement: all creases parallel to the spine. Back-drivable: yes in practice, pressing the pop-up flat helps close the card, as it is one connected loop ⚑ [uncertain: no source tested this].
- **Power:** passive (no power needed): powered by the reader opening the card
- **Load:** light: it carries only its own card and light decorations. Strongest against pushing straight down when fully open as designed; weak against sideways pushes and tearing at glue tabs and corners. A floating platform with a tent-fold centre is 'held rigid by triangulation' (Superplexus); Instructables warns that, depending on the paper and the weight on top, a standing structure may need extra bracing (a double 45-degree fold or an extra parallel fold).
- **Play and precision:** Small play: paper creases are springy, so pieces do not stop exactly at the same place every time, and a symmetrical movable platform can drift off-centre (Superplexus). It tolerates little error in the balance rule: a mismatch of length or tab position stops the card closing flat or makes pieces stick out (Georgia Tech; Warwick 'Assessment'). Misplaced tabs that are not lined up with the centre fold can tear or crease in the wrong place (Superplexus 'V-Folds', glue-tab advice that applies to all glued pop-ups).
- **Taking it apart:** permanent: single-sheet pop-ups are cut from the card itself, and glued-on pieces tear if pulled off
- **How often it can be reused:** once: it is not taken apart; it is made to open and close many times, and Superplexus's course criteria require showing it 'will withstand opening and closing multiple times'
- **Sealing:** none: nothing makes it seal
- **Adjustable:** no once glued; before gluing, sliding a tab closer to or further from the fold changes how it leans, and Superplexus suggests trial and error while 'gluing the Support Arm in place when the spread is closed shut'
- **Access needed:** both sides: the card must be opened flat to glue tabs or push a cut strip through to the inside; glued pieces are best set by closing the card onto the second glue tab so it finds its own position (Barton; Superplexus)
- **Materials:** Made for paper and card. Good: smooth card stock or Bristol board, about 80 lb / 216 gsm / 10 pt (0.01 in) according to Superplexus; Make: and Sabuda use 110 lb card stock; Chibitronics says 110 lb is ideal but 65 lb works; Instructables used 160 g card; Barton cuts the pop-up from text-weight paper and the base from card stock. Keep the base heavier than the mechanism paper 'otherwise things might tear' (Superplexus 'Paper'). Poor: very textured cotton-rag or cold-press watercolour paper (frays, too much friction), and construction paper according to Superplexus ('too flimsy ... not at all archival'), although Sabuda's beginner pages allow construction paper or a manila folder. Thin plastic film can stand in for paper in some parts. Not suited to rigid sheets (wood, metal, acrylic, ordinary 3D prints): the motion depends on paper creases, so rigid panels would need real hinges, which is a four-bar linkage instead.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Paper and card: damp makes creases soft and paper cockles; too much wet glue warps or wrinkles paper (Superplexus 'Physical Tools'), and Sabuda warns that paint buckles paper. Acidic papers such as newsprint and construction paper yellow, turn [brittle](../glossary.md#brittle--ductile) and fade in light; use acid-free, lignin-free card if it must last (Superplexus 'Paper'). Creases lose spring after many openings, so pop-ups stand less crisply over time ⚑ [uncertain: no measured source]. No heat, chemical or vibration limits beyond those of paper and glue were found.

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - hand cutting and folding (scissors or craft knife, ruler, bone folder)
  - laser cut or die-cut paper (Wikipedia 'Origamic architecture' notes CAD and laser cutting; popuplady glossary: die-cut, 'more recently ... cut into shapes with a laser'; Barton describes die-cutting with a letterpress)
  - printing a template on card stock before cutting (Sabuda, Instructables, Chibitronics templates)
  - not 3D print (FDM), 3D print (resin), CNC, woodwork or sheet metal in its normal form
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: it is made of folded paper; a 3D printer is only useful for tools such as scoring boards and bone folders
- **Tools:** scissors or a craft knife, a cutting mat, a ruler, a scoring tool (bone folder, empty ballpoint or blunt point), and glue (PVA, glue stick or double-sided tape) for glued-on pieces (Sabuda; Chibitronics; Superplexus 'Physical Tools')
- **Difficulty:** easy: a single-sheet layer pop-up needs one folded sheet, scissors and two cuts (Sabuda); glued-on boxes, tents and floating planes are moderate because tab placement must be accurate

### 3D printing tips

not applicable: parallel-fold pop-ups are cut and folded from paper or card. ## Rules of thumb

- Balance rule: on each side of the centre fold, (length of the piece) + (its distance from the fold) must be equal, even for lopsided pieces (Georgia Tech Paper Museum, 'Physics of Paper Engineering'); written a1+b1=a2+b2 (Instructables 'Pop-Up Card'). Carol Barton's version for a step: the strip joining the image to the back page equals the distance from the centre fold to the base of the image (Barton 1998).
- The piece must be longer than the gap between its glue points ('The pop-up element must be larger than the distance between the base anchors', Georgia Tech). Warwick's tent: an 18 x 10 cm card rectangle with 1 cm tabs at each end (8 cm sides), glued along lines 6 cm either side of the centre crease; it stands up with the card open at 180 degrees (Warwick lesson 2).
- A piece built on the outside of another fold must be bigger than the gap it bridges: a1&lt;b1 and a2&lt;b2 (Instructables 'Pop-Up Card', 'Outside mechanism').
- Every pop-up must cross a fold, and each new layer pops the opposite way to the fold it crosses: over a valley fold it pushes outward, over a mountain fold inward (Barton 1998).
- Two-cut steps and boxes have the most depth at about 90 degrees and none when fully open or fully shut; tents, fixed platforms and floating planes are designed for the card opened flat at 180 degrees (Superplexus 'Scenery Flats' and 'Tent Folds'; Georgia Tech; Barton 1998).
- Floating plane: three 'feet' about 1 in wide and 2 in long, grain long, with 1/2 in glue tabs at both ends; extra feet are fine but must be parallel to the centre fold (Barton 1998). In a movable platform all support arms are the same length and parallel (Superplexus 'Basic Platforms').
- Glue tabs about 1/4 in (6 mm) deep are a good starting point (Superplexus 'V-Folds'); Barton uses 1/2 in tabs, Warwick 1 cm.
- Card: about 80 lb / 216 gsm / 10 pt Bristol or card stock (Superplexus 'Paper'); 110 lb card stock (Make:, Sabuda); 110 lb ideal, 65 lb workable (Chibitronics). Make the base heavier than the pop-up pieces (Superplexus).
- Crease each fold both ways, up to three times, to break the fibres so it folds smoothly (Warwick lesson 2).
- Glue one tab, close the card onto the glued second tab so it finds its own place, and let the glue set before opening hard: Superplexus gives about 30 minutes for white glue to set and about 24 hours for PVA to [cure](../glossary.md#cure).

## Common failures

The card will not close flat, or pieces stick out of the closed card: the balance rule is broken, or the piece is too long for the card; check lengths and tab positions on both sides and that everything fits inside the closed card (Georgia Tech; Warwick 'Assessment'; Superplexus 'Technical and Aesthetic Criteria'). The pop-up does not rise by itself: creases not broken in, or the strip not pushed through; fold each crease both ways and press the card shut firmly once (Warwick; Sabuda; Barton: 'pull it forward with a finger as you close the card'). A floating platform drifts to one side: the folds' springiness all pulls one way; build the arms as a Z and a reverse Z, or brace the middle with a tent fold (Superplexus). It sags under decorations: add bracing such as a double 45-degree fold or an extra parallel fold (Instructables). Tabs tear or the glue lets go: tabs too shallow or glue not set; use about 6 mm tabs, glue fully to the crease, and let it set (Superplexus). Closed ends on boxes stop the card shutting completely; their folded width must fit inside the closed card (Superplexus 'Complex Platforms').

## Safety

Craft knives: cut to the side of yourself, use a cutting mat, use a safety ruler with a raised edge for straight cuts, keep the blade sharp so you do not press hard, and wear eye protection when changing blades (University of Greenwich workshop guidance for scalpels and craft knives). Let glue set before opening the card hard, or tabs pull loose (Duncan Birmingham, 'Automatic Pull Strip'; Superplexus 'Gluing Techniques'). Light-up versions use coin cells: Chibitronics now offers a 'safer' Launchpad battery in its templates ⚑ [uncertain: the risk is not described on the pages opened; keep coin cells away from young children].

## Related

- V-fold pop-up (angle fold, mouth fold): its creases meet at one point on the spine instead of running parallel to it, so the piece rises at an angle; works with the card opened flat
- Pull-tab and slider mechanism: moves a picture when a strip is pulled (by hand or by the page opening) instead of standing a shape up; the automatic pull-strip can raise a parallelogram on a flap
- Four-bar linkage (including parallelogram linkage): the rigid version with pins; a parallel-fold pop-up is a paper parallelogram whose hinges are creases
- Origami mechanisms (waterbomb, Kresling, thick-panel origami): fold patterns in uncut sheets; pop-ups are cut and glued from stiff card and powered by opening a page
- Score-and-fold hinge (scored crease): the single crease each pop-up is built from
- [Living hinge](../glossary.md#living-hinge): a thin bendy hinge in plastic, the plastic cousin of a paper crease
- Spring-powered paper pop-up (rubber-band snap-up cube): stands up from stored energy instead of the page opening
- Tab-and-slot joint and cross-halving slot joint: slots used in slot-and-joint arms and multi-layer flats
- Bellows boot: an accordion fold used as a cover; tunnel books use accordion strips as folding spacers instead
