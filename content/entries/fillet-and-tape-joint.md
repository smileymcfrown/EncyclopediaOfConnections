A seam made by pulling two panels together with temporary stitches, filling the inside corner with a smooth cove of thickened epoxy (the [fillet](../glossary.md#chamfer--fillet)) and laying resin-soaked glass tape across it, so the panels are welded into one stiff shell without frames or fasteners.

**In plain words:** Two panels held edge to edge (often with twists of wire or cable ties), then a rounded bead of thick glue-paste smoothed into the inside corner and a strip of glass-fibre cloth soaked in resin laid over it, so the seam becomes one solid piece with no frames, nails or screws, as in home-built plywood boats and kayaks. **Proper term:** Fillet-and-tape joint (stitch-and-glue seam; glass-tape tabbing).

**Also called:** stitch and glue, stitch-and-glue construction (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'); tack and tape, stitch and tape (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'; John Welsford 'How Stitch & Tape Seams'); taped seam, fillet and tape, epoxy fillet joint (CLC Boats 'Fillets'; JW Boat Designs); tabbing, glass tabbing, tabbing in a bulkhead or stringer (fibreglass boat building; Epoxyworks 'Common errors in fiberglass laminate repair'; boatdesign.net thread); secondary bonding ⚑ [uncertain: common composites term for bonding to an already-cured laminate; not defined on an opened page]; taped butt joint, 'hybrid' plywood joint, 'save a butt' (Epoxyworks 'Joining Plywood'; boatdesign.net forum term seen in search results); tack fillets (small dabs that tack a part in place before the full fillet; Duckworks 'Quick and Dirty Stitch and Glue'); Instant Boats method (Phil Bolger and Harold 'Dynamite' Payson designs; Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue').

## How it works

The stitches are only clamps: they hold the panels in the right shape until the epoxy sets, then they are snipped off, pulled out or left buried (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'). The fillet fills the sharp inside corner with a curved ramp of glue, which gives a large glue area and lets load spread gradually into each panel instead of at a hard edge, so there is no [stress concentration](../glossary.md#stress-concentration) (a 'hard spot') where one panel meets the other (boatdesign.net thread 'Proper way to tab bulkheads'). The glass tape laid over the wet fillet and onto both panels then carries the load across the seam like a bandage, making a small composite joint; taping the outside as well makes a double-sided joint (Duckworks 'Quick and Dirty Stitch and Glue'; Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'). Tabbing a bulkhead or stringer into a fibreglass hull is the same joint: a fillet, then layers of glass that step out in width onto both parts (Epoxyworks 'Common errors in fiberglass laminate repair').

## Parts

A few parts, made in place: the two panels (plywood, fibreglass laminate or a bulkhead), temporary stitches (copper or steel wire, cable ties, or tape and staples), thickened epoxy for the fillet (epoxy plus a filler such as wood flour or colloidal silica), glass-fibre tape or cloth, and unthickened epoxy to wet it out. Tools: a rounded fillet spreader, brushes, mixing pots and gloves (CLC Boats 'Fillets'; Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue').

## Everyday examples

- home-built plywood kayaks, canoes and dinghies (e.g. the Mirror dinghy, whose kit used copper wire stitches; Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue')
- bulkheads, seat fronts and stringers glassed into the hull of a fibreglass yacht or motorboat (boatdesign.net thread)
- plywood 'Instant Boats' and one-sheet boats built at home (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue')

## When to use it

When you want to join thin panels at an angle or edge to edge into a light, stiff, watertight shell with no frames or fasteners: plywood boats, kayaks, boxes, fairings, and adding or repairing bulkheads and stringers in a fibreglass hull. Good when the panels themselves can define the shape, when the joint must be sealed, and when you can reach both sides of the seam. Also a way to join plywood sheets end to end (taped butt) when you can't cut a scarf.

## When not to use it

Not for anything that must come apart (use screws, bolts or the Glued joint design entry's mechanical options). Not where you can't reach the inside corner. Not for thick solid timber joints where ordinary woodworking joints and glue are simpler (Butt and lap wood joints; Glued joint design). Not on polyethylene or polypropylene. Avoid polyester resin over polystyrene foam. For joining tubes, use a Bonded tube joint; for adding a threaded mounting point to a panel, use a Bonding fastener or Potted insert instead of tabbing on a block.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: joins panels at an angle (chines, keels, bulkheads, stringers) or edge to edge (taped butt joints) into one rigid shell
  - glue or bond: the fillet and glass tape are glued (epoxy) joints; the stitches only hold the shape while they [cure](../glossary.md#cure)
  - seal it, or pass cables or fluid through: a filled and taped seam is part of a waterproof hull when the whole surface is sealed (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'; boatdesign.net notes the fillet also seals the joint against moisture)
- **Movement:** none
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: once cured the seam is rigid and the panels cannot move relative to each other.
- **Special behaviour:**
  - self-aligning or self-centring: the panel shapes themselves set the hull form; stitching the edges together pulls the flat panels into the right curve without moulds or frames (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue')
  - none otherwise: it is a permanent glued and laminated seam
- **How it holds:**
  - [material bond](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond) (glue, solvent or melting): epoxy glues the fillet and the resin-soaked glass tape to both panels
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** Medium to heavy for its weight. Strong in tension and bending across the seam when glass is on both faces; small boats' cloth-taped seams are strong enough that the plywood next to the seam usually fails first (boatdesign.net thread: cloth 'is more then strong enough to reach the [peel](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear) strength of the surrounding wood'). Wikipedia claims 'something close to 8-10 times the strength of fastenings and timber framing' ⚑ [uncertain: unsourced on Wikipedia, ⚑ needs confirming]. Weakest in peel: a single thin layer of tape, or tape with dry (unwetted) patches, can lift from the edge; bigger, more heavily loaded boats need heavier double-bias (±45°) tape, where every fibre crosses the seam (boatdesign.net thread; Fiberglass Supply 'Stitch and Glue Boatbuilding Materials Guide').
- **Play and precision:** No play once cured. [Accuracy](../glossary.md#repeatability--accuracy) depends on the cut panel shapes and on the stitching; the method tolerates small gaps along the seam, which the fillet fills (JW Boat Designs: the seam can be open on the outside as long as it is a close fit on the inside edge).
- **Taking it apart:** permanent: the seam can only be undone by cutting or grinding it out; stuck wire stitches can be freed by heating them with a heavy soldering iron or blowtorch so the epoxy lets go (JW Boat Designs 'How Stitch & Tape Seams')
- **How often it can be reused:** once
- **Sealing:** watertight: the epoxy fillet fills the gap and the glass tape over it is saturated with resin, so a well-made seam keeps water out; taping the outside too, and sealing plywood [end grain](../glossary.md#end-grain) with epoxy, keeps water from wicking into the wood (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'; boatdesign.net thread). Masking tape along the outside of an open seam stops resin draining out and leaving a 'starved seam' while taping the inside (Guillemot Kayaks).
- **Adjustable:** Only before the epoxy sets: copper wire stitches can be twisted tighter or looser to adjust the fit (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'); once tack fillets or the full fillet cure, the shape is fixed.
- **Access needed:** both sides: you need to reach the inside corner to lay the fillet and tape, and the outside to snip or pull stitches, fill and (usually) tape it too (Duckworks; Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'). Tabbing a bulkhead needs access to both faces of the bulkhead where it meets the hull.
- **Materials:** Suits: marine and other plywood panels (the classic use), solid wood, fibreglass (GRP) laminates and hulls, plywood bulkheads into GRP hulls, and composite sandwich panels (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'; boatdesign.net thread). Epoxy is strongly recommended for wood ('nothing sticks as well to wood as epoxy', Fiberglass Supply); polyester resin is the traditional production-boat choice for GRP but bonds poorly to already-cured laminate (Practical Sailor 'Bulletproof Sailboat Deck Hardware': the secondary bond is about 4-6 times weaker for polyester and 2-4 times weaker for vinylester). Fabrics: plain glass tape or cloth for small boats; knitted double-bias tape for larger ones; mat-backed tapes waste resin in epoxy (Fiberglass Supply; boatdesign.net thread). Foam and 3D prints: glass and epoxy can wrap a foam or printed shape (see making tips), but polyester resin dissolves polystyrene foam ⚑ [uncertain: forum consensus in search results; no authoritative page opened] and a PLA print's plastic can peel away from the epoxy-glass skin (3DPrint.com 'strengthening 3D parts'). Poor: polyethylene and polypropylene (epoxy does not stick) ⚑ [uncertain: not stated on a page opened for this entry; see the Two-part epoxy entry].
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Water: cured epoxy and glass are waterproof, but exposed plywood end grain wicks water and must be sealed with epoxy (boatdesign.net thread). Sunlight: epoxy is damaged by UV and needs paint or varnish over it; some resin systems add UV inhibitors (Fiberglass Supply mentions WEST SYSTEM 105/207 'with UV inhibitors') ⚑ [uncertain: no figure]. Heat: epoxy softens as it warms; check the resin's data sheet for its heat limit ⚑ [uncertain: no figure on an opened page]. Cold: cure slows; full cure can take over two weeks at room temperature (WEST SYSTEM 'General Epoxy Safety Guidelines'). Hard landings or flexing: a stiff fillet edge on a solid laminate can crack the hull along a 'hard spot' (boatdesign.net thread).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - woodwork (plywood panels cut with a saw or CNC, then stitched)
  - off-the-shelf part (glass tape, epoxy, fillers, kits)
  - 3D print (FDM) for fillet tools and for printed cores wrapped in glass or carbon
  - laser cut or CNC for panel kits ⚑ [uncertain: kits are machine-cut but no opened page says how]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: it is a wet lay-up joint made by hand; only the tools (fillet spreaders) or a printed core are printed
- **Tools:** Drill for stitch holes; pliers and wire or cable ties; mixing pots, stir sticks and gloves; a fillet spreader (a plastic body-filler squeegee with a 1 in radius cut in one end, or a 1/2 in radius stick for small fillets; CLC Boats 'Fillets'), brushes or a 'brush on a stick' for long kayak seams (Guillemot Kayaks 'Precision Seam Taping'); scissors for glass tape; sanding block, coarse abrasive and a dust mask for finishing (JW Boat Designs; WEST SYSTEM 'General Epoxy Safety Guidelines').
- **Difficulty:** moderate: no special tools and very forgiving ('messy but very easy, sort of like making mud pies', CLC Boats), but it needs careful epoxy handling, working fast before the mix goes off, and a lot of sanding to finish neatly.

### 3D printing tips

Print your own fillet tools: a flat card of PLA or PETG with radiused ends (parametric models exist, e.g. 1-15 mm or 15-25 mm radii; Printables 565974 and 138672) is easy to replace when epoxy builds up on it. For wrapping a printed part in a composite shell, one maker printed a hollow shell with 1 mm walls, filled it with fibreglass and epoxy, and found the printed plastic later peeled off, leaving an epoxy-glass part, so allow for the shell not staying bonded (3DPrint.com 'strengthening 3D parts'). Another maker wets both sides of the carbon cloth with epoxy, lays it on the PLA part, does one side at a time and leaves each side 24 hours before trimming (hackaday.io project log 'How we made our materials more resistant on a low budget'). Lightly sanding the print first and testing a small sample is wise, since bond strength to PLA, PETG and ABS varies ⚑ [uncertain: forum advice only, no test data opened]. Treat a printed core as a former (a shape to build on), not a load-bearing part of the joint.

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

- fibreglass tape (woven glass tape, e.g. 3 in wide, 9 oz with woven 'selvedge' edges, as supplied in a CLC kayak kit; Guillemot Kayaks)
- double-bias (±45°) knitted glass tape, e.g. 12 oz without mat ('DB120') or 17 oz with mat ('DB1708') (Fiberglass Supply)
- 6 oz glass cloth (styles 7533 plain weave or 3733 flat weave) for sheathing over the taped seams (Fiberglass Supply)

- fillers: wood flour, colloidal silica (Cab-O-Sil (brand)), filleting blend (WEST SYSTEM 405 (brand)), low-density fairing filler (WEST SYSTEM 410 Microlight (brand)) (CLC Boats; Fiberglass Supply; JW Boat Designs)
- copper wire, soft steel wire or plastic cable ties for stitching (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue'; JW Boat Designs)
- plastic body-filler spreaders (squeegees) to cut into fillet tools (CLC Boats)
- precut triangular or trapezoid foam fillets for under bulkhead edges (boatdesign.net thread)

## Rules of thumb

- Fillet size: cut a 1 in (25 mm) radius into a spreader for the main seams and a 1/2 in (13 mm) radius for bulkheads (CLC Boats 'Fillets'); about 30 mm (1 1/4 in) radius for 1/4-3/8 in plywood, a little bigger for 1/2 in and up (JW Boat Designs 'How Stitch & Tape Seams').
- Stitch spacing: tie at close intervals, about 15 times the plywood thickness, so the edges stay put while you work (JW Boat Designs).
- Fillet mix: thicken epoxy with wood flour to 'peanut butter consistency' so it doesn't sag; a little colloidal silica makes it smoother (CLC Boats).
- Tape onto the wet fillet ('wet on wet'): brush unthickened epoxy out to the tape line, lay the tape, wet it out; do a couple of metres at a time on hot days so the fillet doesn't cure first (CLC Boats; JW Boat Designs).
- Multiple tapes: stagger the tape edges so they don't build up in one place (JW Boat Designs); when tabbing stringers and frames, [taper](../glossary.md#taper) the layers to give a gradual thickness change, and fillet inside corners (Epoxyworks 'Common errors in fiberglass laminate repair').
- Hiding tape edges: after a day or two, knock off the ragged edges, then spread a wide, shallow fillet of low-density filler: about 60 mm wide for one layer of 6 oz tape, about 100 mm for two layers or one layer of 10 oz (JW Boat Designs).
- Fabric choice: plain cloth is enough on small taped-seam boats; heavily loaded bulkheads need double-bias (biax) tape, because in woven cloth only half the fibres cross the joint and the woven fibres must straighten before they take load (boatdesign.net thread).
- Hard spots: on a solid (uncored) laminate, leave a small gap or put a tapered foam filler under a bulkhead edge so it doesn't press a hard line into the hull; if in doubt, make fillets bigger (boatdesign.net thread; forum opinion, not a standard).
- Taped plywood butt joint ('hybrid' joint): edge-glue the panels, make a shallow bevel along the joint on both faces, then add one or two layers of glass (biaxial is a good choice) on each side; for comparison a wooden butt block should be at least 16 times the plywood thickness wide, and a scarf about 8:1 (Epoxyworks 'Joining Plywood').

## Common failures

- Starved seam (too little resin, dry white patches in the tape): resin drains through an open seam or is soaked up by the wood; tape the outside of the seam and saturate the tape well (Guillemot Kayaks).
- Sagging or runny fillet: the mix is too thin; thicken to peanut-butter consistency (CLC Boats).
- Lumpy fillet and endless sanding: scrape up the squeeze-out with the flat of the tool as you go, and don't go back to touch up a fillet that has started to stiffen (CLC Boats).
- Tape lifting or not bonding: laid on after the fillet cured, or on a contaminated surface; lay tape wet on wet (CLC Boats; JW Boat Designs).
- Visible tape edges and dips in the plywood from sanding them: feather with a wide low-density filler fillet rather than sanding into the plywood (JW Boat Designs).
- Hard spot or crack where a bulkhead meets a solid hull: add a foam fillet, a gap, or a bigger fillet, and taper the tabbing layers (boatdesign.net thread; Epoxyworks).
- Tabbing too light for the load (e.g. a single layer of roving in production boats): scale the glass to the load and use directional (biax) fabrics for structural bulkheads (boatdesign.net thread).
- Wire stitches stuck in cured epoxy: heat with a soldering iron or blowtorch to release, or use plastic ties that can be planed off (JW Boat Designs).

## Safety

Epoxy skin allergy (sensitisation): fewer than 10% of users react, less than 2% become allergic, but once sensitised even tiny amounts can trigger worse reactions, so always wear gloves, eye protection and long sleeves, and never clean epoxy off skin with solvent (WEST SYSTEM 'Health effects from overexposure to epoxy'; 'General Epoxy Safety Guidelines'). Sanding dust: never breathe dust from partly cured epoxy, which is still reactive; use ventilation and at least an N95 dust mask (WEST SYSTEM). Glass-fibre dust from sanding and cutting causes itchy rashes and eye and throat irritation: wear long sleeves, gloves, goggles and a mask, wash with soap and water afterwards, and wash work clothes separately (Illinois Department of Public Health 'Fiberglass' fact sheet). Heat from large batches: a pot of mixed epoxy can get hot enough to ignite nearby materials and give off fumes that ordinary respirators don't stop, so mix small batches and move a hot pot outside, away from people and anything that burns (Epoxyworks 'Health and safety'). Hardener on sawdust can self-heat: don't put hardener in a bin with sawdust (WEST SYSTEM). Never use a home-built boat without testing it near shore with buoyancy aids ⚑ [uncertain: general advice, no source opened].

- Bonded tube joint: the same idea of glued or fibre-wrapped overlaps, but for tubes (carbon, bamboo) instead of panels
- Potted insert: how to make a hard point in a cored panel that you will later tab or bolt to
- Bonding fastener: a glued-on threaded stud, sometimes laminated in with a square of glass over it
- Sewn seams and hand stitches: in historic 'sewn boats' the stitches were the permanent joint; in stitch and glue they are only temporary clamps (Wikipedia 'Stitch and glue')
- Adhesive tape joint and tape hinge: pressure-sensitive tape across a seam, a much weaker, quick version for cardboard and foam board
