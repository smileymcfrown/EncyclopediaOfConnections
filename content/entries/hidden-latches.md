A latch hidden inside a door, drawer or box that stays locked until a secret action moves it: a magnet held against the outside pulls the catch back, or tilting, spinning or tapping the box makes hidden weights (balls, pins or bolts) slide out of the way.

**In plain words:** A catch you can't see from outside, that only lets go when you hold a magnet in the right spot, or tip, spin or tap the box in the right way so loose pins or balls inside move out of the way. **Proper term:** Hidden latch (concealed magnetic lock, magnetic-key latch; in puzzle boxes: gravity lock, centrifugal lock, inertia or knock lock).

**Also called:** concealed magnetic lock; magnetic secret latch; hidden magnetic cabinet lock / magnetic child-safety lock; magnetic cabinet lock with magnetic key; Tot-Lok (brand), MagLock (brand) magnetic child locks; secret compartment lock; puzzle box lock / trick lock / trick-opening box; secret box / himitsu-bako (Japanese puzzle box, opened by sliding panels); gravity lock / gravity pin / gravity weight; centrifugal lock / spin-to-open box; inertia lock / knock lock / tap lock; sequential discovery puzzle (puzzle-box term for boxes where you use a found part, e.g. a magnet, on the box).

## How it works

The locking part (a bolt, hook or pin) sits inside where nobody can see or reach it, and blocks the door or lid. In a magnetic-key latch a spring, a springy arm or a reset magnet keeps the bolt in the locked position; a strong magnet held on the outside at the right spot pulls a magnet or steel piece on the bolt through the wood or plastic, moving the bolt clear so the door opens. In puzzle boxes the blocking parts are loose weights: tilting the box lets a ball or pin roll or drop out of the way (gravity lock), spinning it fast throws pins outwards into pockets (centrifugal lock), and a sharp tap knocks a pin off a small magnet that was holding it (inertia or knock lock). Closing the door or lid usually lets the latch drop back or spring back, so it locks itself again.

## Parts

A few parts. Magnetic-key version: a latch or bolt that slides or pivots, a return spring (or a springy printed arm, gravity, or a small reset magnet), a magnet or steel piece on the latch, a catch or strike on the frame, and a separate key magnet. Gravity, centrifugal or knock version: a lid or panel with holes, loose weights (steel balls, pins, bolts or dowels) in hidden channels, and sometimes a small magnet that holds a weight until it is knocked free.

## Everyday examples

- magnetic child-safety locks inside kitchen and bathroom cupboards, opened with a magnetic key held on the outside of the door
- secret drawers or compartments in furniture and floating shelves that open with a magnet hidden in a knob or ornament
- puzzle boxes and trick boxes that open when spun, tilted or tapped (Japanese secret boxes, himitsu-bako, use a sequence of sliding panels instead)

## When to use it

When you want a door, drawer or lid to look plain, with no keyhole or handle, and to stay shut against children, pets or casual snooping: baby-proofing cupboards, hiding a compartment in furniture or a shelf, gift and puzzle boxes, escape-room props, or a 'safe' for small valuables. Good when you can reach the inside to fit it and the outside panel is thin enough for a magnet to work through.

## When not to use it

Not for real security against a determined adult: a strong magnet, prying or shaking may open it, so use a proper keyed lock (pin-tumbler or combination lock) or a lockable safe. Not for medicines or poisons as the only protection (use child-resistant containers too). Avoid gravity or spin locks on things that get carried or bumped a lot, as they may open or jam by accident. Avoid magnetic keys near people with pacemakers or near magnetic media ⚑ [uncertain: general magnet caution, not from an opened source]. If you need it to open from a phone or keypad, use a solenoid or servo latch instead.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - lock, and release by hand: holds a door, drawer or lid shut until someone who knows the secret action (or has the magnet key) releases it
- **Movement:** multiple: the hidden bolt slides or pivots a few millimetres; in puzzle boxes the loose pins, balls or bolts slide or roll inside channels while the whole box is tilted, spun or knocked; the door or lid then opens on its own hinge or slide
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 between door and frame while locked. Inside, the bolt or each loose weight has 1 (it slides along its channel or swings on its pivot). Once released, the door, drawer or lid has its normal 1 (hinge, slide or lift-off).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - hand-release only (locks under load, releases when a person operates it): the lock holds against pulling and only lets go when the person applies the secret action (magnet key in the right place, or the right tilt, spin or knock); most magnetic cabinet locks and spring latches also lock themselves again when the door is shut
- **How it holds:** shape (a hidden bolt, pin or ball blocks the door or lid; form-fit), kept in the locked position by spring force (a spring, a springy printed arm or a reset magnet), by gravity, or by magnetic force (a small magnet holding a pin); opened by magnetic force or by inertia
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed): permanent magnets, springs, gravity and inertia do all the work. Electronic hidden latches (solenoid or servo latches worked by RFID, a keypad or an app) are covered in the powered latch entries.
- **Load:** light: enough to stop a toddler or a curious visitor, not a burglar. Strong only in the direction the bolt blocks; a door can often be flexed or pried, adhesive-mounted locks can pull off, and a strong enough magnet or a lucky shake can defeat it. One child-lock guide quotes a required breaking force of at least 45.3 lb (about 200 N) ⚑ [uncertain: secondary source, standard not named].
- **Play and precision:** not applicable (it does not locate parts). Some play is normal and helpful: DIY magnetic latches use slightly oversize guide holes so the bolt slides freely, but the latch and catch must line up closely or it may fail to lock.
- **Taking it apart:** tool-free quick release: opens by hand as soon as you use the magnet key or the secret move; the latch hardware itself is screwed, glued or printed in and can be removed from the inside
- **How often it can be reused:** many times: it relocks every time the door or lid is closed ⚑ [uncertain: no cycle-life figures found; a printed PLA spring arm may slowly lose its springiness]
- **Sealing:** none
- **Adjustable:** Mostly no: position is set when fitting. Some commercial units have a self-adjusting magnetic pin that suits a range of door thicknesses (Tot-Lok, 1/2 to 1-9/16 in). You can add or swap magnets to make it stronger or more sensitive.
- **Access needed:** both sides: it is fitted from the inside (hidden), but opened from the outside only, with no hole or handle showing
- **Materials:** Neodymium magnets (discs, blocks, rods); steel pins, dowels, bolts or ball bearings as moving weights or magnet targets; plastic latch housings (commercial child locks are hard white plastic); wood, MDF, plywood or 3D-printed PLA or PETG for the box or door. The magnetic field has to pass through the panel: commercial locks work through wood, particleboard, plastic and glass (Tot-Lok also lists metal), and one guide says glass doors are not suitable for stick-on locks because the adhesive fails. Use non-magnetic (e.g. brass or stainless) screws near the latch so they don't attract the key or latch magnet.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Heat: neodymium magnets weaken when warm and can lose strength permanently above their maximum operating temperature (K&J Magnetics); PLA parts soften above about 60 °C and PETG is fine below about 80 °C (Prusa). Plastic spring arms relax over time if left bent (DuPont). Adhesive-mounted child locks can let go over time under repeated pulling, and stick-on locks don't hold well on glass (Baby Proof Me). Humidity makes wooden drawers and doors swell, which can bind a hidden latch ⚑ [uncertain: general woodworking knowledge]. Vibration and knocks can accidentally shift gravity or knock-lock weights.

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: magnetic child-safety cabinet locks and hidden magnetic furniture locks with a magnetic key
  - 3D print (FDM): boxes, shelves and latches with pockets for magnets, steel balls, pins or bolts inserted during a print pause
  - woodwork: secret drawers and compartments with a dowel or bolt latch moved by a magnet; puzzle boxes with nail or pin locks
  - laser cut: puzzle boxes and latch parts ⚑ [uncertain: no specific laser-cut example opened]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** partly: the box and latch can be printed in one go, but magnets, steel balls, pins or bolts have to be dropped in during a print pause (or glued in afterwards), and the key magnet is separate
- **Tools:** To fit: drill and screwdriver (commercial magnetic cabinet locks screw or stick on the inside of the door and cabinet); for printed versions, a print pause to drop magnets, steel balls or bolts into their pockets, and glue. To open: the magnet key (keep a spare) or nothing but your hands for gravity, spin and knock locks.
- **Difficulty:** easy to moderate: commercial magnetic cabinet locks are cheap and screw or stick on; printed boxes with embedded magnets or spin-to-open pins are easy prints but need a print pause, correct magnet polarity and careful alignment; designing a reliable gravity or centrifugal lock that can't be opened by shaking takes trial and error

### 3D printing tips

Magnets: pause the print at the layer that closes the pocket and drop the magnets in. Make the pockets a snug fit or add a drop of superglue, because a loose magnet will jump up and stick to the print head as it passes (Prusa). Check the polarity of every magnet before assembly, so the key attracts rather than repels (the Printables 'Box with a Secret Magnetic Lock' makes this point). Keep the wall between the key and the latch magnet as thin as practical, because magnetic pull drops quickly with distance (K&J Magnetics). Remember that the sideways (sliding) force between two magnets is only about half their listed pull force (K&J Magnetics). Latch return: a springy printed arm works for occasional use (one design relies on PLA's own springiness to spring the latch back), but plastic held bent all the time creeps and relaxes, so use a metal spring, gravity or a small reset magnet when the latch stays loaded (DuPont design guide, p. 36). Bolt guides: make the holes slightly oversize so the bolt slides freely (K&J). Examples of moving weights in printed boxes: four M6 bolts screwed into printed pins as centrifugal weights, inserted at a print pause (Printables 751961); a 5 x 20 mm steel dowel that a 5 x 3 mm magnet in the lid holds until the box is knocked (Printables 687754); three 6.35 x 25.4 mm rod magnets in 7.5 mm holes that are lifted by three key magnets (Printables 467795). Use PETG rather than PLA for anything that will sit in a warm place, because PLA softens above about 60 °C (Prusa). Test that shaking or tipping the box in ordinary handling does not open it, and that it locks again reliably when closed (one maker notes a rare-earth magnet can also pull the balls of a spin-to-open box outwards, bypassing the spin, Make:). How deep the channels and how heavy the weights need to be for a given spin speed was not found in any guide ⚑ [uncertain].

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Magnetic child-safety cabinet locks and drawer locks (latch, catch and magnetic key; sold as 'magnetic cabinet lock', 'adhesive magnetic lock', 'hidden magnetic lock'); spare magnetic keys; hidden magnetic furniture latches operated by a magnetic knob; neodymium disc, block, ring (countersunk) and rod magnets; steel balls (ball bearings), steel dowel pins; non-magnetic brass or stainless screws; magnetic keyed padlocks and cylinder locks (magnet-coded tumblers) for visible locks.

## Rules of thumb

- The force two magnets give when one slides past the other (sliding or shear force) is about half their listed pull force, so size the latch magnet with that in mind; keep the gap between key and latch magnet as small as possible and use non-magnetic screws nearby (K&J Magnetics, https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/diy-magnetic-lock).
- In K&J's tests a sliding dowel latch worked through about 3/4 in of wood with about 0.15 lb of sliding force, and a rotating latch worked through 1-1/8 in of wood with about 0.3-0.4 lb of pull (https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/diy-magnetic-lock).
- Commercial hidden magnetic cabinet locks suit doors from about 1/2 in to 1-9/16 in thick (Tot-Lok, https://www.rockler.com/tot-lok-lock-mechanism); most systems are designed for doors up to about 1.5 in thick (https://babyproofme.com/es/blogs/the-parenting-journal/do-magnetic-cabinet-locks-work-on-all-cabinet-types).
- A bigger key magnet works from further away: a TotLok opened at about 1/2 in with its own key, but a large cylinder magnet opened it from about 2-1/8 in, so a strong magnet can defeat a magnetic lock (K&J Magnetics, https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/magnetic-locks).
- Neodymium magnets lose some strength when warm (typically 5-10%, recovered on cooling) up to their maximum operating temperature; above it they lose strength for good. The safe temperature depends on the magnet's grade and shape (K&J Magnetics, https://www.kjmagnetics.com/blog/temperature-and-neodymium-magnets).
- Plastic springs held under constant load or bending relax and creep; use spring steel for springs that stay loaded (DuPont General Design Principles, Module I, p. 36, https://www.distrupol.com/General_Design_Principles_for_Engineering_Polymers.pdf).

## Common failures

(1) Won't lock or won't open because the latch and catch are misaligned: fit carefully, use slotted holes to fine-tune. (2) Key too weak or panel too thick: use a thinner panel, a stronger key magnet, or move the latch magnet closer. (3) Magnet fitted the wrong way round, so the key pushes instead of pulls: check polarity before gluing or printing in. (4) Printed spring arm has crept and no longer springs back: use a metal spring, gravity or a reset magnet. (5) Bolt sticks because the guide holes are too tight, swollen or dirty: oversize the holes. (6) Adhesive pad lets go: screw it in instead. (7) Gravity or spin lock opens by accident when shaken, or jams: add an anti-pick feature or test many times. (8) Magnet falls out of its pocket or jumps to the nozzle during printing: make a snug pocket and use glue.

## Safety

Small strong magnets are a serious swallowing hazard: two or more swallowed magnets (or a magnet and a steel part) can pinch the intestines together, causing holes, blockage and even death (US CPSC), so fix magnets in securely and keep the key away from children. Losing the magnetic key can lock you out, so keep a spare somewhere safe; some commercial locks have a way to disable them. Child locks are not childproof: in 2012 one brand of magnetic locks was recalled after children as young as 9 months opened them (Baby Proof Me). Keep poisons and medicines out of reach as well.

## Related

- Magnet joint (magnetic catch): holds a door shut by magnetic pull alone, with nothing hidden to unlock; pull harder and it opens
- Push-push latch (touch latch): also hidden and handle-free, but anyone can open it by pushing the door
- Compliant one-piece latch: a springy printed catch like the arm in some magnetic-key boxes, but opened by pressing a visible handle
- Solenoid latch and servo or motorised latch: hidden latches moved by electricity (keypad, RFID, app) instead of a magnet key
- Magnetic keyed lock: a normal-looking lock whose tumblers are moved by coded magnets in the key rather than by its shape
- Pin-tumbler and combination locks: visible locks that give real security against adults
- Inertia-locking reel (seat-belt lock): uses the same inertia and centrifugal effects, but to lock when jerked rather than to hide a latch
- Sliding-panel puzzle boxes (himitsu-bako): hidden, but opened by a sequence of sliding panels rather than a latch
