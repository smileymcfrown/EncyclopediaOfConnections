A threaded nut that, when tightened, drives a ring (ferrule or olive) into a tapered seat in the fitting body, squeezing the ring onto the outside of a tube so it both seals and grips the tube, for water, air, gas, oil and hydraulic lines that you may need to take apart later.

**In plain words:** A nut on a pipe that you tighten with spanners; as it screws down it squashes a small ring (the olive) round the pipe, so the joint grips the pipe and doesn't leak, and you can undo it again later. **Proper term:** Compression fitting (ferrule fitting, flareless tube fitting).

**Also called:** olive fitting, 'nut and olive' (British English: the ring is called an olive; Wikipedia, Compression fitting; DIYdata); ferrule fitting, compression ring fitting (American English: the ring is called a ferrule; Wikipedia, Compression fitting); flareless fitting (Wikipedia, Compression fitting; Cutting ring fitting); Type A or 'non-manipulative' compression fitting (British; the tube isn't reshaped) versus Type B or 'manipulative' fitting (the tube end is shaped with a tool) (Wikipedia, Compression fitting); tube fitting, instrumentation tube fitting, single-ferrule and double-ferrule (twin-ferrule) fitting (Beswick; Swagelok instructions; Wikimedia Commons 'Zweiklemmring-Rohrverschraubung'); cutting ring fitting, bite-type fitting (hydraulic version whose ring cuts into the tube, standardised in ISO 8434; Wikipedia, Cutting ring fitting); Klemmringverschraubung, Schneidringverschraubung (German; Wikimedia Commons categories), knelkoppeling (Dutch; Wikimedia Commons file name); Swagelok-type fitting (Swagelok is a brand); Ermeto (brand, the original cutting ring fitting; Wikipedia, Cutting ring fitting); 'compression fitting' is also what PC water-cooling builders call a barb with a screw-down collar (soft tube) or an O-ring fitting (hard tube); these work differently (TitanRig); lock-bush fitting (Wikipedia 'Piping and plumbing fitting').

## How it works

You slide the nut and then the ring onto the tube and push the tube into the body until it hits the stop. Tightening the nut pushes the ring into the cone-shaped seat inside the body, and the cone squeezes the ring inwards onto the tube, which makes the seal (Beswick). The ring seals against both the tube and the body's seat, so fluid never reaches the threads and no thread sealant is needed; the squeeze also grips (and slightly deforms) the tube so it can't pull out (Wikipedia, Compression fitting). Hydraulic 'cutting ring' versions have sharp edges that cut into the tube's surface for more hold at high pressure (Wikipedia, Cutting ring fitting). Two relatives use the same squeeze-a-ring idea. The slip-joint fitting on sink and bath waste traps has a loose slip nut and a rubber or nylon slip-joint washer; the pipe doesn't need to be cut to an exact length, and many can be done up or undone by hand to clear a trap. Press (crimp) fittings come with an O-ring or sealant already inside and are squeezed permanently onto the pipe by a powered press tool; the joint can even be made on wet pipe (Wikipedia 'Piping and plumbing fitting').

## Parts

A few parts: the fitting body (with a thread, a tapered seat and a stop, or shoulder, for the tube), a compression nut, and one or two rings (ferrules or olives); for soft plastic pipe also a stiffening insert pushed into the pipe end (Beswick; Kalsi Plastics; Wikipedia, Compression fitting).

## Everyday examples

- The small isolating valves and tap connectors under a UK sink or basin (Wikimedia Commons photo of a 15 mm compression isolating valve)
- Copper pipe joins made with a nut and olive in home plumbing (DIYdata; Brassland)
- Brass or plastic fittings on blue MDPE water pipe to garden taps and outbuildings (Kalsi Plastics)
- Compression fittings holding PTFE tube on some 3D-printer extruders instead of push-fit couplers (Prusa forum; Printables 890869)

## When to use it

Use a compression fitting to join rigid or semi-rigid tube (copper, steel, stainless, hard plastic, or plastic pipe with an insert) when the joint must hold pressure, seal gas or liquid, and still come apart for maintenance, or where you can't use a flame to solder (Wikipedia, Compression fitting). For makers: water and air lines on projects, compressed-air tools, hydroponics, lab rigs, coffee-machine or cooling projects, and gripping a PTFE Bowden tube firmly where push-fit collets let it wear or slip (Prusa forum; Printables 890869). Use a slip-joint fitting (hand-tight nut and washer) on waste traps that you may need to take off to clean, and press fittings where you want a fast, permanent joint and have the press tool (Wikipedia 'Piping and plumbing fitting').

## When not to use it

Don't use it on soft, stretchy tubing such as silicone or soft PVC: use a hose barb with a clamp, or a push-to-connect fitting for semi-rigid tube (Beswick). Avoid it where the pipe will be flexed, bent or shaken, e.g. by water hammer: a soldered joint copes better (Wikipedia, Compression fitting). If you connect and disconnect often, use a quick coupler. For gas and high-pressure lines, flare fittings are often used instead (Wikipedia, Compression fitting), and gas work is usually regulated, so follow local rules ⚑ [uncertain]. Keep compression joints where you can reach them: because they sometimes need re-tightening to stop slow leaks, they are generally restricted to accessible places such as under a sink and 'prohibited in concealed locations such as the interiors of walls' (Wikipedia 'Piping and plumbing fitting'). Never repair car brake pipes with them: the UK MOT inspection manual says 'compression joints of a type using separate ferrules are not suitable' (DVSA MOT inspection manual 1.1.11); see the Flare fitting entry.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - seal it, or pass cables or fluid through: joins a tube to another tube, a valve or a threaded port without leaking
  - join and hold: grips the tube so pressure or pulling can't push it out
- **Movement:** none: the tube is fixed in the fitting once the nut is tight
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: a tightened fitting holds the tube so it can't slide or turn; Swagelok's high-pressure instructions tighten until the tube 'will not turn by hand or move axially in the fitting' before the final turn (Swagelok MS-12-01)
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it is a plain sealing joint. The only 'clever' part is that the thread doesn't seal; the squeezed ring does, so it can be undone and remade (Wikipedia, Compression fitting)
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the ring is squeezed radially onto the tube (Beswick)
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the ring deforms the tube (Wikipedia, Compression fitting) and cutting-ring versions bite into it (Wikipedia, Cutting ring fitting), so the tube is also hooked in place
- **Input and output:** not applicable: it joins tubes and passes fluid, not motion
- **Power:** passive (no power needed)
- **Load:** medium to heavy: holds the full working pressure and strong pulls along the tube (the ring grips and bites it), but joints dislike bending, flexing and the shaking from water hammer, which soldered joints tolerate better (Wikipedia, Compression fitting)
- **Play and precision:** not applicable: it doesn't locate parts or pass on motion. The tube must go in straight and right up to the stop (shoulder) before tightening, or the ring seats badly (Swagelok MS-12-01; Brassland)
- **Taking it apart:** removable: undo the nut with spanners and pull the tube out. The ring stays squeezed onto the tube, so you either refit the same tube and ring (instrument fittings) or cut off the tube end and fit a new olive (UK plumbing advice) (Swagelok MS-12-01; Brassland)
- **How often it can be reused:** a few times: the body and nut last many cycles, but the ring limits it. Beswick says most designs allow only a limited number of remakes before the ferrule must be replaced; Swagelok says its fittings can be remade 'many times'; UK plumbing guides treat olives as single-use (Beswick; Swagelok MS-12-01; Brassland)
- **Sealing:** airtight or pressure: the ring squeezed between the tapered seat and the tube makes the seal (Beswick). Beswick rates its stainless compression fittings at 3,000-5,000 psig; plastic MDPE compression fittings seal with an O-ring instead, with a grab ring for grip (Kalsi Plastics)
- **Adjustable:** slightly: if a new joint weeps, you can tighten the nut a little more until it stops (Wikipedia, Compression fitting); there is no position adjustment
- **Access needed:** end of a tube or shaft: the nut and ring slide on from the tube's cut end, and you need room round the fitting for two spanners. Bulkhead versions pass through a panel and are tightened from both sides ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Materials:** Bodies and nuts: brass (plumbing), stainless steel and steel (instrument and hydraulic), and plastics (Beswick; Wikipedia, Compression fitting). Rings: copper or brass olives in plumbing (copper olives are barrel-shaped so can't go on backwards), brass or plastic single ferrules, stainless double ferrules (Wikipedia, Compression fitting; Beswick). Tubes: hard tubing that resists the squeeze, such as copper, steel, stainless steel and hard plastic; soft tube such as polyurethane or vinyl collapses unless a metal insert is pushed in (Beswick). PE, PEX and MDPE pipe always need an insert (Kalsi Plastics; ESG). PTFE filament tubes can also be held with small M8 compression fittings (Prusa forum).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Metal fittings handle heat and cold well, but the tube and ring material set the limits. Vibration and flexing can loosen or fatigue the joint (Wikipedia, Compression fitting). Plastic pipe and printed parts can creep: a PLA printed nut may need re-tightening every few prints (Printables 890869), and PLA softens at about 60 °C and PETG at about 80 °C (CNC Kitchen). Mixing parts from different makers can cause leaks (Swagelok MS-12-01).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: brass, stainless and plastic compression couplers, elbows, tees, reducers, isolating valves and tube-to-thread adapters
  - 3D print (FDM): low-pressure nuts and ferrules for PTFE Bowden tube, and parametric printed pipe couplings (Printables 890869, 784765; Thingiverse 6955018)
  - CNC: turned metal bodies and nuts on a lathe ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: the body, nut and ring are separate parts that must be threaded onto the tube in order (Printables 784765 prints the ferrules as separate small parts with mouse-ear brims)
- **Tools:** Two spanners (one to hold the body steady, one to turn the nut; Swagelok MS-12-01; Brassland); a wheeled pipe cutter and a deburring tool (Brassland); a marker pen for turn-marks; optionally a gap inspection gauge (Swagelok) and an olive puller to remove old rings (Wikipedia, Compression fitting)
- **Difficulty:** easy: buy a ready-made fitting and tighten it with two spanners; printing your own nut and ferrule for low-pressure or PTFE tube is moderate (needs 100% infill and fine threads, and PLA may need re-tightening; Printables 890869, 784765)

### 3D printing tips

1) Use it for low pressure only unless tested: the open-source parametric 'easy-connect' pipe fitting printed in PLA, PETG, ASA and TPE passed hydrostatic tests, with PETG couplings holding up to 4.551 ± 0.138 MPa (Fini et al. 2025, summarised on Thingiverse 6955018); whether that design uses a ferrule is not stated in the summary ⚑ [uncertain]. 2) Infill and walls: print nuts and ferrules at 100% infill (Printables 890869, 784765); for watertight bodies use at least 3-4 perimeters, raise the nozzle temperature 5-10 °C and keep layer height to 60-65% of the nozzle width (Prusa Knowledge Base, Watertight prints). 3) Orientation: print the nut upside down with its narrow hole on the bed to reduce overhangs (Printables 890869). 4) Threads: fine layers help; one designer used 0.08 mm layers and a support threshold angle that keeps supports off the threads (Printables 784765). 5) Material: PETG grips PTFE tube better and suits a permanent fitting; PLA works but may need re-tightening every few prints (Printables 890869); PLA softens at about 60 °C and PETG at about 80 °C (CNC Kitchen), so keep printed fittings away from hot water and hot ends. 6) A printed nut can't crush a metal olive: swage the brass olive onto the copper tube with a metal fitting first, then screw on the printed part (Printables 797450). 7) Soft tube needs a stiff insert inside it or it will collapse (Beswick).

## Rules of thumb

- Instrument (double-ferrule) tube fittings: push the tube fully against the shoulder, tighten finger-tight, mark the nut, then turn 1¼ turns; for 1/16, 1/8 and 3/16 in and 2, 3 and 4 mm tube, turn ¾ of a turn (Swagelok Tube Fitting Instructions MS-12-01). Hold the body steady with a second spanner.
- To remake a Swagelok-type joint: mark the tube and nut before undoing, then refit and turn back to the marked position, where resistance rises sharply, then tighten slightly; don't use the gap gauge on remade joints and don't mix parts from different makers (Swagelok MS-12-01).
- UK plumbing (copper pipe and olive): advice varies from hand-tight plus about half a turn (Wikipedia, Compression fitting) to one full turn after hand-tight (DIYdata) or 1 to 1¼ turns (Brassland). Over-tightening is the most common cause of leaks; if the joint weeps, tighten a little at a time until it stops (Wikipedia, Compression fitting).
- Plastic MDPE compression fittings: hand-tight plus 1 to 1½ turns with a spanner, and always fit the pipe insert (Kalsi Plastics); PE and PEX pipe need an insert to stop the wall collapsing (ESG).
- No PTFE tape or pipe dope is needed on the thread, because the ring seals, not the thread; a little grease on the thread helps smooth tightening (Wikipedia, Compression fitting). DIYdata warns that PTFE tape can get trapped under an olive.
- Cut the tube square with a wheeled pipe cutter (not a hacksaw) and deburr inside and out (Brassland).
- Use hard tubing, or put a metal insert in soft tubing; soft polyurethane or vinyl tube collapses or pulls away from the ferrule (Beswick).
- Single ferrules suit softer materials such as plastic or brass; stainless fittings use two ferrules to reduce galling and give a more consistent seal (Beswick).
- Fit the ferrule the right way round: in hydraulic ferrules the longer sloping face points away from the nut (Wikipedia, Compression fitting).

## Common failures

Leaks from over-tightening, which deforms the ferrule into surface contact (Beswick; Wikipedia, Compression fitting); leaks from under-tightening (Swagelok gap gauge). Tube not pushed fully in, cut out of square, not deburred, or scratched where the olive sits (Brassland; Swagelok). Ring fitted backwards or missing, or parts from different makers mixed (Wikipedia; Swagelok). Soft or plastic pipe without an insert collapses and leaks (Beswick; Kalsi Plastics). A reused olive fails to reseal; fit a new one on fresh pipe if in doubt (Brassland; Wikipedia, Compression fitting). Joint loosens under vibration or bending (Wikipedia, Compression fitting). Advice on reuse conflicts: instrument fittings are designed to be remade many times, plumbing olives often not. Slow leaks can develop over time and need the nut re-tightening, which is why compression joints are kept where they can be reached (Wikipedia 'Piping and plumbing fitting').

## Safety

Always release the pressure before undoing a fitting (Swagelok MS-12-01): a fitting undone under pressure can spray fluid or blow apart. Pressurised gas and hydraulic lines can store a lot of energy; don't use printed fittings for gas, fuel or high pressure unless tested. Gas pipework is usually regulated, and in many places must be done by a qualified person ⚑ [uncertain]. Mind cuts from sharp tube ends and burrs. Brake pipes are safety-critical: in the UK, brake pipe repairs need suitable connectors, and 'compression joints of a type using separate ferrules are not suitable' (DVSA MOT inspection manual 1.1.11).

## Related

- Hose barb: soft tubing stretched over a ridged spigot, for low pressure; cheaper but not for hard tube (Beswick)
- Push-to-connect (push-fit) coupler: grips the tube with a toothed collet and seals with an O-ring; faster to connect and release, but lower grip on PTFE and can wear it (Prusa forum)
- Flare fitting: the tube end is flared with a tool and clamped by a nut, with no separate ring; used for gas, brake and refrigeration lines, and the joint to use for brake pipe repairs, where ferrule-type compression joints are not allowed (Wikipedia, Flare fitting; DVSA)
- Cutting ring (bite-type) fitting: a hydraulic compression fitting whose ring cuts into the tube (Wikipedia, Cutting ring fitting)
- Cable gland: the same nut-squeezes-a-seal idea, but the seal is a rubber insert round a cable
- Soldered metal joint: a soldered (sweat) copper pipe joint is permanent and more tolerant of vibration, but needs a flame (Wikipedia, Compression fitting)
- PC water-cooling 'compression fittings': a barb with a threaded collar (soft tube) or an O-ring collar (hard tube), not a ferrule (TitanRig)
- Tapered pipe thread: a threaded pipe joint (NPT or BSPT) in which the wedging thread, sealed with PTFE tape or pipe dope, makes the seal; in a compression fitting the thread only pulls the nut on and the ring seals
- Parallel pipe thread with a sealing washer: a straight thread that squeezes a washer, bonded seal or O-ring against a flat face; another pipe joint that can be undone and remade, common on taps and hoses
