A capped tube (the bell) sits over a standpipe that drains a tank; when the rising water spills into the standpipe it pulls the air out of the bell and starts a siphon that empties the tank much faster than it fills, until air rushes in under the bell's rim and breaks the siphon, so a steady trickle in becomes a regular flood-and-drain cycle with no moving parts or timer (University of Hawai'i CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014).

**In plain words:** A pipe with an upside-down cup over it that empties a tank or plant bed all by itself: water slowly fills up until it reaches the top of the pipe, then suddenly the whole lot gushes out down the pipe, and when the water gets down to the bottom of the cup it sucks in air and stops, so the tank fills again. Nothing moves except the water. **Proper term:** Bell siphon (an automatic intermittent siphon, or autosiphon).

**Also called:** bell syphon (British spelling; Printables 507051); autosiphon, auto-siphon (FAO 2014; University of Hawai'i CTAHR BIO-10); automatic siphon, automatic intermittent siphon (Wikipedia 'Siphon'); self-starting siphon, self-siphon (Wikipedia 'Siphon'); bell siphon valve (Printables 1739027); flood-and-drain or ebb-and-flow drain (the job it does in aquaponics and hydroponics; FAO 2014; Wikipedia 'Aquaponics'); bell siphon flushing system (older WC and urinal flushing cisterns; Wikipedia 'Flushing trough'); siphon cap (the small cap over a tube in a washing machine's fabric-softener compartment; whitegoodshelp.co.uk); looped siphon (an alternative autosiphon made from a loop of pipe; FAO 2014).

## How it works

Water flowing steadily into the bed rises inside and outside the bell (it gets in through the teeth) until it reaches the top of the standpipe and starts to trickle down it (CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014). The falling water carries air out of the airtight bell, and the bends in the drain hold the water back so the pipe fills, so suction builds in the bell and pulls water up and over the standpipe at full bore: the siphon has started (FAO 2014; CTAHR BIO-10). The siphon is driven by the drop from the water surface in the bed to the drain outlet below (Wikipedia 'Siphon'), so it drains the bed far faster than the inflow refills it (FAO 2014). When the level falls to the bottom of the bell (the teeth) or the end of the snorkel, air rushes in, the siphon breaks, and the bed starts filling again; the cycle repeats as long as water keeps coming in (CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014).

## Parts

A few parts: a vertical standpipe through a watertight bulkhead fitting or rubber grommet (e.g. a Uniseal (brand)) in the floor of the tank, which sets the top water level; the bell, a pipe about twice the standpipe's width with an airtight cap on top and notches ('teeth') cut round its bottom edge; an air tube ('snorkel') or a small hole near the bottom of the bell that lets air in to stop the siphon; a drain pipe under the tank with two 90-degree elbows; and usually a slotted media guard round it all to keep gravel out (University of Hawai'i CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014).

## Everyday examples

- The fabric-softener compartment of a washing-machine drawer: a small cap over a tube that empties the compartment by siphon once rinse water fills it (whitegoodshelp.co.uk; Printables 1256870)
- Old automatic urinal flushing cisterns that fill from a slow trickle and flush on their own (Wikipedia 'Siphon'); flushing troughs for rows of toilets used a bell siphon system (Wikipedia 'Flushing trough')
- The Pythagorean cup (Tantalus cup), a trick cup that drains completely through its stem if you fill it past a line (Wikipedia 'Pythagorean cup')
- Flood-and-drain grow beds in backyard aquaponics and hydroponics (CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014)

## When to use it

Use a bell siphon when a container that is filled steadily should regularly fill up and then empty completely, without a timer, float, valve or electronics: the classic case is an aquaponic or hydroponic grow bed, where flooding feeds the roots and draining pulls fresh air into them (FAO 2014; Wikipedia 'Ebb and flow hydroponics'; CTAHR BIO-10). It lets the pump run all the time, which keeps water flowing through the fish tank, while the bed still floods and drains (FAO 2014). It also suits other 'fill slowly, dump all at once' jobs, such as automatically flushing cisterns and emptying siphon rain gauges (Wikipedia 'Siphon'). It has no moving parts to corrode, wear out or jam (Wikipedia 'Siphon'), and it is cheap to make from plumbing pipe or a print (CTAHR BIO-10; Printables).

## When not to use it

Don't use one if the inflow is very uneven or may stop and start, because it only cycles reliably within a band of inflow rates (FAO 2014; Go Green Aquaponics); use a timer-controlled pump with a plain standpipe overflow instead, which FAO notes needs no calibration but gives less water circulation through the fish tank (FAO 2014). It can't fill anything: to keep a tank topped up use a float valve (ballcock). It can't lift water higher than the water surface it drains from; use a pump (Wikipedia 'Siphon'). Avoid it where the water carries lots of solids or roots that can block the teeth or snorkel, unless you fit a media guard and clean it (CTAHR BIO-10; Wikipedia 'Siphon'). Where the drain must hold a level exactly or shut off on command, use a valve. For a one-off drain, a plain siphon tube or a pump is simpler.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - seal it, or pass cables or fluid through: drains water out through the floor of a tank or grow bed, back to the fish tank or sump (CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014)
  - change the kind of motion: turns a steady trickle of water in into regular surges out, flooding and draining a bed every 15-20 minutes or so (Wikipedia 'Siphon': it turns 'a continuous trickling flow ... into a large surge volume'; CTAHR BIO-10)
  - store energy: lets water build up in the bed, then releases it all at once, the same idea as an automatic flushing cistern (Wikipedia 'Siphon', Automatic intermittent siphon)
- **Movement:** none: no part moves; only the water level rises and falls, flooding and draining the bed over a cycle of about 15-20 minutes (CTAHR BIO-10) or 1-2 cycles an hour (FAO 2014). The bell simply lifts off the standpipe by hand for cleaning
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: the bell sits on the standpipe fitting and does not move while working; it can be lifted straight off for maintenance (CTAHR BIO-10)
- **Special behaviour:**
  - motion conversion: changes a steady inflow into a regular on-off outflow, with no timer, float or valve (FAO 2014, Glossary 'Autosiphon'; Wikipedia 'Siphon')
  - [over-centre](../glossary.md#over-centre) (snaps past a point and stays): in water terms it has two states and snaps between them: once the water passes the top of the standpipe the siphon starts and keeps going until the level reaches the teeth, and once broken it stays off until the bed is full again. This switching between a high and a low level is what makes it flood and drain instead of just overflowing (CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014)
  - [fail-safe](../glossary.md#fail-safe--fail-secure) or breakaway: the standpipe is also an overflow, so even if the siphon fails to start, the water can't rise above the standpipe top as long as the inflow is less than the pipe can carry (CTAHR BIO-10: 'the standpipe regulates the maximum water level'; FAO 2014)
- **How it holds:**
  - not a holding joint (it moves or passes on motion): it controls the flow of water rather than holding parts together
  - suction or air pressure: it works by the airtight bell trapping air and the siphon's suction; any air leak at the cap or snorkel stops it starting (CTAHR BIO-10)
  - friction or clamping ([force-fit](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond)): the standpipe is pushed through a rubber Uniseal grommet or clamped in a bulkhead fitting in the tank floor (CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014); the bell just rests on the fitting under its own weight
- **Input and output:** Steady water inflow -> intermittent surges out (on-off flow). Typical design figures: for a 1-3 m2 media bed 30 cm deep, an inflow of 200-500 L/h per bed (FAO 2014); set the inflow (usually with a ball valve) so the siphon starts, drains and stops every 15-20 minutes whatever the bed size (CTAHR BIO-10); while siphoning, the outflow is much faster than the inflow (FAO 2014). Not a motion converter in the mechanical sense: efficiency, shaft arrangement and [back-driving](../glossary.md#back-driving) do not apply; the drain simply pours down into the fish tank below (CTAHR BIO-10).
- **Power:** passive (no power needed): the siphon itself uses only gravity and air pressure, but it needs a steady inflow, usually from an electric pump or airlift (FAO 2014). If the inflow stops it simply stops cycling; the bed ends up drained or partly full depending on where in the cycle it stopped ⚑ [uncertain: inferred from how it works; no source describes this]
- **Load:** light: no mechanical load. Its capacity is a flow: the standpipe must carry more than the inflow. Tested sizes: a 1/2 in standpipe with a 1 in bell for a 1 x 4 x 1 ft bed (about 30 US gal); 1 in with 2 in for 4 x 4 x 1 ft (120 gal); 1 1/2 in with 3 in for 4 x 6 x 1 ft (180 gal); 2 in with 4 in for 4 x 8 x 1 ft (240 gal) (CTAHR BIO-10). The bed itself is very heavy once filled with gravel and water, so the stand must be strong (FAO 2014)
- **Play and precision:** not applicable for mechanical play. The top and bottom water levels are fixed by the standpipe and the teeth or snorkel, so each cycle repeats as long as the inflow stays steady, but the cycle is sensitive to inflow: too little and it never triggers, too much and it never breaks (FAO 2014; Go Green Aquaponics). FAO calls bell siphons 'finicky' and says they 'require attention' (FAO 2014). Gravel lodged under the teeth lifts the bell and can stop it working (CTAHR BIO-10)
- **Taking it apart:** tool-free quick release: the bell lifts straight off the standpipe and back on during normal operation and maintenance, and lifting it is also how you break a siphon that won't stop (CTAHR BIO-10). The bell and standpipe can both be taken out so water runs straight through to the sump, e.g. while leak-testing a new system (FAO 2014, Appendix 8)
- **How often it can be reused:** effectively unlimited: it cycles continuously, and the bell can be lifted off and refitted as often as needed (CTAHR BIO-10)
- **Sealing:** airtight or pressure: the cap-to-bell joint and the snorkel entry must be airtight or the suction that starts the siphon never forms (CTAHR BIO-10: [primer](../glossary.md#primer) and glue for the cap, 100% silicone round the snorkel, or a threaded 90-degree hose barb instead). The bulkhead or Uniseal grommet keeps the tank floor watertight (CTAHR BIO-10). It only ever sees a slight suction, not stored pressure
- **Adjustable:** yes: the standpipe height sets the top water level; the snorkel length (or air-hole height) sets where the siphon breaks, and trimming it shorter is the usual [cure](../glossary.md#cure) for a siphon that won't stop; the inflow valve sets how often it cycles; and changing the pipe lengths between the drain elbows tunes starting and stopping (CTAHR BIO-10). Some printed bells screw onto their base so the trigger level can be adjusted a little (Printables 1624282)
- **Access needed:** both sides: the bulkhead or grommet and standpipe are fitted through the floor of the bed, and the drain elbows go on underneath; the bell and media guard are then dropped on from above (CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014)
- **Materials:** Usually schedule 40 PVC pipe and caps, with vinyl tubing or a PVC elbow as the snorkel, 100% silicone sealant, and a Uniseal rubber grommet or barrel connector as the bulkhead (CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014). Printed bells, standpipes, flared inlets and media guards are made in plastics that can live under water, e.g. ASA (Printables 1256870, 507051, 1528319, 507046, 1624282). It handles fish water and nutrient solution; the grow medium round it is gravel, cinder rock or expanded clay balls (CTAHR BIO-10)
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Water quality: debris, biofilm and roots can block the teeth, snorkel or seep holes, so it suits clean water best or needs a media guard and regular checks (Wikipedia 'Siphon'; CTAHR BIO-10; Go Green Aquaponics). Air pockets trapped in some automatic siphons slowly dissolve into the water when there is no inflow, which can trigger it at the wrong time (Wikipedia 'Siphon', Automatic intermittent siphon). Sunlight: keep the water level below the surface of the medium so algae don't grow (CTAHR BIO-10). Frost, UV ageing of printed or PVC parts, and warm water softening printed PLA were not checked in a source ⚑ [uncertain].

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: cut and glued PVC plumbing pipe, caps and elbows (CTAHR BIO-10; FAO 2014), or ready-made bell siphon kits sold for aquaponics (e.g. OFERA, vendor, in 20, 25 and 30 cm heights)
  - 3D print (FDM): printed bells, complete siphon modules, flared standpipe inlets and media guards (Printables 507051, 1624282, 1481841, 1528319, 507046, 1389169)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: there are no moving parts to print assembled. The bell can be printed as one closed piece (Printables 507051 offers a one-piece file and a two-piece version that must be sealed), but it is used with a separate standpipe, bulkhead and air tube
- **Tools:** To build from PVC: a hole saw and drill for the bulkhead hole, a saw and pliers to cut the teeth, PVC primer and cement for the cap, 100% silicone to seal the snorkel, and a cable tie (CTAHR BIO-10); FAO's version also uses an angle grinder for the slots and a wrench for the barrel connector (FAO 2014, Appendix 8). Printed versions need sealant or solvent cement if printed in parts (Printables 507051). Using it: none
- **Difficulty:** easy to build, moderate to tune: it is a few cuts in PVC pipe or a simple print, but getting it to start and stop reliably takes trial and error with the snorkel length, drain elbows and inflow rate (CTAHR BIO-10, Troubleshooting; FAO 2014: 'finicky'; 'labour-intensive to calibrate')

### 3D printing tips

1) The bell must be airtight, so print it with extra [perimeters](../glossary.md#perimeters) (Printables 1624282 recommends 'more perimeter for better air tightness'); for watertight and airtight walls use at least 3-4 perimeters, about 0.15 mm layers and 5-10% more extrusion (Prusa Knowledge Base 'Watertight prints'). 2) If the bell is too tall for your printer and printed in two parts, seal the joint with sealant or solvent cement (Printables 507051). 3) Print the cap as part of the bell rather than gluing a cap on, so there is one less joint to leak ⚑ [uncertain: design suggestion, not from a source]; printing it closed end down on the bed avoids [bridging](../glossary.md#overhang--bridging) over the top ⚑ [uncertain: not from a source]. 4) Add a hole in the top sized for aquarium air-line tubing as the snorkel (Printables 507051), and make sure it ends just above the teeth (CTAHR BIO-10). 5) For threaded printed parts, put a rubber seal under the nut, and add an elbow under the drain to help it start (Printables 1624282). 6) A flared or funnel-shaped top on the standpipe lets more water down at the start and makes triggering more consistent (Printables 1528319; FAO 2014 uses a 25-40 mm reducer on top of the standpipe for the same reason). 7) Use a material that tolerates living under water: one maker printed a washing-machine siphon in ASA (Printables 1256870). 8) Print a perforated media guard to keep clay balls or gravel out; one has worked for over 12 months without maintenance (Printables 507046). 9) Size the printed standpipe so the inlet flow is smaller than what the siphon can carry, 'but not too small' (Printables 1624282).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

PVC pipe (schedule 40) in two sizes for standpipe and bell (e.g. 1/2 in and 1 in up to 2 in and 4 in), a PVC end cap, two 90-degree PVC elbows, a PVC reducer, vinyl tubing or a 90-degree hose barb for the snorkel, 100% silicone sealant, PVC primer and cement, cable ties (CTAHR BIO-10); a bulkhead fitting, barrel connector or Uniseal (brand) grommet with matching hole saw; a larger slotted pipe (e.g. 110 mm) as the media guard (FAO 2014); a ball valve to set the inflow (CTAHR BIO-10); a submersible pump to supply the water (FAO 2014). Ready-made 'bell siphon' kits are sold for aquaponics (OFERA, vendor)

## Rules of thumb

- The bell's diameter should be twice the standpipe's (CTAHR BIO-10); Go Green Aquaponics gives 1.5 to 2 times
- 'Double-double rule': the gravel guard at least twice the bell's diameter, and the bell twice the standpipe's (CTAHR BIO-10)
- The top of the standpipe should be level with the bottom of the bell's cap, so enough air is trapped in the bell to start the siphon (CTAHR BIO-10)
- The snorkel tube should poke about 1/4 in into the cap and end just above the teeth; if it reaches down to the teeth or below, the siphon won't break (CTAHR BIO-10)
- Put two 90-degree elbows joined by a straight pipe in the drain below the bed (or a reducer at the bottom of the standpipe) to restrict the outflow; this helps both starting and stopping (CTAHR BIO-10)
- Aim for a flood-and-drain cycle of about 15-20 minutes whatever the bed size, set with a valve on the inflow (CTAHR BIO-10); FAO says systems usually cycle 1-2 times an hour (FAO 2014)
- Keep the top water level 1-2 in below the surface of the grow medium to stop algae, with a media depth of 8-12 in (CTAHR BIO-10) or about 30 cm (FAO 2014)
- Worked example for a 1-3 m2 bed with 30 cm of media and 200-500 L/h inflow: standpipe 2.5 cm wide x 22 cm high; bell 7.5 cm x 25 cm with two 1 x 4 cm slots 1.5 cm up from the bottom and a 1 cm air hole 5 cm from the bottom; media guard 11 cm x 32 cm (FAO 2014, section 4.2)
- The inflow must be strong enough to trigger the siphon but not so strong that it can't stop (FAO 2014)

## Common failures

1) Won't start (bed stays full and just overflows down the standpipe): an air leak at the cap or round the snorkel, so reseal it airtight; gravel lodged under the teeth lifting the bell, so seat it firmly and use a media guard; or too little inflow (CTAHR BIO-10; Go Green Aquaponics). 2) Won't stop (water trickles out constantly and the bed never refills): the snorkel is blocked or too long, so clear it or trim it shorter; the drain needs different pipe lengths between its elbows; or the inflow is too high (CTAHR BIO-10). Wikipedia calls this steady dribble, matching the inflow, the most common failure of automatic siphons (Wikipedia 'Siphon'). 3) Breaks too early, before the bed is empty (Go Green Aquaponics). 4) Teeth and snorkel clogged by grow medium, roots or fish waste: fit a gravel guard about twice the bell's width and lift the bell out to clean it (CTAHR BIO-10; Go Green Aquaponics). 5) A U-bend water seal in the outlet gets blown out by a vigorous flush, so the siphon stops cycling (Wikipedia 'Siphon'). 6) Printed bells leaking air through thin walls or a badly sealed joint (Printables 1624282, 507051).

## Safety

Low risk in itself: no stored pressure and no moving parts. Related hazards in the system around it: the pump that feeds it needs an RCD-protected outlet kept away from water (FAO 2014); a full grow bed of gravel and water is very heavy, so build a strong stand (FAO 2014); a return pipe that dips into a fish tank can accidentally siphon the tank dry in a power cut unless it has a small anti-siphon hole, which FAO says 'is not optional' (FAO 2014, Appendix 8). Don't start ordinary siphons by sucking on the tube when the liquid is anything but clean water: swallowing or breathing in liquids such as petrol can kill (Wikipedia 'Siphon').

## Related

- Plain siphon: a bent tube that drains a tank once started by hand; the bell siphon is a siphon that starts and stops itself (Wikipedia 'Siphon')
- Float valve (ballcock): uses a float and lever to fill a tank to a level and stop; the bell siphon empties instead, with no moving parts
- Timer-controlled pump with a standpipe: the electronic way to flood and drain a bed; easy to set up but needs a timer and gives less circulation (FAO 2014)
- Looped siphon: a loop of pipe that does the same flood-and-drain job without a bell (FAO 2014 mentions it as an alternative under research)
- Pythagorean cup: the same self-starting siphon hidden in a drinking cup, used as a trick (Wikipedia 'Pythagorean cup')
- Siphonic flushing cistern and flushing trough: a toilet siphon started by a lever-operated plate, with a timing box and vent pipe that let air in to end the flush (Wikipedia 'Flushing trough')
- [Check valve](../glossary.md#check-valve) and anti-siphon hole: stop unwanted siphoning back through a pump line (FAO 2014; Wikipedia 'Siphon', back-siphonage)
- Hydraulic ram pump: another water device with no motor that cycles by itself, but it uses [water hammer](../glossary.md#water-hammer) to lift water higher rather than draining a tank
- Centrifugal pump (impeller pump): usually supplies the steady inflow that the bell siphon turns into flood-and-drain cycles (FAO 2014)
