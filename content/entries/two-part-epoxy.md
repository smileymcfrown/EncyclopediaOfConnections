Two-part epoxy is a resin and a hardener that you mix in a set ratio; the mix reacts into a hard, tough plastic that bonds metal, wood, glass, composites and many plastics, fills gaps, and comes in fast (minutes) and slow (hours) types.

**In plain words:** A glue that comes as two separate liquids or pastes that you mix together; over a few minutes to a day the mix hardens into a tough, strong plastic that sticks to almost anything and can also fill gaps and holes. **Proper term:** Two-part epoxy adhesive (epoxy resin and hardener).

**Also called:** epoxy, epoxy glue, epoxy resin, polyepoxide (Wikipedia); two-part epoxy, two-component epoxy, resin and hardener (curing agent) (Prusa; Loctite design guide); 5-minute epoxy, quick-set or fast epoxy; 30-minute, slow-set or 24-hour epoxy (Wikipedia; Prusa); structural adhesive, engineering adhesive (Wikipedia); epoxy putty (a kneadable two-part stick), e.g. Milliput (brand) (Wikipedia 'Cyanoacrylate'); laminating, boat-building or 'marine' epoxy, e.g. WEST SYSTEM (brand) (Wikipedia; West System); Loctite Epoxy / Hysol, Z-Poxy, J-B Weld, Araldite (brands; Loctite, Prusa, Wikipedia references); J-B Weld or JB Weld (brand): a two-part epoxy whose name sounds like welding (it began as 'Just Bond'), so makers use it as a verb ('you can probably jb-weld it') and suggest it instead of welding, e.g. for fixing a flange onto a small motor shaft (Wikipedia 'J-B Weld'; r/robotics thread read through the Arctic Shift archive).

## How it works

The resin contains reactive ring-shaped groups (epoxide rings) and the hardener contains chemicals, such as amines or mercaptans, that open those rings and bond to them; when the two are mixed they join into a tightly cross-linked network (a thermoset plastic) that can't melt again (Loctite design guide). The two parts react in fixed proportions, so the mix ratio must be right: adding extra hardener does not speed it up but leaves a mix that 'can never fully [cure](../glossary.md#cure)' (Epoxyworks; Loctite design guide). The reaction gives off heat, and warmth speeds it up, so a big lump in a cup sets much faster than a thin film (Epoxyworks). Because it cures all the way through by chemistry, not by air or moisture, it can fill gaps and thick sections (Loctite: 'excellent depth of cure'); it grips surfaces by soaking into their roughness and bonding to them, so clean, sanded surfaces matter (Prusa; Epoxyworks).

## Parts

A few parts: a resin and a hardener, sold in a double syringe or cartridge, two bottles or tins, or as a kneadable putty stick; plus disposable mixing tools and optional fillers (such as colloidal silica or microfibres) to thicken it for gaps (Prusa; Epoxyworks; West System).

## Everyday examples

- The hardware-store double syringe of '5-minute epoxy' with separate resin and hardener sides (Wikipedia 'Epoxy')
- Wooden and fibreglass boat building and repair (Wikipedia 'Epoxy')
- Surfboard fin mounts, bicycles, skis, snowboards and golf clubs, which use epoxy structural adhesives (Wikipedia 'Epoxy')
- Electronics potted in hard resin to seal and protect them (Loctite EA E-00CL data sheet)

## When to use it

Use epoxy when the parts don't fit perfectly and the glue has to fill gaps, when you are joining different materials (metal to wood, plastic to metal, glass), when the joint carries real load or knocks, or when you need time to line things up (Wikipedia; Prusa; Loctite design guide). It's the usual choice for gluing hardware into holes, repairing wood and boats, bonding carbon or glass fibre, filling and fairing, and potting electronics (Epoxyworks; Loctite EA E-00CL). Slow epoxy gives the strongest, toughest bonds; fast epoxy suits small quick repairs (Prusa).

## When not to use it

Don't use it when you need a bond in seconds (use CA) or when parts must come apart again (use screws, threaded inserts or magnets), because cured epoxy won't dissolve (Prusa). On smooth plastics like ABS, PET and nylon, CA is often stronger in [shear](../glossary.md#peel-vs-shear) (Loctite design guide; Prusa). It bonds poorly to polyethylene and polypropylene (Loctite design guide): use a polyolefin [primer](../glossary.md#primer) with CA, a special polyolefin adhesive, or mechanical fastening. For flexible joints, use a flexible adhesive (polyurethane or silicone) rather than a rigid epoxy. Avoid ordinary room-temperature epoxy in hot places such as on an extruder or in a car in the sun (West System heat [deflection](../glossary.md#deflection) temperature 48 °C), and don't leave it uncoated in sunlight (Wikipedia).

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - glue or bond: joins parts permanently, including different materials such as metal to wood or plastic
  - join and hold: fixes screws, threaded rod, nuts and other hardware into holes and pockets (Epoxyworks: 404 filler for 'fastener bonding applications')
  - seal it, or pass cables or fluid through: can pot or encapsulate electronics and seal around parts (Loctite EA E-00CL data sheet: 'bonding, potting, or encapsulating')
- **Movement:** none: a rigid joint with no movement (flexible epoxies exist, but still don't let parts move)
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0: the glued parts are locked together
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it has none of the listed behaviours. Its own tricks are filling gaps and building up missing material (it can be thickened with fillers and shaped), a choice of [working time](../glossary.md#pot-life--working-time) from minutes to over an hour by choosing the hardener, curing in thick sections, and being sandable, machinable and paintable once hard (Epoxyworks; West System; Loctite design guide)
- **How it holds:**
  - [material bond](../glossary.md#form-fit--force-fit--material-bond) (glue, solvent or melting): the cured epoxy sticks to both surfaces
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): when it fills holes, grooves, threads or [undercuts](../glossary.md#undercut) and hardens, it also locks parts mechanically ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed): it cures by chemical reaction at room temperature; gentle heat only speeds it up (Loctite design guide; Epoxyworks).
- **Load:** heavy for a glue on metal, wood, glass and composites: e.g. a 5-minute epoxy reached about 15 MPa lap shear on grit-blasted steel, 28 MPa on etched aluminium, 12 MPa on fir and 15 MPa on glass (Loctite EA E-00CL); slow marine epoxy itself has a tensile strength of about 54 MPa (7,900 psi, West System 105/205). On plastics it is usually weaker than cyanoacrylate: Loctite's tests of several epoxies gave about 3-24 MPa on ABS, 3-6 MPa on nylon, 1-5 MPa on PET and only about 0.3-1.5 MPa on polyethylene and polypropylene, depending on the epoxy, while general-purpose CA broke the ABS test pieces first and reached about 28 MPa on nylon (Loctite design guide). It is tougher than CA in knocks and peel ('good toughness', Loctite; slow types 'stronger and less [brittle](../glossary.md#brittle--ductile)', Prusa), but still weakest in peel and cleavage, like all glues (Loctite design guide).
- **Play and precision:** not applicable (nothing moves). It tolerates poor fit because it fills gaps, but a thin bond line is strongest: 'Maximum shear strength is obtained with a 0.1 to 0.2 mm bond line' (Loctite EA E-00CL); bigger gaps should be bridged with epoxy thickened by a filler (West System).
- **Taking it apart:** permanent: cured epoxy does not dissolve; acetone or vinegar only loosen it enough to scrape or peel it off (Prusa: 'It does not dissolve like when using debonder on a CA glue').
- **How often it can be reused:** once
- **Sealing:** watertight: a joint or pocket completely filled with epoxy keeps water out, and epoxy is used for potting electronics (Loctite EA E-00CL) and for boat building and repair (Wikipedia). Moisture resistance varies a lot: marine epoxies form 'a high-strength, moisture-resistant solid' (West System 105/205), while a fast 5-minute type kept only 35% of its strength after 1,000 hours in water (Loctite EA E-00CL).
- **Adjustable:** no, once it gels; before that, parts can be moved and lined up during the working time, from about 3-5 minutes for fast types to an hour or more for slow ones (Loctite EA E-00CL: working life 3.5 min; Epoxyworks: 105/205 working time 60-70 min in a thin film).
- **Access needed:** not applicable: epoxy is applied to the joint faces before assembly, or packed into a hole or pocket from one side.
- **Materials:** Bonds well: steel, aluminium and other metals, wood, glass, stone, ceramics, fibreglass and carbon-fibre composites, cured epoxy and polyester, and many rigid plastics such as PVC, polycarbonate, ABS, PLA, PETG and resin prints (Wikipedia; Loctite EA E-00CL; Loctite design guide; Prusa). Weaker on nylon and acrylic (about 1-6 MPa in Loctite's tests), and poor on polyethylene, polypropylene, PTFE and silicone (Loctite design guide; Loctite EA E-00CL) ⚑ [uncertain for silicone and PTFE]. Polyester gelcoat does not stick to cured epoxy, although epoxy sticks well to polyester (Wikipedia).
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Heat: room-temperature-cured epoxies soften at modest temperatures: heat deflection temperature 48 °C and ultimate Tg 61 °C for WEST SYSTEM 105/205, and a Tg of only 20 °C for a fast 5-minute type (Loctite EA E-00CL); heat-cured and special high-temperature epoxies do better, and epoxy adhesives in general degrade above 177 °C (Loctite design guide; Wikipedia). Cold: cure slows sharply, with minimum working temperatures of about 4-18 °C depending on hardener (West System). Water: marine epoxies are moisture-resistant (West System), but a fast epoxy lost most of its strength in hot, humid air (10% left after 500 hours of condensing humidity at 49 °C) (Loctite EA E-00CL). Sunlight: epoxy deteriorates in UV light and needs paint or varnish over it outdoors (Wikipedia). Chemicals: generally good resistance; a fast epoxy kept its strength in motor oil, petrol, acetone and isopropanol (Loctite EA E-00CL). Old epoxy may look fine but never cure fully (Prusa).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: double syringes and cartridges (5-minute to 24-hour types), bottles or tins of resin and hardener, epoxy putty sticks, and filler powders
  - 3D print (FDM): bonding printed parts, filling seams, gluing in hardware, and as a coating or putty (Prusa)
  - 3D print (resin): bonding and filling resin prints (Prusa)
  - woodwork: gap-filling structural joints, [fillets](../glossary.md#chamfer--fillet) and repairs (West System; Epoxyworks)
  - CNC and metalwork: bonding metal parts and fixing bushes, studs and inserts (Wikipedia; Epoxyworks)
  - composites and boats: laminating glass or carbon fibre cloth (Wikipedia; Epoxyworks)
  - electronics: potting and encapsulating (Loctite EA E-00CL)
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: it is a glue for joining separately made parts, though you can print pockets, grooves and glue channels into parts to receive it.
- **Tools:** Mixing tray or cup and a stick (or a mixing nozzle for cartridges); disposable gloves; sandpaper (80-grit aluminium oxide for wood, metal and fibreglass per Epoxyworks) and a solvent such as isopropyl alcohol or acetone for cleaning surfaces; clamps, tape or weights to hold parts still while it cures; optional fillers, a scale or metering pumps for accurate ratios, and a warm spot to speed curing (Epoxyworks; West System; Loctite EA E-00CL data sheet).
- **Difficulty:** easy to moderate: widely sold and forgiving of gaps, but you must measure and mix accurately, work within the pot life, protect your skin and keep parts still while it cures (Epoxyworks; West System).

### 3D printing tips

1) Prepare the surfaces: sand the mating faces, clean off dust and grease with isopropyl alcohol or acetone, and glue soon after (Prusa; Epoxyworks; Loctite EA E-00CL). 2) Pick the speed: fast 5-minute epoxy for small quick jobs, slow epoxy for strength, because the long cure lets it '[creep](../glossary.md#creep) in each pore and crack' and slow types are 'usually stronger and less brittle'. A few dots of CA can hold the parts while the epoxy cures (Prusa). 3) Measure the ratio exactly (1:1 for many syringes, 5:1 by volume for some bulk epoxies) and never add extra hardener to speed it up (Loctite EA E-00CL; West System 105/205; Epoxyworks). Mix thoroughly for at least two minutes in a cup, scraping sides and bottom, or about 15 seconds past an even colour for small amounts; add fillers only after mixing (Epoxyworks; Loctite EA E-00CL; West System). 4) Mix small batches and spread them out: a lump in a cup heats itself and sets much faster, and large masses can smoke or melt the cup (Epoxyworks; Wikipedia). PLA starts to soften at about 60 °C (CNC Kitchen), so avoid thick, fast-setting lumps against thin PLA walls ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) Design a little glue gap: about 0.1-0.2 mm gives the strongest shear bond (Loctite EA E-00CL); for bigger gaps, thicken the epoxy with colloidal silica or a high-density filler to a mayonnaise or peanut-butter consistency (Epoxyworks; West System). 6) Clamp or tape just firmly enough to squeeze a little out; clamping too hard squeezes it all out and leaves a weak, 'starved' joint (West System). Keep the parts still until it has cured (Loctite EA E-00CL). 7) Use epoxy to glue nuts, magnets, threaded rod or bearings into printed pockets, or to fill and fair seams, and clear epoxy to imitate glass (Epoxyworks; Prusa). 8) Speed curing with gentle warmth after it has gelled, up to about 49 °C (120 °F) (Epoxyworks), which is below PLA's softening point. 9) Avoid relying on epoxy for nylon, PE or PP prints, where it bonds poorly (Loctite design guide). 10) In Prusa's quick pull test on printed samples, two 5-minute epoxies reached about 2-6 MPa, less than good CA glues (about 10-13 MPa), and in CNC Kitchen's test soaking prints in epoxy did not make them stronger (Prusa; Hackaday); in a long-term PETG test, two-part epoxy held well enough that the PETG broke first (Hackaday on Cosel's test).

### Buying one (off-the-shelf parts)

Search for 'two-part epoxy adhesive' in '5-minute' (fast), '30-minute' and 'slow cure' types; 'structural epoxy' or 'high-strength epoxy'; 'clear epoxy'; 'flexible' or 'toughened' epoxy; 'steel-filled epoxy'; 'epoxy putty'; 'dual cartridge' with a 'static mixing nozzle' and dispensing gun; bulk 'epoxy resin and hardener' with metering pumps; fillers such as 'colloidal silica', 'microfibres', 'high-density filler' and 'microballoons' (Loctite design guide; Epoxyworks; West System). Brand examples: Loctite EA E-00CL / E-05MR, WEST SYSTEM 105 with 205 or 206 hardener, Z-Poxy, J-B Weld (brands).

## Rules of thumb

- Mix exactly at the stated ratio (e.g. 1:1 for Loctite EA E-00CL, 5:1 by volume for WEST SYSTEM 105/205); changing the amount of hardener does not control cure speed and gives a mix that never fully cures (Loctite EA E-00CL data sheet; West System 105/205 data sheet; Epoxyworks 'The Basics of WEST SYSTEM Epoxy Resin and Hardener').
- Temperature: every 18 °F (10 °C) warmer than 72 °F (22 °C) roughly halves the pot life and cure time (Epoxyworks); each hardener has a minimum working temperature, e.g. 4-5 °C for a fast hardener and 16-18 °C for slow ones (West System 105/205 data sheet; West System hardener selection guide).
- Pot life is measured on 100 g in a cup and is much shorter than working time in a thin film (e.g. 9-12 minutes vs 60-70 minutes for 105/205); mix small batches (Epoxyworks).
- Strongest bond line about 0.1-0.2 mm; contact pressure is necessary and parts must not move while it cures (Loctite EA E-00CL). For gaps, thicken with filler and clamp only until a little squeezes out (West System 'Bonding and Clamping').
- Design joints for shear and compression, avoid peel and cleavage, and widen rather than lengthen overlaps (Loctite 'Design Guide for Bonding Plastics', vol. 6, pp. 77-79).
- Full strength takes time: 24 hours at 25 °C for high strength with a fast epoxy (Loctite EA E-00CL), and 1-4 days to working strength for 105/205 (West System 105/205 data sheet).
- Heat limits: room-temperature-cured epoxies soften at fairly low temperatures, e.g. heat deflection temperature 118 °F (48 °C) and ultimate Tg 142 °F (61 °C) for 105/205 (West System), and a glass transition temperature of just 20 °C for a fast epoxy cured at 25 °C (Loctite EA E-00CL). Curing with heat raises it (Loctite design guide), and in general epoxy adhesive strength degrades above 177 °C (Wikipedia 'Epoxy').

## Common failures

Soft, sticky or never-curing epoxy from the wrong ratio, poor mixing or working too cold; scrape it off and redo it, never pile fresh epoxy on uncured epoxy (West System 'Epoxy Problem Solver'; Epoxyworks). A weak, 'starved' joint from clamping so hard that all the glue squeezes out (West System). Bonds peeling off smooth, dirty or low-energy surfaces (PE, PP, nylon) because the surface wasn't sanded and cleaned, or the plastic can't be glued (Loctite design guide; Epoxyworks). Parts moved before the epoxy cured (Loctite EA E-00CL). The mix going hard in the cup before you've finished, because a big batch heats itself (Epoxyworks). A waxy film (amine blush) on some cured epoxies, a normal by-product in some conditions; wash it off with water before sanding or adding more epoxy (West System). Fast epoxy joints softening in warm places or losing strength in damp ones (Loctite EA E-00CL). Old stock that no longer cures properly (Prusa).

## Safety

Skin: liquid resin and hardener irritate skin and eyes, and repeated contact can cause allergic sensitisation; fewer than 10% of users react when overexposed and fewer than 2% develop allergic dermatitis, but once sensitised even tiny amounts can trigger reactions (West System; Wikipedia). Wear gloves and don't clean skin with solvents (Loctite EA E-00CL). Breathing: ventilate; epoxy is a main cause of occupational asthma among plastics users (Wikipedia), and you should never sand epoxy that is not fully cured, because the reactive dust can cause severe respiratory irritation and allergies (West System). Heat: a large mixed batch can overheat, smoke, melt its container or even catch fire, so mix small amounts and spread them out (Wikipedia; Epoxyworks). Disposal: let leftovers cure solid before throwing them away (Wikipedia).

## Related

- Cyanoacrylate (superglue): sets in seconds with no mixing and is often stronger on smooth plastics, but is brittle and needs tight-fitting parts
- Structural methacrylate (MMA) adhesives: two-part, fast, strong on plastics; scored highest in a long-term PETG test (Hackaday)
- Polyurethane adhesives: two-part or moisture-curing, more flexible; a construction polyurethane was the overall winner on PETG in the same test (Hackaday)
- Epoxy putty: the same chemistry as a dough you knead by hand, for filling and building up shapes
- UV-curing adhesive: one-part, cures in seconds under UV light, good for clear parts
- Solvent welding: fuses certain plastics (ABS, acrylic, PVC) with no added glue layer
- Hot-melt adhesive: fast and gap-filling but weak and heat-sensitive
- Polyester resin: cheaper laminating resin; epoxy sticks to it, but polyester gelcoat doesn't stick to epoxy (Wikipedia)
- Heat-set threaded inserts and screws: mechanical alternatives for fixing hardware into prints that can be undone
