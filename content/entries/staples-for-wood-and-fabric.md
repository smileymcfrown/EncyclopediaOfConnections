A U-shaped wire fastener driven straight into wood (or a similar soft base) by a staple gun or hammer tacker, whose flat top (crown) bridges over whatever it holds, which makes it the quick way to fix fabric, leather, mesh, plastic film, insulation and low-voltage cables flat onto wood.

**In plain words:** A small U-shaped wire clip fired from a staple gun (or slapped in with a hammer tacker): its two legs go into the wood while the flat top bridges over fabric, mesh, plastic sheet or a cable and pins it down. **Proper term:** Staple (driven wire staple, tacking staple), fired by a staple gun (tacker) or hammer tacker.

**Also called:** staple, wire staple, construction staple, tacker staple, staple-gun staple (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)', 'Staple gun'); tacking: driving straight-leg staples into a solid base with no anvil, as opposed to 'stapling' paper with bent legs (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'); staple gun, trigger tacker, hand tacker, forward-action staple gun; electric, cordless and pneumatic staplers (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'; Wikimedia Commons 'Tool-heavy-stapler.jpg'); hammer tacker, hammer stapler, slap stapler, slapper (Wikipedia 'Hammer tacker'); narrow-crown staples (crown about 1/4 to 3/8 in, 6.35-9.52 mm) for trim, cabinetry and upholstery; medium and wide-crown staples for sheathing and roofing (aftfasteners.com 'Narrow crown staples'); upholstery staples and outward-clinch (blind) staples, whose legs curl outwards inside foam (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'); insulated cable staple, cable staple, arched or rounded staple, cable clip (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)', 'Staple gun'); fence staple (U-shaped staple hammered over fence wire) (Bekaert 'How to properly staple high tensile wire'); hog ring, C-ring: a sharp-ended C- or D-shaped wire ring squeezed shut with hog ring pliers, used for upholstery, car seats and wire mesh (aftfasteners.com 'Hog rings'); T50 (Arrow, brand) is a widely sold staple-gun staple type that many beginners search for; staples are matched to the gun, and 'staples from one manufacturer may or may not fit another manufacturer's unit' (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'); standard: ASTM F1667 'Driven Fasteners: Nails, Spikes, and Staples' in the US (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)').

## How it works

The tool drives the staple's two legs into the wood in one fast blow; a hand staple gun stores energy in a spring and releases it as 'a sharp and powerful blow', because with no anvil behind the work the staple must be fully in 'before the workpiece has time to move' (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'), while a hammer tacker uses the momentum of a swing (Wikipedia 'Hammer tacker'). The legs hold like two thin nails, gripped by the wood fibres, so their withdrawal resistance grows with their size and how deep they go (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). The crown presses the material flat between the legs: it 'can bridge a piece and fasten it without puncturing, with a leg on either side', which is how cables are fixed, and it gives more bearing area than a nail head on thin materials (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'). Outward-clinch staples have a V-shaped crown and bevelled points so the legs curl outwards inside soft material, and hog rings are squeezed into a closed ring round wire or fabric (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'; aftfasteners.com).

## Parts

Single piece: a staple has a flat or arched top (crown) joining two pointed legs, and is supplied glued into strips that load into the tool's magazine (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)', labelled diagram). The tool (staple gun, hammer tacker or powered stapler) is separate. Variants add a plastic saddle (insulated cable staples) or are closed with pliers (hog rings).

## Everyday examples

- The fabric on the underside of a dining-chair seat (The Bulletin; FOX 2 Detroit)
- Insulation, house wrap and carpet underlay fixed with a hammer tacker (Wikipedia 'Hammer tacker')
- Phone, alarm or TV cable fixed along a skirting board with arched or insulated staples (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)', 'Staple gun')
- Wire fencing stapled to wooden posts, and the big staples that close corrugated cardboard boxes (Bekaert; Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)')

## When to use it

Use staples whenever a thin, flexible material must be fixed flat onto wood quickly: re-covering seats and cushions, stretching canvas or insect screen over a frame, fixing chicken wire or netting to a wooden frame, holding plastic sheet, insulation, roofing felt or carpet, closing crates and boxes, and running low-voltage cables (alarm, phone, speaker, LED strip leads) along woodwork. The crown spreads the hold over a wider area than a nail head, bridges two pieces butted together, and straddles a cable without piercing it (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'), and a staple gun works one-handed from the front (Wikipedia 'Staple gun').

## When not to use it

Not for carrying real loads or joining solid wood structurally: staples hold less than nails or screws (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'); use Wood screws, Nails or Metal brackets. Not where the part must come apart again and again: use screws, Hook-and-loop fastener or snaps. Not into 3D-printed plastic, acrylic or other brittle materials (use screws, inserts or glue). Not for mains cable with ordinary staples, and not with a tool its maker says is unsuitable for electrical cables (Toolstation PCT162 manual): mains cable is fixed only with fittings designed for that cable, following local wiring rules (Baltimore City Code 334.30; UK Approved Document P). For fabric that must be sewn to fabric, use stitching (see Load-bearing stitch patterns); for strap ends, use rivets or sewing.

## Details

- **What it does (jobs):**
  - join and hold: pins fabric, leather, vinyl, mesh, netting, plastic film, insulation, paper and thin board, or a cable, flat onto wood; hog rings join mesh, wire and fabric to frames and springs
- **Movement:** none: a driven staple holds things still.
- **Directions it can move (degrees of freedom):** 0 once driven. Exception by design: fence wire is 'loose stapled' so it can slide under the staple as it expands and contracts (Bekaert).
- **Special behaviour:**
  - none: it simply holds by its legs in the wood and its crown over the material
- **How it holds:**
  - friction or clamping (force-fit): the legs are gripped by the wood fibres like nails (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8)
  - shape (the parts interlock; form-fit): the crown straddles the cable, wire or folded fabric so it can't lift off; outward-clinch staples and hog rings are bent into a hook or closed ring (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'; aftfasteners.com)
- **Input and output:** not applicable
- **Power:** passive (no power needed) for the fixing; the tool is manual (spring-loaded staple gun, hammer tacker) or powered (electric, cordless or pneumatic stapler) (Wikipedia 'Staple gun').
- **Load:** Light. Each leg resists pull-out like a small nail: the Wood Handbook uses its nail equation p = 54.12 G^2.5 D L (N, mm) for one staple leg, though 'no verification tests have been done'; for a leg about 1 mm thick going 10 mm into wood of specific gravity 0.5 that gives roughly 95 N (about 10 kgf) per leg, under 200 N per staple, as a short-term breaking value (calculated here; safe loads are far lower) ⚑ [uncertain]. Sideways (shear) strength varies with leg diameter to the 3/2 power, so each leg must be about two-thirds the diameter of a nail to match it (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8). Staples 'generally have lower holding power compared to nails or screws' (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'). Weak points: pulling straight out, thin fabric tearing out round the crown, and staples driven parallel to the grain splitting the wood ⚑ [uncertain]. Strongest when the load slides along the surface (shear), as when taut fabric is pulled across a frame.
- **Play and precision:** not applicable: staples hold flexible material in place; they do not locate parts accurately.
- **Taking it apart:** semi-permanent: 'Once a staple has been driven, it is difficult to remove without causing damage to the surrounding material' (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'); it is prised out with pliers, a thin screwdriver or a staple or tack remover (The Bulletin, Bend, 'DIY: Reupholster chairs'), leaving two small holes.
- **How often it can be reused:** once: a removed staple is bent and thrown away; the material can be re-stapled in fresh places a few times before the wood or fabric edge is shredded ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Sealing:** none: staples leave holes; seal or tape over them if the sheet must be weatherproof (e.g. house wrap is taped later) ⚑ [uncertain].
- **Adjustable:** no: to move or re-tension fabric, pull the staples and re-staple (upholsterers start in the middle of each side and work outwards so tension can be evened out) (The Bulletin; FOX 2 Detroit).
- **Access needed:** one side only (blind): staple guns and hammer tackers have no anvil and drive into a solid base from the front (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'); hog rings need the pliers to reach round both sides of the wire or fabric (aftfasteners.com).
- **Materials:** Staples: zinc-plated (galvanised) steel wire, with stainless steel and coated staples for damp or corrosive places (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'; USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8 on staple coatings); insulated cable staples have a plastic saddle (Wikimedia Commons 'U_Shape_Insulated_Staple_01.JPG'). Base material: softwood, plywood and similar; staple guns are sold for 'wood, plastic, or masonry' (Wikipedia 'Staple gun') but hand guns struggle in hardwood ⚑ [uncertain]. Holds: fabric and upholstery, leather and vinyl, insulation, house wrap, roofing felt, carpet and underlay, plastic film, insect screen, wire mesh and netting, card and thin board, and low-voltage cables (Wikipedia 'Staple gun', 'Hammer tacker'). Not for: 3D-printed plastic or brittle acrylic as the base (it cracks; see making tips), hard metals, or holding loads people rely on.
- **Heat, cold, water and wear:** Plain steel staples rust outdoors and in damp places and can stain wood; use galvanised or stainless staples there (Wikipedia 'Staple (fastener)'; USDA Wood Handbook 'Corrosion and Staining'). Wood that goes through wetting and drying cycles loses much of its grip on driven fasteners: nail withdrawal can fall to 25% of its first value (USDA Wood Handbook, for nails, which staples resemble). Sunlight degrades the fabric and plastic held by the staple more than the staple itself ⚑ [uncertain]. Fence wire expands and contracts with temperature, so it is loose-stapled (Bekaert).

## Making it

- **Ways to make or get it:**
  - off-the-shelf part: staples, staple guns and hammer tackers are bought
  - woodwork: fabric and mesh onto wooden frames, canvas onto stretcher bars, cabinet backs and crates
  - 3D print (FDM): printed cable clips and hangers with a thin tab that a staple is fired through (see make_it_yourself); printed parts are not a good base to staple into
  - laser cut: plywood frames and panels that fabric or mesh is stapled onto ⚑ [uncertain]
- **Printing it in one piece (print in place):** no: staples are bought wire parts; only clips and frames that are stapled are printed.
- **Tools:** A staple gun (manual, electric, cordless or pneumatic) or hammer tacker loaded with staples made for it; for cable staples, a gun with a wire guide or a cable-staple gun, or hammer-in cable clips (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'); hog ring pliers for hog rings (aftfasteners.com); pliers, a thin screwdriver or a staple/tack remover to take staples out (The Bulletin); a hammer to tap down proud staples ⚑ [uncertain]; safety glasses (Toolstation PCT162 manual).
- **Difficulty:** easy: buy a hand staple gun and the matching staples; the skill is choosing leg length, keeping fabric taut and keeping fingers out of the way.

### 3D printing tips

1) Don't staple into printed plastic as if it were wood: there are no fibres to grip the legs and the blow can crack the part ⚑ [uncertain]. To fix fabric to a printed frame, glue or screw in a wooden strip to staple into, or clamp the fabric with a printed bar and screws instead ⚑ [uncertain]. 2) To fix a printed clip, hanger or cable holder to wood with a staple gun, give it a thin flat tab with a groove the width of the staple crown, so the staple passes through the plastic and both legs go into the wood; makers print such clips so the gun lines up every time (Printables 'Cable Clips for Makita Staple Gun', 'Staple-Mount Cable Hanger'). 3) Use a tough material for stapled parts: one maker reports that ABS works, nylon, HIPS and ASA should, but 'PLA and PETG probably will not, because they tend to shatter' when stapled (Printables 'Cable clip for mounting with a staple gun', TheParrotGuy). 4) Keep the staple well away from the edges of the printed tab so the legs don't split it out ⚑ [uncertain]. 5) A printed wire guide on the gun's nose holds a cable centred so the staple straddles it instead of piercing it (Printables 'Stanley Staple Gun Helper/Wire Holder'); never do this on mains cable (see safety_notes). 6) Printable staple removers and staple-gun holders are popular workshop aids (see make_it_yourself).

## Rules of thumb

- Staples behave like small nails: withdrawal of each leg varies almost directly with its circumference and depth, the nail equation p = 54.12 G^2.5 D L (N, mm) has been used for one leg, and each leg must be about two-thirds the diameter of a nail to give the same sideways load; neither has been verified by tests for staples (USDA Wood Handbook 2021, ch. 8, pp. 8-9 to 8-10).
- Corrosion, sustained loads and durability matter as much as the first-day strength when judging a stapled joint (USDA Wood Handbook ch. 8, p. 8-10); in moist, preservative-treated wood use stainless or suitably coated fasteners (USDA Wood Handbook 'Corrosion and Staining').
- Fabric on a seat: start with a couple of staples in the middle of one side, then the opposite side pulled taut, then the other two sides, working out to the corners; staples about 1 in (25 mm) apart and about 1 in from the edge; about three staples per corner; 1/4 to 3/8 in (6-10 mm) staples (The Bulletin, Bend, 'DIY: Reupholster chairs', 2014).
- Don't staple too close to the edge of a seat: the points can poke through the fabric and cut someone (FOX 2 Detroit upholstery feature).
- Fence wire: 'loose staple every wire' so it can move under the staple and the strainer post, not the stapled post, takes the impact (Bekaert).
- Cables: some staple guns have wire guides and some take rounded staples so the staple straddles a small cable without piercing it (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'); flat cables must not be stapled on edge (Baltimore City Code 334.30, an adoption of the US National Electrical Code).
- Hammer tackers suit sheet materials: 'typically the shallower the staple, the better hold its grip on the surface' (Wikipedia 'Hammer tacker').

## Common failures

Staple stands proud of the work: legs too long for a hard base, too little force, or the gun not pressed down; use shorter staples, press the nose firmly, increase the hammer force on a powered stapler, or tap it down ⚑ [uncertain]. Legs fold over or the staple jams: hardwood, a knot, or the wrong staple size for the gun; use the gun's own staple type and clear jams safely (Toolstation PCT162 troubleshooting: 'wrong size fasteners', 'bent fasteners'). Fabric pulls out round the staples: staples too close to the edge, too far apart, or single-layer thin fabric; fold the edge over, staple closer together and further from the edge (The Bulletin). Wood splits: staples near an edge or in thin brittle wood ⚑ [uncertain]. Cable pierced or crushed: staple not centred or driven too hard; use a wire guide or insulated cable staples (Wikipedia 'Staple gun'). Rust streaks outdoors (USDA Wood Handbook). Wrinkled or uneven fabric: stapling round from one corner instead of from the middle of each side outwards (The Bulletin).

## Safety

Staple guns and powered staplers fire like nail guns: always assume the tool is loaded, never point it at yourself or anyone nearby, only fire with the nose pressed firmly on the work (otherwise 'the fastener may be deflected away from your target'), and always wear eye protection (Toolstation PCT162 tacker and power-tool safety warnings); keep your other hand clear of the nose and of the back of thin material ⚑ [uncertain]. Unplug or disconnect a powered stapler before clearing a jam, and take care when clearing any jam because 'the fastener may be forcefully discharged' (Toolstation PCT162). Never fix mains cable with plain staples: a staple can pierce or crush the insulation and cause 'electric shock and fire hazards'; use only fasteners designed for electrical cable installations, make sure cables are not live, and check the insulation afterwards (Toolstation PCT162). Mains wiring work must follow local law (e.g. US electrical codes as adopted locally, such as Baltimore City Code 334.30; Building Regulations Part P in England), so this entry gives no wiring steps. Staple points that stick through fabric near an edge can cut people (FOX 2 Detroit); hog ring and staple points are sharp (aftfasteners.com).
